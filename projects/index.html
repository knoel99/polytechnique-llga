What these projects are for

These notes are independent study notes. Every project on this page is a proposal, not an official subject: the programme publishes no Year 1 project subject, and the Transverse project leaves brief and grading to the official project setup.

The aim is to make the courses work together on one real question at the end of each year, the way a research internship or a job will. Each brief names the official vehicle it can serve, the course chapters it relies on and the master objectives it exercises.

Year 1: the Period 2 project vehicles

Every Year 1 project combines the three mandatory Period 2 courses (Reinforcement Learning, Graph ML, Text Mining & NLP) with the Period 1 foundations (Machine Learning, one Deep Learning course, the refreshers) and at least one elective. It is a dress rehearsal for the 16-week Research-oriented internship.

Period 2 already holds three project slots, none of them with a subject:

  • Graph ML team project. The course description announces “quizzes and team projects”. In the session plan of these notes, session 9 is “lab: Example 8.1 (start the team project)”.
  • RL course project (50 %). The course description announces a course project (50 %) and practical work (50 %). The closest material is Ex. 16, “Agent project”.
  • NLP capstone. Ex. 14 is a capstone pipeline that exports its triples to the Graph ML mini-GraphRAG.

The proposal: one team repository, three graded components. The graph work package is graded as the Graph ML team project, the RL work package as the RL course project, and the NLP work package as the NLP capstone.

Timeline: six weeks, from Graph ML session 9 (W1) to about two weeks after the end of Period 2. Period 2 runs from 4 January to 19 March 2027 on the course sheets; the exact weeks depend on the official timetable. The project ends before the research internship starts.

Assumed workload: 60–80 h per student in a group of 3 to 5, and 45–60 h for a solo variant.

Year 2: the Transverse project

Every Year 2 project combines the two Year 2 core courses (Large Language Models and Analysis and Deep Learning on Geometric Data) with at least two Year 2 advanced themes and the Year 1 foundations.

The vehicle is the official Transverse project (8 ECTS, mandatory). At the usual 25–30 h per ECTS, that is about 200–240 h of student work; the programme publishes no split.

Proposed shape: a charter week (W0) at the end of Year 2 Period 1, a six-week build phase (W1–W6) in Period 2, then the defence. Every Year 2 project also feeds the Ethics seminar (one case memo) and the Final internship (one open question).

Common rules

These rules apply to every project. Each brief adapts them to its own question.

Charter

A two-page charter is due at the end of W1 in Year 1 and at W0 in Year 2. A tutor reviews it, and it is re-read against the final report. It fixes six things.

  1. One question, one metric, one baseline per work package, before any stacking of models: the rule of the research-internship page.
  2. “Done” in usage terms, as the Transverse project page asks: an acceptance test stated as what a user can do, not only as a model metric. Mandatory in Year 2, encouraged in Year 1.
  3. Module map: for each work package, the 2–3 course chapters it applies, as the final-internship page asks. The internship report reuses this “path evidence”.
  4. Compute and cost budget in FLOPs, GPU-hours and euros, with the calculators of Deep Learning (P2) ch. 1 and ch. 6 (Ex. 7) or Emerging ML ch. 7. Year 2 adds the euros-per-million-tokens model of MLOps ch. 4.
  5. Data licence and consent: the licence of each dataset (several below are non-commercial), and consent forms whenever people are recorded or rate outputs.
  6. Courses actually taken. Several Year 2 courses are “slot TBC” and the Year 2 Period 2 list is unpublished. Each brief names a fallback chapter for every slot-TBC course it uses.

Shared evaluation kit

Every team builds or reuses the same evaluation kit. Its pieces already exist in the notes, scattered across courses:

  • leak-free pipelines and the “golden rule” of Machine Learning ch. 4: 4.2 nested CV, 4.3 leaks, 4.5 calibration, 4.6 reference pipeline;
  • leakage-free graph splits and temporal splits: Graph ML Example 8.1 and the causality rule of Graph ML 5.6–5.8;
  • paired comparisons with confidence intervals: the “Graph × LLM transfer check” of the statistics refresher’s exit checkpoint (paired gains, cluster bootstrap by source group, shuffled-edge control) and its ch. 6 (tests; 6.6 multiple testing);
  • the winner’s curse when the best of many runs is picked (Example 3.6, the maximum of noisy scores): final numbers come from a test set touched once;
  • seeds: at least 3 per trained model in Year 1 and at least 5 in Year 2 (Advanced Deep Learning Def. 1.1, the Advanced GNN ch. 1 protocol, Boosting ch. 9);
  • in Year 2, significance-aware CI gates (MLOps ch. 2, Def. 2.1, Ex. 6).

Reproducibility and logs

  • The repository template of the CS refresher, ch. 3 (pytest, lockfile, Dockerfile, logging) and the training checks of Deep Learning ch. 8 (seeding, overfit one batch, LR range test).
  • A weekly log “hypothesis, trial, result, next question” (research internship) and a decision and technical-debt log (Transverse project).
  • Year 2 adds artefact lineage for data, prompts, indexes, graph snapshots and adapters (MLOps ch. 1, DVC + MLflow).

Trust work package

Year 1. Each report has a “failure modes and limits” section (leakage, bias, misuse, privacy of the data used) with at least one failure mode measured.

Year 2. A trust work package is mandatory. It holds four pieces:

  • a threat model in the format of Security & Robustness Def. 1.1;
  • an attack suite scored by attack success rate (ASR) per family, following ESP Ex. 11: 30 attacks, 10 of them adaptive (20 and 5 for a solo variant);
  • one uncertainty or privacy guarantee, written up as a “guarantee sheet” in the format of Privacy & UQ Ex. 11;
  • successful attacks turned into CI regression tests.

Year 2 red-team swap (W4). Each team attacks another team’s system for one week with a fixed budget, like the simulated post-mortem of MLOps Ex. 12. The defending team reports its detection time and its fixes.

Y1.1 GraphRAG with a learned retrieval router

Route Graph × LLM. Domain enterprise knowledge, with open-domain multi-hop QA as a measurable proxy (variant: a regulatory or corporate corpus).

Pitch. Build the mini-GraphRAG of the Graph ML final lab properly. Extract a knowledge graph (KG) from the paragraphs of a multi-hop QA benchmark with NER and relation extraction, scored against gold triples, and complete it by KG completion.

Answer each question in four ways: lexical, dense, graph traversal, and pseudo-code prompting over a subgraph. Then let a contextual bandit learn, per question, which retrieval mode is worth its token cost.

Question. On multi-hop questions, when does an LLM-built knowledge graph beat lexical and dense retrieval, and at what token cost? Can a cost-aware contextual bandit learn a routing policy that dominates every fixed retrieval mode on the accuracy–cost plane?

Courses used

  • Text Mining & NLP: ch. 1 (normalisation, subword tokenisation); ch. 2 (BM25, inverted index, MAP / nDCG / MRR of Def. 2.7); ch. 6 (NER, BIO decoding, entity-level F1); ch. 7 (Protocol 7.1 fine-tuning, Sentence-BERT); Ex. 14 as the ingestion skeleton.
  • Graph ML: ch. 0 (modularity, Louvain / Leiden); ch. 3 (node2vec, Example 3.4) for structural features of seed entities; ch. 6 (TransE, R-GCN + DistMult, link-prediction protocol); Example 8.1 (leakage-free split for KG completion); ch. 10 (10.1 pipeline, 10.2 serialisation and pseudo-code prompting, 10.3 hygiene, final lab).
  • Reinforcement Learning: ch. 4 (UCB1, Alg. 4.4 Thompson sampling, Alg. 4.5 LinUCB, regret); ch. 7 (Def. 7.1 POMDP framing, ReAct tool loop, guiding lab and Ex. 16 with cost metrics).
  • Machine Learning ch. 4 (metrics, 4.5 calibration, 4.6 reference pipeline); the statistics refresher’s exit checkpoint (paired gains, cluster bootstrap, shuffled-edge control) and its ch. 6.
  • Deep Learning ch. 6 (6.4 transformer block) and ch. 7 (7.3 LoRALinear) for the supervised relation classifier. AI-systems students use Deep Learning (P2) ch. 5 for the serving budget instead.
  • One elective, to choose:
    • Topological Data Analysis ch. 2 (Def. 2.9 ToMATo), ch. 6 (Mapper) and ch. 7 (bootstrap stability): compare density-persistence clusters of entity embeddings with Leiden communities for the community summaries;
    • or Optimal Transport ch. 5 (FGW, Example 5.5): align the entity sets of two extraction runs;
    • or Multimodal Generative AI ch. 5 (symmetric InfoNCE): fine-tune the dense retriever on question–paragraph pairs.

Objectives. O5 (core), O3, O4, O1, O7, and O8 through citation faithfulness and the re-identification note of Graph ML 10.3.

Data and resources

  • 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., COLING 2020): multi-hop questions with gold supporting facts and evidence triples. The gold triples score the relation extractor. The four question types (comparison, inference, compositional, bridge comparison) give natural strata.
  • HotpotQA in the distractor setting (Yang et al., EMNLP 2018) as a transfer check; MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., TACL 2022) as an optional harder set.
  • Libraries: spaCy, sentence-transformers, rank_bm25, NetworkX, python-igraph + leidenalg, PyTorch Geometric (RGCNConv; TransE and DistMult in torch_geometric.nn.kge); Microsoft’s open-source graphrag (Edge et al., 2024) as a reference; GUDHI (TDA option), POT (OT option).
  • A 7–8B open-weight instruct model (for example Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct or Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, or a newer model of the same size) served with vLLM or llama.cpp.

Risks and mitigations

  • Relation extraction is never taught: NLP ch. 6 names it and the capstone exports “NER + relations”, but the chapter covers only NER. The 2Wiki gold triples give a supervised signal and a scorer; the team documents its method in one page.
  • The Graph ML final lab names no corpus, LLM or baseline, and ch. 10 is prose only. This brief supplies them.
  • LLM extraction is costly and non-deterministic: cache every call, use temperature 0, log prompts, cap the corpus.
  • Memorisation: Wikipedia facts may already be in the LLM. A closed-book baseline serves as a control.
  • The test set is small: use paired tests and do not over-read differences inside the CI.

Y1.2 Demand-aware fleet dispatch with natural-language bookings

Route AI systems. Domain logistics (urban fleet operations).

Pitch. A city fleet must decide where idle vehicles wait. Forecast zone-level demand from public trip records with signal-processing models and a spatio-temporal GNN. Learn a repositioning policy with RL in a simulator built from the same records, and accept advance bookings written in natural language.

Then measure how forecast errors and parsing errors propagate to the service rate, within a latency budget per decision.

Question. How much of the gain of a learned repositioning policy over greedy dispatch survives when the policy is fed real forecasts (seasonal and AR models, gradient boosting, spatio-temporal GNN) instead of oracle demand? How much service does a natural-language booking parser lose relative to perfectly structured bookings?

Courses used

  • Signal Processing: ch. 5 (digital filtering), ch. 6 (time-frequency analysis), ch. 7 (periodogram, Welch, AR models and Yule–Walker: identify daily and weekly periodicities, AR baselines), ch. 8 (the bridge to graph signal processing: graph Fourier transform, polynomial filters).
  • Graph ML: ch. 4 (spectral filters, ChebNet on the zone graph), 5.6–5.8 (temporal graphs and the causality rule), Example 8.1 (time-respecting split).
  • Reinforcement Learning: ch. 1 (MDP formulation of repositioning), ch. 3 (Q-learning as a tabular sanity check), ch. 5 (REINFORCE, actor-critic, PPO of Theorem 5.5), ch. 6 (6.1 DQN; Def. 6.5 constrained MDP for a cap on empty kilometres; 6.4 MPC as a model-based baseline).
  • Text Mining & NLP: ch. 1 (normalising place names), ch. 6 (sequence labelling, CRF, BIO, entity-level F1), ch. 7 (Protocol 7.1 fine-tuning of a small encoder).
  • Machine Learning ch. 4 (temporal validation, leakage) and ch. 6 (gradient-boosting baseline); Refresher in Computer Science ch. 7 (Dijkstra and A* on the road or zone graph) and ch. 3 (repository template).
  • Elective: Deep Learning (P2 variant) ch. 1 (counting FLOPs and memory), ch. 5 (efficient inference: int8, batching) and its Example 5.4 (the four-column protocol: quality, p50 / p99 latency, throughput, energy).

Objectives. O7 (core: logistics, real-time constraints), O4, O1, O3 (light: slot filling with an encoder or an LLM), O8 (light: robustness to parse errors, service disparity across boroughs).

Data and resources

  • NYC TLC Trip Record Data (New York City Taxi & Limousine Commission): monthly Parquet files for yellow taxis and high-volume for-hire vehicles. The TLC Taxi Zones shapefile (263 zones) gives the zone adjacency graph.
  • MultiWOZ 2.2 (Zang et al., 2020), taxi domain (slots departure, destination, leave-at, arrive-by), for slot filling.
  • Optional: METR-LA and PEMS-BAY (Li et al., ICLR 2018, DCRNN), standard benchmarks for the spatio-temporal forecaster; OSMnx (Boeing, 2017) for road-network travel times.
  • Libraries: DuckDB or pandas, scipy.signal, statsmodels, LightGBM, PyTorch Geometric Temporal (Rozemberczki et al., CIKM 2021), Gymnasium, sklearn-crfsuite, Hugging Face transformers, OR-Tools (assignment baseline), ONNX Runtime.

Y1.3 Text, knowledge graph and bandits for cold-start news recommendation

Route both (the elective changes with the route). Domain recommendation.

Pitch. News articles are cold by nature: they live for hours. Combine text encoders, the knowledge-graph entities attached to each article and a user–news click graph to score fresh items. Then let a contextual bandit decide what to show. One elective adds privacy, serving efficiency or exposure fairness.

Question. For items with no click history, do knowledge-graph entity signals and graph-propagated user representations improve click ranking over text-only encoders? Does a LinUCB or Thompson policy that uses these representations as context earn more clicks than epsilon-greedy and popularity, in a simulator calibrated on held-out logs?

Courses used

  • Text Mining & NLP: ch. 2 (TF-IDF content baseline); ch. 4 (embeddings, SIF); ch. 7 (Sentence-BERT, Protocol 7.1); ch. 8 (8.5: content-based Def. 8.2, matrix factorisation and the hybrid q_i = W x_i of Model 8.3; 8.3: MinHash-LSH to merge near-duplicate stories); Ex. 13.
  • Graph ML: ch. 3 (node2vec on the entity co-occurrence graph); ch. 5 (GraphSAGE or GAT on the user–news bipartite graph); ch. 6 (link prediction, PinSage, TransE entity embeddings); ch. 7 (neighbour sampling); Example 8.1 (time-respecting split).
  • Reinforcement Learning: ch. 4 (UCB1, Alg. 4.4 Thompson sampling, Alg. 4.5 LinUCB, regret); 6.2 (offline-RL caveats, used to frame off-policy evaluation).
  • Machine Learning ch. 4 (AUC, calibration, leakage); Refresher in Statistics ch. 8 (Beta–Bernoulli), Example 3.6 (winner’s curse), 6.6 (multiple testing).
  • One elective, by route:
    • Graph × LLM: Emerging Subjects in ML ch. 3 (FedAvg over simulated user devices) and ch. 5 (DP-SGD on the user encoder, privacy accountant);
    • AI systems: Deep Learning (P2 variant) ch. 5 (two-tower serving, int8, batching) and ch. 1 (cost counting) under a latency SLO;
    • either route: Optimal Transport ch. 6 (exposure fairness across categories by OT post-processing) or Optimization for AI ch. 8 (exposure constraint by stochastic primal–dual).

Objectives. O7 (core: recommendation), O4, O3, O5 (knowledge-graph entities × text), O1, O8 (privacy or fairness variant).

Data and resources

  • MIND, the Microsoft News Dataset (Wu et al., ACL 2020), in its MIND-small version: impression logs with clicked and non-clicked candidates; news title, abstract and category; entities linked to Wikidata, with pre-trained TransE entity embeddings. Microsoft Research licence (non-commercial research use).
  • Optional: the Open Bandit Dataset and Open Bandit Pipeline (Saito et al., NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks; ZOZOTOWN, with a uniformly random logging policy) for an unbiased off-policy check. MovieLens-25M as a fallback.
  • Libraries: recommenders-team/recommenders (NRMS and NAML baselines for MIND), PyTorch Geometric (LightGCN, SAGEConv), sentence-transformers, obp.
  • Elective libraries: Flower and Opacus for the Graph × LLM variant, with FedNewsRec (Qi et al., Findings of EMNLP 2020) as the reference design; ONNX Runtime for the AI-systems variant; POT for the OT variant.

Y2.1 Semi-structured biomedical QA: GraphRAG, GNN retriever or graph tokens, with calibrated abstention

Route Graph × LLM, the flagship for O5. Domain healthcare: biomedical knowledge, not clinical advice.

Pitch. Biomedical questions mix text (“drugs described as kinase inhibitors”) and relations (“… that target a gene associated with disease X”). Over the PrimeKG knowledge base, build three structure-aware retrievers and compare them with a strong hybrid text retriever:

  • community GraphRAG;
  • a GNN subgraph retriever that feeds soft tokens to an LLM, in the G-Retriever style;

Y2.4 The natural-language dispatcher: an LLM agent over exact and learned routing with built-in symmetry

Route AI systems, with a Graph × LLM option. Domain logistics (last-mile delivery). This project continues Y1.2.

Pitch. A dispatcher types “add a 15:00–16:00 pickup at stop 42, van 3 is off after 14:00, re-plan”. The agent turns the request into a validated formulation (grammar-constrained), runs an exact solver and a learned neural routing policy, compares them, explains the change, and serves the result under latency SLOs.

The learned policy is invariant to rotations and reflections of the map by construction. It is compared with policies that only learn the symmetry from augmentation.

Questions

  1. At equal training budget, does building E(2) symmetry into the neural routing policy (an EGNN-style invariant encoder) generalise to unseen city layouts better than symmetry augmentation (POMO and Sym-NCO style)?
  2. Can an LLM agent that delegates to a solver portfolio answer what-if requests on real last-mile routes with route quality close to solver-only and P95 latency within budget? How often does it mis-formalise a request?

Usage-based “done” (examples, on 100 dispatcher requests with gold specifications)

  • Formalisation accuracy at or above an agreed level, with zero silent errors: every change is shown and confirmed.
  • Route cost within an agreed percentage of PyVRP, and P95 response time under an agreed bound.

Y2.5 Fraud alerts you can defend: temporal GNN, spectral descriptors and grounded LLM narratives under attack

Route both. Domain finance: anti-money laundering on a cryptocurrency transaction graph.

Pitch. Detect illicit transactions on a real transaction graph with a strictly temporal protocol. Compare a tuned gradient-boosting model (GBDT), temporal GNNs and spectral structural descriptors. Attach to each alert a narrative written by an LLM, every claim of which is checked against the evidence subgraph. Then audit the whole pipeline for regime shift, evasion attacks and privacy leakage.

Question. Under a strictly temporal protocol, which signals (account features, temporal GNN embeddings, multi-scale heat-kernel descriptors) add detection value over a tuned GBDT? Which of them survive a budgeted edge-injection attack and the regime shift reported late in the Elliptic timeline? Can an LLM write alert narratives in which every factual claim is verifiable against the subgraph?

The geometric hypothesis comes from the HKS stability bound of Geometric Data ch. 3: descriptors that are Lipschitz in the metric should be harder to move with a few injected edges than learned message-passing features.

Usage-based “done” (examples)

  • Recall at a fixed alert budget (for example 100 alerts per time step) above the GBDT baseline, with a CI that excludes zero.
  • 100 % of narrative claims machine-checkable, and at most an agreed share flagged false.
  • A drift alarm that fires within an agreed number of time steps after the known shift.
  • A model card that a compliance reviewer accepts.

  • Large Language Models: ch. 7 (grounded generation over retrieved evidence); 9.3 (hallucination); ch. 6 (serving a small model). Control in Generative AI ch. 3 (constrained narrative template).
  • Boosting to Foundation Models (tabular): ch. 1–4 (GBDT, encodings); ch. 9 (honest benchmarking); Ex. 2 (leakage) and Ex. 7.
  • Security & Robustness: ch. 1 (Def. 1.1); ch. 9 (graph attacks, Nettack-style); ch. 8 (Def. 8.1 model extraction against the scoring API, Def. 8.3 membership inference); Ex. 9 (threat analysis of a bank scoring system).
