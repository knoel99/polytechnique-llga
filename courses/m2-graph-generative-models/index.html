0. Recap: generating graphs in M1

Recap of the Graph ML module (M1 P2, ch. 9) — what is assumed known

  • The problem: sample G \sim p_\theta(G) where p is learned on a family of graphs (active molecules, networks…), with three specific difficulties: variable size, discrete outputs, a space quotiented by isomorphism;

  • The GNN foundation: message passing, MPNN, GIN and the 1-WL bound (cf. Advanced GNN module ch. 1) — the encoder of all the models in this module.

1. The problem, formalized

Concrete scene: a generator that produces statistically close… and chemically invalid graphs. This chapter fixes the space of graphs.

What breaks Ignoring permutation invariance; evaluating without motifs / orbits; masking valence too late.

Definition 1.1 — The labeled graph space and its measure A labeled graph is a triple G = (X, E, n): n nodes, node types X \in \mathcal{X}^n (atoms, tags, features), edge types E \in \mathcal{E}^{n \times n} (single/double/triple bonds…). For fixed n, the space \mathcal{G}_n = \mathcal{X}^n \times \mathcal{E}^{n\times n} is finite (cardinality \sim \|\mathcal{E}\|^{n^2}) but gigantic: with \|\mathcal{E}\| = 4 and n = 30 atoms, 4^{900} \approx 10^{542} adjacencies — hence the need for a strong structural prior. The total space is the disjoint union \mathcal{G} = \bigsqcup_{n \geq 1} \mathcal{G}_n; a law p on \mathcal{G} mixes a law on size p(n) and conditional laws p(G \mid n). Generating = being able to sample from p_\theta \approx p_\star, the empirical law of a dataset \mathcal{D} = \{G_1, \dots, G_N\}.

Definition 1.2 — Exchangeability (in-depth recap) A law p on \mathcal{G} is exchangeable if for every permutation \pi of \{1..n\}: p\big((X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(n)}),\ (\pi E \pi^\top),\ n\big) = p(X, E, n). The Aldous-Hoover theorem — precisely: Aldous (1981) and Hoover (1979), see Lovász (2012) — states that exchangeable laws are exactly those representable by a graphon: x_i \sim \mathcal{U}([0,1]), A_{ij} \sim \mathrm{Bern}(W(x_i,x_j)). Practical consequence: a correct generator must be permutation-invariant, either by construction (one-shot VAEs, diffusion: exchangeable GNN architectures), or on average (autoregressive models: randomize the construction order, cf. ch. 2).

Proposition 1.3 — Aldous-Hoover, forward direction (guided sketch) If p is exchangeable, there exists W such that p is the law of the graphon model.

Proof. Exchangeability \Rightarrow the law of the adjacencies is invariant under every subgroup of permutations; the De Finetti representation for exchangeable arrays (extension of the De Finetti theorem from exchangeable sequences to symmetric matrices) then gives the existence of iid uniform latent variables (x_i) and a conditioning measurable function W: conditionally on the x_i, the A_{ij} are independent with laws W(x_i, x_j). The idea of De Finetti’s proof (sequence case): the empirical frequency \hat F_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \leq k} \mathbf{1}[S_i] of an event converges a.s. to a random variable F (bounded martingale, martingale convergence theorem — cf. Refresher Statistics module); exchangeability forces conditional independence given F, and F plays the role of the latent parameter. The symmetric-array case adds the product structure [0,1]^2. ∎

Theorem 1.4 — Counting to generate: subgraph counting determines the graphon Two graphons W, W' are equal in cut metric \delta_\square iff they have the same homomorphism densities t(F, W) for all graphs F: t(F, W) = \int_{[0,1]^{\|F\|}} \prod_{(i,j) \in E(F)} W(x_i, x_j)\, dx_1 \dots dx_{\|F\|}. Now t(F, W) is estimated on a large graph by the subgraph density: t(F, G_n) \to t(F, W) a.s. (counting lemma). This is the theoretical foundation of ch. 6: comparing generators by their motif statistics (triangles, paths, stars) amounts to comparing their limiting graphons — orbit-MMD evaluation is a finite, empirical version of this theorem.

2. Autoregressive models: DeepGMG and GraphRNN in depth

The founding idea (You et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018): sequentialize the construction — a graph becomes a trajectory of local decisions (add a node, add an edge, choose a type), and all the machinery of sequential models (RNNs, then transformers) applies. Two competing formalizations: by nodes (GraphRNN) and by neighborhood expansion (DeepGMG).

Theorem 2.1 — DeepGMG (Li et al., ICML 2018): factorization by graph states Let G_t denote the partial graph after t decisions. The construction chain defines the factorization p_\theta(G) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(a_t \mid G_{t-1}), where a_t \in \{\textsf{ADDNODE}(x),\ \textsf{ADDEDGE}(e),\ \textsf{STOP}\}, each decision being a categorical parameterized by a graph network: h_{G_{t-1}} = \mathrm{GN}\big(\{h_v\}, \{h_{uv}\}, u\big) \quad\text{(node, edge, and global states)}, and the heads p(a_t \mid h_{G_{t-1}}, h_v) read these states — e.g. p(\textsf{ADDEDGE}(u,v)) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(f_e(h_u \oplus h_v)\big). Three technical points: (i) training follows a canonical order of the data (a fixed arbitrary order, e.g. that of the file); (ii) the likelihood is exact — no variational bound; (iii) the model is exchangeable only on average: if one trains on several random orders (\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[p(G \mid \pi)]), the marginal law is exchangeable.

Proposition 2.2 — GraphRNN (You et al., ICML 2018): two nested RNNs, proven complexity With the BFS order of the nodes, the graph is the sequence of adjacency rows S^i = (A_{i,1}, \dots, A_{i,i-1}) \in \{0,1\}^{i-1}: h_i = \mathrm{RNN}_{node}(h_{i-1}, s_i), \qquad P(S^i \mid h_i) = \prod_{j \< i} \mathrm{Bern}\big(\mathrm{RNN}_{edge}(h_i, s^i_{\<j})\big).

Proof. Complexity. The edge level emits i-1 bits for node i, each in one RNN step: \sum_{i \leq n} (i-1) = \Theta(n^2) steps in total, each in \mathcal{O}(1) with respect to n (the state h_i has fixed size); the node level adds \Theta(n) steps. Training cost per graph: \Theta(n^2) RNN steps versus \Theta(n^3) for a decoder that would recompute the whole history; memory \mathcal{O}(n) states. Why BFS? an arbitrary order makes the prefix S^1..S^k depend on the numbering; the BFS order depends only on the explored neighborhood: two BFS numberings of the same graph differ only by intra-level permutations, and the prefixes remain identical in law — exchangeability is approximate, not exact; aromatic rings “closed at the end of the order” remain the textbook failure case. ∎

Modern variants: when the “token” becomes a node Autoregressive graph generation has been absorbed by the LLM wave: (i) textual representation — generate SMILES (a linearized molecule) with an LLM: this is today’s industrial production (ch. 7); (ii) graph tokenization — define a vocabulary of fragments (scaffolds, synthesis motifs) and generate the graph fragment by fragment, the generalization of junction trees (Jin et al., 2018: decompose the molecule into a tree of chemically valid fragments, generate the tree — structural validity guaranteed by construction); (iii) action-by-action — the chemical agent ChemCrow (Bran et al., 2023) orchestrates tools (synthesis NMR, docking) rather than atoms: the “local decision” becomes a tool call — cf. LLMs module (M2) ch. 8 for the agent layer. The loop is closed: GraphRNN sequentialized the graph for an RNN; LLMs industrialize the same sequentialization.

  • Strengths of autoregressive models: exact likelihood, sequential sampling with natural stopping, local constraints injectable at every step (ch. 8 of the Control in Generative AI module);
  • Limits: \Theta(n^2) cost in steps, exposure to the order, errors that propagate (a wrong node-type choice contaminates everything after it); diffusion (ch. 5) answers exactly these last two limits.

3. VAEs: GraphVAE, NeVAE and valence constraints

3.1. The graph VAE: ELBO and one-shot decoder

Theorem 3.1 — GraphVAE (Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018) and the matching problem GNN encoder q_\phi(z \mid G) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(G), \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2_\phi(G))), one-shot decoder: p_\theta(G \mid z) = p(n \mid z) \cdot p_\theta(\hat A, \hat X \mid z) with continuous adjacency \hat A \in [0,1]^{n\times n} (independent sigmoids) and features \hat X. The likelihood is written against the data via a correspondence matrix (Hungarian matching, an \mathcal{O}(n^3) algorithm): the decoder does not emit in a canonical order, its nodes must be matched to those of G — the price of one-shot decoding. The ELBO: \log p_\theta(G) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid G)}[\log p_\theta(G \mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z\mid G)\,\\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big), proven in the M1 (Multimodal GenAI module ch. 1); here the reconstruction term is not a simple sum of Bernoullis because of the matching — it is lower-bounded by the optimal matching (sum of the log-probabilities over the retained pairs), which preserves the validity of the ELBO.

3.2. NeVAE in detail: valence as the architect

Theorem 3.2 — NeVAE (Samanta et al., AAAI 2019): granular VAE with constrained decoding Four ingredients, to be known precisely:

  1. Sparse encoder: one embedding per atom z_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, MPNN messages between neighboring atoms only — \mathcal{O}(n + m) memory instead of \mathcal{O}(n^2): the molecular graph is sparse, exploiting this divides memory by \Theta(n); the posterior law is a product of local Gaussians q(z_i \mid G) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2);
  2. Remaining valence as state: let r_v^{(t)} \in \{0, \dots, 4\} denote the available valence of atom v at step t (4 for C, 2 for O, 1 for H…). Each decoding head is conditioned on this state: p\big(\text{bond }(u, v, b)\big) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(f\,[\,z_u, z_v, r_u, r_v\,]\big), where the mask forbids any b with b \> r_u or b \> r_v — a saturated carbon (r = 0) can no longer receive a bond, by construction;
  3. Aromaticity: aromatic rings are not alternating single/double bonds but a dedicated type; specific heads sample ring membership — otherwise the model massively underestimates benzene cores;
  4. Noise correction: during training, if a sampled bond violates valence, it is resampled among the legal ones before the loss is computed — the penalty on forbidden bonds is softened and the gradient does not break; measured result: chemical validity > 90% without RDKit sanitization, versus a few percent for unconstrained GraphVAE.

Proposition 3.3 — Why is the valence mask a good statistical idea? Masking the forbidden outputs amounts to replacing p_\theta by its renormalization on the legal subset \mathcal{L}: \tilde p_\theta(y \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(y \mid x)\,\mathbf{1}[y \in \mathcal{L}]} {\sum_{y' \in \mathcal{L}} p_\theta(y' \mid x)}.

Proof. Two effects. (i) Support correction: the forbidden mass (typically \varepsilon per step, but 1 - (1-\varepsilon)^T \to 1 over T steps) is redistributed; the support of \tilde p is included in the valid molecules by construction, which no post-processing guarantees (post-processing repairs, it does not guarantee that the repaired law is close to the target law). (ii) Bias: if the model estimates the ratios of the legal options well, \tilde p_\theta \approx p_\star(\cdot \mid \mathcal{L}) — now molecular datasets are (almost) entirely in \mathcal{L}, so p_\star(\cdot \mid \mathcal{L}) \approx p_\star: the mask is nearly free in bias and massive in validity gain. The same analysis holds for all the decoding masks of the Control in Generative AI module (ch. 3). ∎

From GraphVAE to NeVAE: the general lesson Injecting domain knowledge into the architecture (valence, aromaticity) beats post-processing; this lesson will repeat: DiGress auxiliary losses (ch. 5), chemically valid fragments of junction trees (ch. 2), valence constraints of the Control module (ch. 8). Trade-off: the model becomes specific to one domain (organic chemistry) — for general graphs (social networks), the “hard constraint” analogue does not exist, and that is precisely one of the open problems of ch. 10.

Example 3.4 — NeVAE decoder step by step (by hand) Prefix: C—C—O (remaining valences C: 3, C: 2, O: 1). Next step: choose the next atom and its bond. Heads: p(\text{atom}) = (\text{C}: 0.6,\ \text{O}: 0.3,\ \text{N}: 0.1); suppose C is drawn, attached to the O (the only usable r_v \neq 0 with high probability); bond masked to \{0, 1\} (O has r=1); after fixing C—O, the O has r = 0 — it is closed forever. To be written as a table over 4-5 steps: each row = a draw, the applied mask, the new r_v; exercise 2 schedules it.

4. Normalizing flows: change of variables and GraphAF

4.1. The change of variables, proven

Theorem 4.1 — Additive/affine flows: exact likelihood Let f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d be a diffeomorphism (a differentiable bijection with differentiable inverse) and z \sim p_Z (e.g. \mathcal{N}(0, I)). Then y = f_\theta(z) has density p_Y(y) = p_Z\big(f_\theta^{-1}(y)\big)\,\Big\|\det \frac{\partial f_\theta^{-1}}{\partial y}(y)\Big\| = p_Z(z)\,\Big\|\det \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}(z)\Big\|^{-1}.

Proof. First point: for a diffeomorphism f and a measurable set B, \mathbb{P}(Y \in B) = \mathbb{P}(z \in f^{-1}(B)) = \int_{f^{-1}(B)} p_Z(z)\,dz = \int_B p_Z\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\Big\|\det J_{f^{-1}}(y)\Big\|\,dy by the change of variables of multivariate integration (the volume element is multiplied by the Jacobian in absolute value — cf. Refresher Statistics module, Jacobians chapter); identify the integrand with p_Y(y). Second point: J_{f^{-1}}(y) = \big[J_f(z)\big]^{-1} (derivative of an inverse), and \det(M^{-1}) = 1/\det(M). ∎

Proposition 4.2 — Composition and triangular Jacobians (i) \det(f \circ g)' = \det(f') \cdot \det(g') (product rule for determinants, \det(AB) = \det A \det B): a flow is a composition f = f_L \circ \dots \circ f_1 and the log-Jacobian adds up. (ii) If each f_\ell alters only one coordinate conditionally on the previous ones (coupling: y_a = x_a, y_b = s(x_a) \odot x_b + t(x_a)), its Jacobian is triangular — the determinant is the product of the diagonal elements \prod_i s_i(x_a), computable in \mathcal{O}(d) instead of \mathcal{O}(d^3).

Proof. The Jacobian of a coupling has blocks \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ \ast & \mathrm{diag}(s) \end{pmatrix} — block-triangular, determinant \prod_i s_i. Limiting case: autoregressive masks (MADE): y_i = \tau(x_i\,;h_i(x_{\<i})) give a strictly lower triangular Jacobian with diagonal \partial y_i/\partial x_i — this is exactly the building block of GraphAF below. ∎

4.2. GraphAF: the autoregressive flow for graphs

Theorem 4.3 — GraphAF (Shi et al., ICLR 2020): exact likelihood + guidable sampling GraphAF combines the sequential factorization of ch. 2 with the flow of ch. 4.1: each decision (new atom, new bond) starts from a source z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) transformed by a conditional flow: x_t = f_\theta\big(z_t\,;\ s_t\big), \qquad s_t = \mathrm{GNN}\big(G_{t-1}, \text{candidates}\big), where s_t is the state of the partial graph (MPNN embeddings of the candidate nodes), and the density of the decision is read exactly via Theorem 4.1: \log p_\theta(G) = \sum_t \Big[\log p_Z(z_t) - \log\big\|\det J_{f_\theta}(z_t\,;\ s_t)\Big\|\Big]. Key results: (i) exact likelihood (unlike VAEs); (ii) 68% of valid molecules without chemical rules, 100% with the valence mask of ch. 3 — the mask and the flow are orthogonal; (iii) sampling remains sequential (\Theta(n^2) steps), BUT each step is a draw from a discretized continuous law — hence gradient guidance: to maximize a property Q(G) (solubility, docking), one moves z_t along of \nabla_{z_t} Q\big(G_t\big) during generation: guidance compatible with the exact likelihood — impossible with a VAE whose latent is not in bijective correspondence with the data.

Discrete flows: the cardinal difficulty Theorem 4.1 requires a continuous space (Jacobian!). Two answers: (i) GraphAF stays continuous upstream and discretizes downstream (the flow lives on z); (ii) discrete flips (Tran et al., 2019; Hoogeboom et al., 2021): on categorical variables, replace the Jacobian with a learned correction term — the discrete analogue of the change of variables; GraphDF (Cai et al., 2020) applies them to graphs; each flip is a bijection on a finite set, the “density” becomes a mass law and the log-likelihood remains exact. To be known to exist; the module’s lab implements only the (continuous) GraphAF route, simpler and sufficient for the properties.

# GraphAF skeleton (pedagogical): one conditional flow step
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class CouplingStep(nn.Module):
    """y_b = s(h) * x_b + t(h); diagonal Jacobian = prod(s)."""
    def __init__(self, d):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Linear(64, 2 * d)      # GNN state (64) -> (s, t)
    def forward(self, x_b, h):
        s, t = self.net(h).chunk(2, dim=-1)
        s = torch.sigmoid(s + 2.0)           # s > 0: positive det, stable
        y = s * x_b + t
        return y, s.log().sum(-1)            # y and log|det J|

# generation of an atom/bond decision:
#   h = GNN(G_t); z ~ N(0,1); x, ld = flow(z, h)
#   atom = argmax or sample of softmax(x)  (+ valence mask!)
#   log p(x) = log N(z) - ld   (change of variables theorem)
  • Flow balance sheet: exact likelihood, interpretable latents, gradient guidance — at the price of a sequential cost equal to the autoregressive models and strong architectural constraints (triangular Jacobians);
  • LLGA link: the internal GNN of GraphAF is an MPNN — replacing it by a PathNN (ch. 9) or a graph transformer (cf. Advanced GNN module ch. 4) is an open research question, at the boundary of the two M2 modules.

5. Discrete diffusion: DiGress in detail

Continuous diffusion (Multimodal GenAI module ch. 3) noises with Gaussians — pointless for categorical atom types. DiGress (Vignac et al., ICLR 2023) transposes the machinery to the categorical, via the D3PM framework (Austin et al., 2021): noising = Markov chains on the categories, denoising = closed-form Bayesian posterior. Goal of this chapter: master all the equations.

Definition 5.1 — Forward process by transition matrices A type x (atom among K) is represented as a one-hot X \in \{0,1\}^K. The noising is a Markov chain independent per node and per edge: q(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(X_t;\ Q_t X_{t-1}\big), where Q_t \in [0,1]^{K \times K} is stochastic (rows summing to 1): row k is the law of the new type given the old one k. The usual cases: uniform Q_t = (1 - \beta_t) I + \beta_t \frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top (with probability \beta_t, forget and redraw uniformly); absorbing (toward a mask state [MASK] — the analogue of BERT’s MLM, cf. LLMs module M2 ch. 3); discretized Gaussian (transition toward neighboring types). The composition is exact: q(X_t \mid X_s) = \mathrm{Cat}(X_t\,;\ \bar Q_s^t X_s) with \bar Q_s^t = Q_{s+1} \dots Q_t.

Proposition 5.2 — Stationary marginal (proven in the uniform case) For the uniform kernel above, q(X_t \mid X_0) = \mathrm{Cat}\big((1-\bar\beta_t) X_0 + \bar\beta_t\, \mathbf{u}\big) with \mathbf{u} the uniform law and \bar\beta_t = 1 - \prod_{s \leq t}(1 - \beta_s): at large t = T, the law is (almost) uniform — independent of X_0, the condition for “total” noising.

Proof. By recursion: let p_t = q(X_t \mid X_0) (row vector); p_t^\top = Q_t\, p_{t-1}^\top = \big[(1-\beta_t) I + \frac{\beta_t}{K}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\big] p_{t-1}^\top = (1-\beta_t)\,p_{t-1}^\top + \beta_t\, \mathbf{u}^\top since \mathbf{1}^\top p_{t-1}^\top = 1 (mass law); unrolling, p_t^\top = \big(\prod_s (1-\beta_s)\big) X_0^\top + \big(1 - \prod_s(1-\beta_s)\big)\mathbf{u}^\top. At t = T, the weight of X_0 is \prod_s (1-\beta_s) \approx 0 (schedule \beta_s \to 1): the dataset marginal law is also accessible — one can make it converge toward the empirical marginal \mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i X_0^{(i)} rather than the uniform, by replacing \mathbf{u} with \mathbf{m}: DiGress uses this option (“no bond” edges dominate, reflecting them in the final law helps the denoising). ∎

Theorem 5.3 — Posterior q(X_{t-1} \mid X_t, X_0) in closed form (the heart of DiGress) q(X_{t-1} = j \mid X_t = i,\ X_0 = k) = \frac{(Q_{t-1})_{k j}\,(\bar Q_{t})_{j i}} {(\bar Q_{t-1})_{k i}}, all quantities being rows/columns of the transition matrices — direct computation, no network is involved; the network only serves to estimate \hat X_0.

Proof. Bayes on the chain: q(X_{t-1} \mid X_t, X_0) \propto q(X_t \mid X_{t-1})\, q(X_{t-1} \mid X_0) (Markov: X_t \perp X_0 \mid X_{t-1}). In one-hot, q(X_t = i \mid X_{t-1} = j) = (Q_t)_{j i} and q(X_{t-1} = j \mid X_0 = k) = (\bar Q_{t-1})_{k j}; the denominator is the marginal q(X_t = i \mid X_0 = k) = \sum_j (Q_t)_{j i} (\bar Q_{t-1})_{k j} = (\bar Q_t)_{k i} (matrix product — this is exactly the composition of Definition 5.1). ∎

Theorem 5.4 — DiGress: architecture, losses and topology perturbations

  1. Denoising network: a hybrid GNN (MPNN + global attention layers, graph-transformer style — cf. Advanced GNN module ch. 4) predicts \hat X_0, \hat E_0 = \mathrm{softmax}_K\big(\mathrm{GNN}(X_t, E_t)\big); training maximizes the likelihood of the clean types at the drawn steps t: \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t, G_0}\;\mathbb{E}_{q(X_t, E_t \mid X_0, E_0)} \big[-\log p_\theta(\hat X_0 = X_0) - \log p_\theta(\hat E_0 = E_0)\big]\,;
  2. Chemical auxiliary losses: penalties on derived features — valence violations, bond lengths, angles: weighted by \lambda’s; they do not change the likelihood but steer the denoising toward chemically plausible regions (same lesson as NeVAE ch. 3, transposed);
  3. Noised topology: edges also appear/disappear: at each step, a fraction of edges is redrawn (added/removed) around the density diagonal — without this, the number of edges would be frozen by the conditioning; exchangeability is exact: no node order exists in the process (unlike GraphRNN);
  4. Sampling: from (X_T, E_T) (marginal law), iterate \hat X_0 \to q(X_{t-1} \mid X_t, \hat X_0) (Theorem 5.3); cost: T GNN passes (typically T reduced by discrete DDIM), each \mathcal{O}(n^2) — parallel over all nodes: the contrast with the \Theta(n^2) sequential steps of GraphRNN;
  5. Guidance: classifier-free conditioning (recap from the Multimodal GenAI module ch. 4): DiGress trains the network with a conditional descriptor c sometimes masked; at sampling, \hat X_0^{cfg} \propto \hat X_0^{uncond}\cdot\big(\hat X_0^{cond}/\hat X_0^{uncond}\big)^{w} — the “guided” sample sequences follow the target property (e.g. QED, logP penalty) at a measured cost in diversity (the trade-off of ch. 6 of the Control in Generative AI module);
  6. Inpainting: freeze a scaffold (known types and edges), noise the rest, generate: the posterior 5.3 is constrained trivially (the frozen positions are not denoised) — conditional generation by partial graph without retraining.

Cousins and descendants EDP-GNN (Niu et al., 2020): continuous score matching on the permuted adjacency (with permutation annealing); GDSS (Jo et al., ICML 2022): a coupled node/edge SDE system, solved as a joint continuous diffusion; EDM/EDT (3D): equivariant positions in addition to types — the geometric entry point (cf. Geometric Deep Learning M2 module for equivariance); NGG (ch. 9): the recipe moved into a latent space of graph features. The diffusion family dominates the 2023-2026 molecular state of the art on the quality/exchangeability trade-off — autoregressive models keep the exact likelihood, flows the gradient guidance: ch. 6 shows how to compare them fairly.

6. Evaluating rigorously: MMD, validity, FCD

A graph generator is not scored image by image but distribution against distribution (M1 recap); at the M2 level, we require: the formulas, their biases, and their attacks. Why so much rigor? because evaluation drives research: a truncated metric produces truncated models (ch. 10).

Definition 6.1 — MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy) on graph features For a kernel k on \mathbb{R}^d (e.g. Gaussian k(u,v) = e^{-\\|u-v\\|^2/2\sigma^2}) and a feature \phi : \mathcal{G} \to \mathbb{R}^d (degree histogram, clustering coefficients, orbit counts…): \mathrm{MMD}^2\big(\{G_i\}_{i\leq n}, \{G'_j\}_{j\leq m}\big) = \Big\\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_i k(\phi(G_i), \cdot) - \frac{1}{m}\sum_j k(\phi(G'_j), \cdot)\Big\\|^2_{\mathcal{H}} = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,i'} k_{ii'} + \frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{j,j'} k_{jj'} - \frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i,j} k_{ij}, computable in \mathcal{O}(n^2 + m^2); the estimator is unbiased if one removes the diagonals (\frac{1}{n(n-1)}). In practice: three canonical features (degrees, clustering, orbits) and a Gaussian kernel with median bandwidth.

Proposition 6.2 — Orbits: the right scale of motifs Orbit counts count the occurrences of each orbit of a small graph F (4-5 nodes: triangles, squares, paws…) modulo automorphisms: the number of orbits of 4-node graphs is 15 (not the number of graphs, 6); each node of G is classified into the orbit of its pattern neighborhood. Raw subgraph counts (without quotient by \mathrm{Aut}) over-count: a complete triangle counts 3 times as “closed paths”; the quotient corrects this constant factor per pattern. The theoretical link: these features are exactly empirical t(F, \cdot)’s of Theorem 1.4 — comparing orbit MMDs = comparing the graphons in the limiting sense (up to motif size).

Theorem 6.3 — FCD: the Fréchet ChemNet distance (Preuer et al., 2018) Let e : \text{molecule} \to \mathbb{R}^d be the embedding of an activity-predictor network (ChemNet, pre-trained) assumed approximately Gaussian on each family; \mathrm{FCD}(\mathcal{D}_{gén}, \mathcal{D}_{réf}) = \\|\mu_1 - \mu_2\\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2(\Sigma_1 \Sigma_2)^{1/2}\big), with (\mu_k, \Sigma_k) the means/covariances of the embeddings of the two sets — exactly the d^2_{\mathcal{W}_2} metric of two Gaussians (the analogue of the FID; Multimodal GenAI module ch. 8); the matrix square root is computed via eigenvalues. Key property: the FCD penalizes invalid molecules (no embedding → infinite distance or fixed penalty): it integrates validity and distributional similarity of properties — why it became the reference metric of molecular benchmarks (GuacaMol, MOSES).

The pitfalls of evaluation (to be known by heart)

  1. Validity ≠ quality: 100% validity is obtained by memorizing the dataset (perfect validity, 0 novelty); always report validity and uniqueness and novelty and FCD;
  2. MMD sensitive to sample size: the bias of the estimator grows like \mathcal{O}(1/n); comparing two models with different numbers of generated n is invalid — protocol: same n, same seeds, several trials; significance is tested by bootstrap;
  3. Chosen features = favored families: degrees + clustering ignore the motif-position correlation; a generator can pass the exam on marginal distributions and fail on the joints (molecules whose fragments are right but assembled absurdly); hence the addition of orbits, FCD, and transport distances (Gromov-Wasserstein, Optimal Transport module);
  4. Metric overfitting: optimizing against the FCD (e.g. by guidance) eventually inflates it — honest evaluation keeps one metric kept secret (the held-out principle, cf. healthy benchmarking: Dwivedi et al., JMLR 2023, recalled in the Advanced GNN module ch. 6).

7. Conditional generation and text-to-graph

7.1. Three conditioning mechanisms

Definition 7.1 — Conditioning a graph generator One goes from p_\theta(G) to p_\theta(G \mid c) where c is the conditioning; three mechanisms, with different properties:

  • at the input (concat/fusion): the GNN receives c (property vector, text embedding) — trained on pairs (G, c) or classifier-free (dropout of c); simple, soft control;
  • by guidance: a predictor Q(c \mid G) or a classifier steers the sampling (gradients for flows ch. 4, CFG for diffusion ch. 5); strong control, zero or light retraining, price: diversity;
  • by search: generate massively then filter/optimize (Bayesian optimization in the latent space — the classic QSAR pipeline); exact control a posteriori, high sampling cost.

The control-fidelity / diversity trade-off is quantified in ch. 9 of the Control in Generative AI module; here we retain: for graphs, “hard” conditioning (scaffold, valences) and “soft” (properties, text) combine — inpainting + CFG in DiGress.

7.2. Text-to-graph: the LLM × graphs encounter

Theorem 7.2 — Four text-to-graph routes (2022-2026)

  1. Molecule ↔︎ text translation: MolT5 (Edwards et al., EMNLP 2022) pre-trains a T5 on text + SMILES and treats description → molecule as a translation; the text is the conditioning, the SMILES the target; graph generation is delegated to the SMILES grammar (valence ≈ free);
  2. The LLM writes the generator: instead of generating the graph directly, the LLM writes code (NetworkX) that generates the graph; “Demystifying the Power of LLMs in Graph Generation” (Wang et al., Findings NAACL 2025) shows that network-science prompting (clustering, motifs) suffices to match generators trained on classic families; the agent produces target properties, the code implements them — control moves up one level of abstraction;
  3. Simulator agents: generate dynamic networks by making LLM agents interact (relations, citations, conversations): “Dynamic and Textual Graph Generation via Large-Scale LLM-based Agent Simulation” (Ji et al., 2024) reaches 105 nodes — the structure emerges from the interaction, the text animates the nodes;
  4. LLM + GNN hybrid: the LLM proposes, the GNN evaluates, the loop optimizes (Chen et al., 2023; ChemCrow, Bran et al., 2023: 18 tools including property predictors and docking) — the “proposal/evaluation” loop of ch. 9 of the M1 Graph ML, industrialized by agent tools (cf. LLMs module M2 ch. 8).

Why SMILES won (for now) Generating graphs by direct LLM (edge lists) fails at small scale: local (valence) and global (connectivity) coherence constraints drowned in the long sequence; SMILES encodes valence in the grammar (parentheses, aromaticity): the next token has little illegal freedom — the same lesson as NeVAE (ch. 3): move the constraints from post-processing into the syntax. The counterpart: SMILES is not canonical (one molecule, several encodings; cf. GOAT, ch. 9, on the cost of orders); SELFIES (Krenn et al., 2020) makes the grammar totally valid: every sequence decodes — the practical reference for the 2026 labs.

Example 7.3 — Toy molecular CFG (pen and paper) For the target c = “high logP”; logits of step t of the denoiser without conditioning: (C: 2.0,\ O: 1.5,\ N: 0.5); with conditioning: (C: 3.0,\ O: 0.5,\ N: 0.5); guidance w = 3: guided logits = uncond + w(cond − uncond) = (C: 5.0,\ O: -1.0,\ N: 0.5); probabilities \approx (0.95,\ 0.007,\ 0.04); carbon dominates: high logP = carbon chains — chemically coherent. Increasing w → collapse onto carbon (diversity drops): the fidelity/diversity curve of the Control module, readable on a single step.

8. Applications: drugs, materials, synthetic networks

Generating graphs is not an end in itself: it is the operational arm of inverse design — specify what one wants (a property, a structure, a statistic) and let the model explore the space \mathcal{G} on our behalf. This chapter visits the module’s three application territories, each time with the same three questions: which hard constraints? which objectives? which evaluation?

Definition 8.1 — Inverse design, Pareto, scalarization We have predictors Q_1, \dots, Q_m : \mathcal{G} \to \mathbb{R} (solubility, synthesizability, predicted activity…). Inverse design seeks to maximize a vector objective Q = (Q_1, \dots, Q_m): in general no G optimizes all the Q_i at once (solubility against permeability, activity against toxicity). One defines Pareto dominance: G \succ G' if Q_i(G) \geq Q_i(G') for all i, with at least one strict inequality; the non-dominated ones form the Pareto frontier. Two usual scalarizations bring the problem back to a scalar objective: J(G) = \sum_{i} w_i\, Q_i(G) \quad\text{(weighted)}, \qquad J_{\times}(G) = \prod_{i} \pi_i\big(Q_i(G)\big)^{w_i} \quad\text{(geometric)}, where \pi_i : \mathbb{R} \to [0,1] maps each property into [0,1] (sigmoids, normalization); the geometric form (that of GuacaMol’s MPO tasks) penalizes heavily any collapsed objective: a product does not forgive a zero. The role of the generator: proposing candidates where direct combinatorial optimization (10^{500} adjacencies, ch. 1) is hopeless — the gradient guidance of ch. 4, the CFG of ch. 5 and the reinforcement-learning optimization of ch. 7 are the three interfaces between p_\theta and J.

Theorem 8.2 — Drug discovery: where does the generator plug in? The industrial pipeline, simplified but faithful:

  1. Target and hypothesis: a protein involved in a disease;
  2. Proposal: the generator samples G \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c) (c = target descriptor, properties) or optimizes J in the latent space — GraphAF guidance (ch. 4), DiGress CFG (ch. 5), RL policies on SMILES (industrial pipelines of the REINVENT type);
  3. Filtering: validity (valence, ch. 3), QED — the “drug-likeness” score on [0,1] (Bickerton et al., 2012) —, synthesizability SA, logP, diversity (ch. 6);
  4. Costly evaluation: docking (predicted interaction energy), ADMET models (absorption, toxicity) — each step costs orders of magnitude more than the previous one;
  5. Synthesis and trials: the real bottleneck — hence the operational metric: the quality of the top-k (the k candidates actually synthesized), not the batch average.

An often-ignored consequence: optimizing the mean of J over 104 molecules can be worse than optimizing the maximum — the useful generator is the one that explores the corners of the Pareto frontier; this is exactly the fidelity/diversity curve of the Control in Generative AI module (ch. 9), reread from the application side.

Molecular benchmarks: ZINC, MOSES, GuacaMol

  • ZINC (Sterling & Irwin, 2015): the database of purchasable compounds — the reservoir; the subset ZINC-250k (≈ 250,000 lead-like molecules, popularized by grammar VAEs) is the standard training set of chapters 3-5 (NeVAE, GraphAF train and evaluate on it);
  • MOSES (Polykovskiy et al., 2020): ~1.9M ZINC molecules with scaffold split (the test scaffolds are unknown at training time) and standardized metrics: validity, uniqueness, novelty, FCD, scaffold similarity — the “distribution-learning” protocol of ch. 6;
  • GuacaMol (Brown et al., 2019): two families of tasks — distribution-learning (reproduce a library: FCD, KL divergences on physico-chemical properties, validity/novelty) and goal-directed (rediscover a given molecule, similarity, isomers, and the MPO tasks: maximize the geometric product of properties — Definition 8.1 in action).

The common trap: metric learning (ch. 6, point 4); on goal-directed tasks, a model can memorize the targets — hence the evaluation on targets kept secret. These benchmarks are to molecular generation what CIFAR/ImageNet are to vision (cf. Deep Learning module): convenient stops, not the destination.

Theorem 8.3 — Materials: the periodic constraint (CDVAE, MatterGen) A crystal is not a molecule: it is a triple (\Lambda, X, L) — lattice \Lambda (three cell vectors), positions X of n atoms in the cell, the invariance being the group of integer translations: every quantity (distances, energies, densities) is computed modulo the periodic images. Two models bound the domain:

  1. CDVAE (Xie et al., ICLR 2022): a VAE whose decoder denoises by diffusion; the initialization places the atoms far from any equilibrium and the diffusion makes them “fall” toward realistic bonds — the physical inductive bias (stability is a local energy minimum) is inscribed in the dynamics: the exact analogue of the valence mask of ch. 3, moved into the space of positions;
  2. MatterGen (Zeni et al., Nature 2025): joint diffusion on atom types, coordinates and lattice — with conditioning by adapter: the base model (learned on the whole periodic table) is frozen, a small per-task module redirects the denoising toward the target property (composition, symmetry, magnetism…) — the industrial analogue of the classifier-free guidance of ch. 5: one large model, a thousand cheap adaptations.

Evaluation specific to materials: stable fraction (predicted energy close to the convex hull of known phases), novelty against crystallographic databases (the analogue of the novelty of ch. 6), symmetry validity (space groups). The module’s lesson repeats: periodicity is a hard constraint — it is architectural (equivariance to translations, cf. Geometric Deep Learning M2 module), never a post-processing.

Proposition 8.4 — Synthetic networks: generating to protect Sharing a real graph (contacts, transactions, calls) means exposing individuals: the structure itself re-identifies — knowing the neighbors of a node suffices to find it again in most real networks, and anonymizations by masking are systematically broken. Generating a synthetic network that reproduces the useful statistics (degrees, clustering, motifs — the features of ch. 6) without copying the individuals is the alternative; NetGAN (Bojchevski et al., ICML 2018) opened the way: learn the distribution of the graph’s biased random walks (a GAN on node sequences), then reconstruct the adjacency from the co-occurrences of the walks — the graph is never copied, only its “walk signature” is.

Proof. The fidelity/confidentiality trade-off, quantified. Fidelity is measured by MMD on degrees/clustering/orbits (ch. 6); confidentiality by node-level membership inference tests (the graph counterpart of the attacks of the Security and Robustness M2 module): a generator that memorizes (overfitting on a single graph) spits out exact subgraphs — detectable, real damage. Mastering the trade-off goes through (i) the regularization of p_\theta (limiting memorization as one limits overfitting — cf. Machine Learning module ch. 7); (ii) noise at the node/edge level (the analogue of the Gaussian mechanism of DP — cf. Emerging ML ch. 5 and Privacy and Uncertainty M2 modules); (iii) evaluation on both sides: publishing a synthetic network without an attack test is a rerun of the “100% validity” of ch. 6 — half a proof. ∎

Example 8.5 — Pareto by hand (pen and paper) Four candidates, two normalized objectives (Q_1 = predicted activity, Q_2 = synthesizability): A = (0.9;\ 0.2), B = (0.7;\ 0.7), C = (0.6;\ 0.9), D = (0.2;\ 0.95). Dominance: does B dominate D? 0.7 \> 0.2 but 0.7 \< 0.95 — no; step by step, all four are on the frontier. Weighted scalarization J = w Q_1 + (1-w) Q_2: at w = 0.3, C wins (J = 0.81) ahead of D (0.725) and B (0.70); at w = 0.6, C prevails again (0.72) against B (0.70) and A (0.62); A overtakes C only for w \geq 0.7 — the ranking pivots with the weights: report the frontier, never an isolated argmax. Geometric version J_\times = Q_1 Q_2: C (0.54) and B (0.49) dominate, A (0.18) and D (0.19) are eliminated — unbalanced profiles are crushed: the signature of MPO.

9. Research of the LLGA team

The LLGA master is driven by the DaSciM laboratory (Data Science and Mining, LIX, CNRS — École Polytechnique: M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer, G. Nikolentzos and their PhD students), the host laboratory of the internships and PhDs. This chapter presents four of its works, chosen because they touch directly on the objects of this module: the encoder of the generators (PathNN), the order question (GOAT), a latent-diffusion generator conditioned by features (NGG) and the LLM × graphs frontier (pseudo-code prompting). The other axes of the laboratory are covered by the Graph ML (ch. 10) and Advanced GNN (ch. 8) modules; here, each paper reads as the extension of a chapter.

Work 9.1 — PathNN: aggregating paths, not neighbors (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Standard message passing (ch. 0) aggregates immediate neighbors; PathNN aggregates paths starting from the node: h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\Big(\big\{\, \mathrm{LSTM}\big(h_v^{(0)}, h_{u_1}^{(0)}, \dots, h_{u_\ell}^{(0)}\big)\ :\ (v, u_1, \dots, u_\ell) \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} \,\big\}\Big), where \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} is a set of paths of length \ell — three variants: shortest paths (to all destinations), random walks, sampled simple paths. Two reasons to be interested here: (i) expressivity: paths of length \ell capture multi-hop dependencies that \ell layers of MPNN irretrievably mix — strictly above 1-WL on chosen pairs (cf. Advanced GNN module ch. 1-2); since the number of simple paths grows exponentially, sampling \mathcal{P}_v bounds the cost: an expressivity/cost trade-off tuned in the encoder; (ii) role in this module: PathNN is an encoder — the natural candidate for the q_\phi(z \mid G) of the VAEs (ch. 3) and the backbone of the NGG below; replacing the MPNN with a more expressive encoder is the simplest improvement lever for latent-based generators.

Work 9.2 — GOAT: rehabilitating the order of neighbors (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) The whole module has run into order: autoregressive models impose a canonical order on it (BFS, ch. 2), the one-shot decoder must match its nodes (Hungarian matching, ch. 3). GOAT attacks the root: the usual aggregators (sum, mean, max, attention) are invariant — they throw away all order information between neighbors, while this order carries signal (synergies, redundancies between neighbors). The GOAT layer is a permutation-sensitive attention: each node learns a local order of its neighborhood (position scores) and encodes the neighbors in that learned order — global invariance is preserved (the order is a function of the graph, not of a numbering), local sensitivity is restored. Results: prediction of centrality metrics (betweenness…) where invariant GNNs struggle. The “generation” reading: an encoding that distinguishes neighborhoods that 1-WL confounds gives the latent more to say — the flip side of the matching problem of ch. 3: better encoding the order means better recovering it at decoding.

Work 9.3 — NGG: latent diffusion conditioned by graph features (Evdaimon, Nikolentzos, Xypolopoulos, Kammoun, …, Vazirgiannis, 2024) The Neural Graph Generator applies the latent diffusion recipe to graphs (recap: Multimodal GenAI module ch. 3-4):

  1. Encoder: a GNN compresses G \mapsto h_G (latent);
  2. Conditioned latent diffusion: a denoiser learns to denoise h_G given a vector of target features c — spectrum of the Laplacian (the first eigenvalues), degree statistics, clustering coefficient… descriptors that are global, interpretable, measurable;
  3. Decoder: from the denoised latent to the adjacency. Sampling: choose c — the statistical “specification sheet” — then generate.

Position with respect to the chapters: DiGress (ch. 5) denoises the complete object (types, edges); NGG denoises a representation — the exchangeability is that of the encoder, the per-step cost much lower, and the conditioning is “macro” (global features) where the molecular CFG of ch. 7 is “micro” (a property of one molecule). The evaluation is that of ch. 6: MMD on degrees/clustering/orbits between generated graphs and target families (SBM, citation networks…). It is also the “synthetic networks” building block of ch. 8: specify a spectrum and a degree distribution, obtain whole graphs.

Work 9.4 — Pseudo-code prompting: reasoning about graphs with programs (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024) A series of graph problems (connectivity, shortest path, existence of cycles, maximum flow, spanning tree…) is submitted to LLMs: the same instruction formulated in pseudo-code (algorithmic specification: loops, conditions, invariants) systematically beats its natural-language formulation — on both axes: understanding (executing the described algorithm) and producing (writing the algorithm for the posed problem). The reading for this module: (i) it is the experimental confirmation of route 2 of ch. 7 (“the LLM writes the generator”): the program is the most reliable graph representation for an LLM — unambiguous, verifiable, executable; (ii) the natural bridge with tool-using agents (ch. 7, ChemCrow): describe the task in pseudo-code then have it verified by a solver (NetworkX) — the proposal/verification loop of the Control module (ch. 10), instantiated on graphs.

10. Open problems

The grid of ch. 1 (exchangeability, likelihood, constraints) closes here: four work sites where it still has no satisfactory answer — and a fifth, theoretical one. For each: the 2026 state, the promising direction, and the master’s modules that contribute to it.

Problem 10.1 — Evaluation: a metric without a theory of the measure Chapter 6 showed the biases; the heart of the problem is that there is no canonical distance on \mathcal{G}: the edit distance is NP-hard to compute, Gromov-Wasserstein costs \mathcal{O}(n^3) per pair (cf. Optimal Transport module), and MMD depends on an arbitrary choice of features and kernel — a generator can satisfy degrees + clustering + orbits and fail on every untested statistic. The FCD itself depends on a frozen bioactivity predictor (ChemNet, 2015): the metric ages with it. Promising directions: (i) task-based evaluation (do the generated graphs improve a downstream predictor, as augmentation data?); (ii) learned “critics” (a generated/real classifier trained to discriminate — if it succeeds, the distribution is betrayed); (iii) cheap surrogates of GW. Methodological reference: the lesson of healthy benchmarking (Dwivedi et al., JMLR 2023 — Advanced GNN module ch. 6).

Problem 10.2 — Global hard constraints The valence mask (ch. 3) is local: each decision only looks at the remaining valences — hence its tractability. The useful constraints are often global: connectivity, cycle sizes, total number of edges, quotas per type, stability. The automaton that would enforce them at decoding is exponential (the technical point of the Control module ch. 3, on text, carries over as is); the auxiliary losses of DiGress (ch. 5) penalize them without guaranteeing them; post-processing repairs them at the price of the bias of ch. 3. Direction: constrained generation with guarantee — projection at each diffusion step onto the admissible set (the discrete posterior of thm. 5.3 is no longer in closed form as soon as it is constrained: the whole challenge), solvers in the loop, learned rejection methods. This is the shared work site with the Control in Generative AI module (ch. 8).

Problem 10.3 — Scaling up All the complexities of the module are at best \mathcal{O}(n^2) per object: square adjacency, \Theta(n^2) sequential steps for GraphRNN, T GNN passes for DiGress — and \mathcal{O}(n^2) tokens to describe a graph to an LLM (ch. 7). Real networks reach 108-109 nodes. Leads: generation by fragments (junction trees, ch. 2 — the fragment as the decision unit divides the number of steps); latent generation (NGG, ch. 9: one denoises a fixed-size representation); training on subgraphs (mini-batch — at the price of a sampling bias); sparsification. Underlying open question: sample complexity — how many graphs are needed to learn a law at large n? (the generational counterpart of the generalization problem of the Statistical Learning Theory module).

Problem 10.4 — The integration of LLMs Chapter 7 showed four routes; their blind spots: (i) tokenization — no canonical serialization of graphs is satisfactory (SMILES is not canonical, the edge list is \mathcal{O}(n^2) tokens, cf. GOAT ch. 9 on the cost of orders); (ii) guarantee — the LLM hallucinates invalid adjacencies; the remedy is grammatical (SELFIES) or agentic (the tool verifies); (iii) memorization — an LLM can spit out training graphs (the privacy problem of ch. 8, displaced); (iv) evaluation — text-to-graph does not yet have its GuacaMol. Directions: graph foundation models (multi-family pre-training, adaptation by prompt — cf. Boosting and Foundation Models M2 module), LLM + GNN hybrids (propose/verify), and standardized evaluation (the “code” protocol of ch. 7 as a provisional standard).

Problem 10.5 — Theory: beyond exchangeability and likelihood Two frontiers. (i) Limits: the Aldous-Hoover theorem (ch. 1) describes exchangeable laws — now real networks grow (preferential attachment), evolve (temporal), and their limits require non-exchangeable extensions (graphexes): what is the theory of neural generators on these objects? (ii) Likelihood against quality: the ELBO of VAEs and diffusions (ch. 3, 5) is an uncontrolled bound — the gap between ELBO and log-likelihood can be enormous without the samples suffering, or the opposite; and for autoregressive models (ch. 2), the exact likelihood depends on an arbitrary order — what invariant quantity do they really optimize? These two questions condition any theory of learning for graph generators — the natural extension of the Statistical Learning Theory and Advanced Deep Learning modules.

(a) Write the masked sequential decoder (state = remaining valences r_v, Proposition 3.3); (b) sample 1,000 molecules without the mask (unconstrained heads, logits \mathcal{N}(0,1)) then with the mask, up to 12 atoms; (c) measure the validity (all valences exactly zero at the end of generation) and compare it with the reasoning of Proposition 3.3.

    Proof. Solution: without the mask, each sampled bond violates a valence with increasing probability as generation proceeds (the r_v are no longer respected); the observed validity is typically a few percent at 12 atoms — and 1-(1-\varepsilon)^T \to 1 explains the collapse (prop. 3.3). With the mask: 100% validity by construction (the support is restricted to legal outputs), at the price of a slight bias if the logits are poorly calibrated — the renormalization redistributes the forbidden mass onto the legal ones. The code: maintain r_v, forbid bonds b with b \> r_u or b \> r_v, resample among the legal ones — eight lines, the most profitable investment of the module. ∎

  3. The absorbing posterior, in closed form (ch. 5). For the absorbing kernel (stay in state k with probability \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, be masked otherwise: sink state [\textsf{MASK}]), prove: q(X_{t-1} = X_0 \mid X_t = \textsf{MASK},\ X_0) = \frac{A_{t-1}\,\beta_t}{1 - A_t}, \qquad A_t = \prod_{s \leq t} \alpha_s, and check that the remaining mass is on X_{t-1} = \textsf{MASK}.

    Proof. Solution: X_t = \textsf{MASK} occurs with probability 1 - A_t (complement of “never masked up to t”); jointly X_{t-1} = X_0, X_t = \textsf{MASK} requires “never masked up to t-1 then masked exactly at t”: probability A_{t-1}\beta_t. The quotient gives the formula; the remaining mass equals (1 - A_{t-1})/(1 - A_t) — the two sum to 1 since 1 - A_t = (1 - A_{t-1}) + A_{t-1}\beta_t. This is the D3PM-absorbing posterior: when X_t is masked, X_{t-1} is never revealed — the network must guess it (\hat X_0) then sample it with these probabilities: the inpainting machine of ch. 5. ∎

    Proof. Hints: (a) the Jacobian is block-triangular \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ \ast & \mathrm{diag}(s) \end{pmatrix} (prop. 4.2): the determinant is the product of the diagonals; (b) chain rule on z_t \mapsto x_t = f_\theta(z_t) \mapsto G_t; since the discretization is not differentiable, one replaces it with a relaxation (temperature softmax) or propagates back the gradient of the continuous predictor: the guidance step moves z_t without changing the weights — but each displacement pulls the trajectory away from the learned law: too large, it fabricates out-of-distribution graphs that the predictor scores well but that chemistry rejects (the fidelity/diversity trade-off of the Control module ch. 9). ∎

  6. Step-function graphon and triangle density (ch. 1 and 6). Let W be constant by blocks on [0,1]^2 with two blocks: density p in the block 1 (x, y \leq \tfrac{1}{2}), q in block 2, r between blocks. (a) Show t(K_3, W) = \tfrac{1}{8}\big(p^3 + q^3 + 3 p r^2 + 3 q r^2\big) (integral over the 8 block combinations); (b) verify numerically with p = 0.9, q = 0.6, r = 0.05 and by sampling a graph with n = 100 nodes (50/50) — why do the two coincide to within \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})?

    Proof. Solution: each triplet (a, b, c) of blocks contributes \tfrac{1}{8} W_{ab} W_{bc} W_{ac}; (1,1,1) gives p^3, (2,2,2) gives q^3, the three permutations of (1,1,2) give p r^2 each, those of (2,2,1) give q r^2. Numerically t = (0.729 + 0.216 + 0.0068 + 0.0045)/8 \approx 0.1195. The sampling realizes Bernoulli variables with parameters W(x_i, x_j), iid conditionally on the latents: the empirical triangle density is an average of bounded variables — concentration (Hoeffding) around t(K_3, W) at rate \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n}): this is the counting lemma of Theorem 1.4, verified hands in the mud. ∎

  7. FCD in dimension 2 (ch. 6). Two Gaussian embedding families of diagonal \mathbb{R}^2: \mu_1 = (0, 0), \Sigma_1 = \mathrm{diag}(1, 4); \mu_2 = (1, 0), \Sigma_2 = \mathrm{diag}(4, 9). Compute the Fréchet distance; what does it become if the two families have the same mean?

    Proof. Solution: for diagonal covariances, (\Sigma_1 \Sigma_2)^{1/2} is diagonal with square roots of the products: \sqrt{1 \times 4} = 2, \sqrt{4 \times 9} = 6. Hence \\|\mu_1 - \mu_2\\|^2 = 1 and \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2(\Sigma_1\Sigma_2)^{1/2}) = (1 + 4 + 4 + 9) - 2(2 + 6) = 2: FCD = 3. With equal means, only the trace remains (here 2): the FCD separates means and covariances — why it detects at once a shift in mean properties (logP…) and an impoverishment of diversity (reduced variances), the two typical failures of generators. ∎
