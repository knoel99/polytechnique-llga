0. Review: SGD and its analysis

Every algorithm of this module — distributed, federated, robust or private — is a variant of stochastic gradient descent in which the gradient is averaged, delayed, compressed, filtered or noised. To measure what each variant costs, one first needs the exact convergence rate of plain SGD and the reason why its noise cannot be beaten; this review proves both.

L3/M1 review — the stochastic foundation

  • Gradient descent: \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla f(\theta_t); convex and L-smooth ⇒ \mathcal{O}(1/t) decrease.
  • SGD: replace \nabla f with a batch gradient g_t = \frac1m \sum_{i \in B_t} \nabla \ell(\theta_t; x_i), unbiased, \mathrm{Var}(g_t) = \mathcal{O}(1/m) (CLT, see the Refresher Statistics module).
  • Strong convexity ⇒ linear convergence in the deterministic case, \mathcal{O}(1/t) in the stochastic case; the definition reads f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \leq \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y) - \tfrac{\mu}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\|x - y\|^2 .
  • Descent inequality — the basic lemma for this entire chapter: for f L-smooth, f(\theta_{t+1}) \leq f(\theta_t) - \eta(1 - \tfrac{\eta L}{2})\|\nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2 .

Working assumptions (A1)-(A3)

  • (A1) f convex, L-smooth, with minimizer \theta^\star (write D = \|\theta_0 - \theta^\star\|, f^\star = f(\theta^\star));
  • (A2) unbiased stochastic gradient \mathbb{E}[g_t \mid \theta_t] = \nabla f(\theta_t);
  • (A3) bounded variance \mathbb{E}\|g_t - \nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2 \leq \sigma^2.

We write g_t = \nabla f(\theta_t) + \xi_t with \mathbb{E}\xi_t = 0.

Theorem 0.1 — Constant-step SGD: full proof Under (A1)-(A3), with step size \eta \leq \frac{1}{2L}:

Key result — constant-step SGD \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}\big[f(\theta_t) - f^\star\big] \;\leq\; \frac{D^2}{\eta T} \;+\; \eta \sigma^2 \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{L D^2}{T}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt T}\Big).

The last term is obtained for \eta = \min\big(\tfrac{1}{2L}, \tfrac{D}{\sigma\sqrt T}\big).

Proof. Step 1 (recursion on the distances). With \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta g_t and \mathbb{E}[\xi_t \mid \theta_t] = 0 (the cross term vanishes): \mathbb{E}\|\theta_{t+1} - \theta^\star\|^2 = \|\theta_t - \theta^\star\|^2 - 2\eta \big\langle \nabla f(\theta_t),\, \theta_t - \theta^\star \big\rangle + \eta^2\, \mathbb{E}\|g_t\|^2.

Step 2 (the three ingredients).

    1. convexity: \langle \nabla f(\theta_t), \theta_t - \theta^\star\rangle \geq f(\theta_t) - f^\star ;
    1. bias-variance decomposition + smoothness, since \|\nabla f(\theta)\|^2 \leq 2L(f(\theta) - f^\star) for f convex and L-smooth: \mathbb{E}\|g_t\|^2 = \|\nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\xi_t\|^2 \leq 2L\big(f(\theta_t) - f^\star\big) + \sigma^2 .

Combining: \mathbb{E}\|\theta_{t+1} - \theta^\star\|^2 \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}\|\theta_t - \theta^\star\|^2 \;-\; 2\eta(1 - \eta L)\,\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \;+\; \eta^2 \sigma^2.

Step 3 (telescoping). Sum over t = 0, \dots, T-1: the distance terms telescope and their sum is at most D^2. With \eta \leq \frac{1}{2L} (hence 2\eta(1 - \eta L) \geq \eta): \eta \sum_t \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \;\leq\; D^2 + T\eta^2\sigma^2, that is \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \;\leq\; \frac{D^2}{\eta T} + \eta \sigma^2.

With the step size \eta = \min\big(\tfrac{1}{2L}, \tfrac{D}{\sigma\sqrt T}\big), the first term is at most \frac{2LD^2}{T} + \frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt T} and the second at most \frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt T}, which gives the last inequality. ∎

Reading. Two terms: optimization (D^2/\eta T, vanishes with T) and statistics (\eta\sigma^2, the “floor” of the constant step size); the choice of \eta arbitrates.

In practice, networks are trained with constant step sizes (or step sizes decreasing in stages). With a decreasing step size \eta_t \propto 1/t, the average of the iterates reaches \mathcal{O}(\log T / T), and \mathcal{O}(\sigma^2 / \mu T) if f is \mu-strongly convex.

Theorem 0.2 — Lower bounds (statements)

    1. Stochastic oracle (Nemirovski-Yudin): for any algorithm accessing f only through noisy gradients of variance \sigma^2, there exists a convex, L-smooth function such that \mathbb{E}[f(\hat\theta_T) - f^\star] \geq c\, \sigma D/\sqrt T . The rate of Theorem 0.1 is therefore optimal; the noise is not a flaw of the algorithm but a law of the problem.
    1. Strongly convex: the bound becomes \Omega(\sigma^2/\mu T): the statistical floor cannot be beaten, even with all the available structure.

Consequence for this module: parallelizing (ch. 2) can do no better than reducing \sigma — which is exactly what the aggregation of K workers does.

Proposition 0.3 — Variance reduction: SAGA and SVRG (sketch) The \sigma/\sqrt T floor comes from the constant variance of the gradient; on a finite sum f = \frac1n \sum_i f_i, it can be made to decrease:

  • SVRG (Johnson & Zhang): periodically compute the exact gradient \nabla f(\tilde\theta) at a reference point \tilde\theta, then use the control variate v_t = \nabla f_{i_t}(\theta_t) - \nabla f_{i_t}(\tilde\theta) + \nabla f(\tilde\theta) . It is unbiased, with variance proportional to \|\theta_t - \tilde\theta\|^2 — which decreases over the course of the optimization; linear rate \mathcal{O}((n + \kappa)\log 1/\varepsilon) (versus \mathcal{O}(n\kappa \log 1/\varepsilon) for GD);
  • SAGA: keep in a table the latest \nabla f_i seen for each i and correct by their average — same effect, without periodic recomputation.

These control variate ideas will reappear in federated learning (SCAFFOLD, ch. 3) and in the Byzantine setting (Bulyan, ch. 8): this is THE algorithmic motif of the module.

Proposition 0.4 — Momentum, Nesterov, sampling

  • Momentum (heavy ball): v_{t+1} = \beta v_t + g_t, \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta v_{t+1} — damps oscillations along steep directions (poorly conditioned gradient covariance matrix); typical choice \beta = 0.9.
  • Nesterov: evaluate the gradient at the anticipated point \theta_t - \gamma v_t; in the deterministic case, the rate goes from \mathcal{O}(LD^2/T) to \mathcal{O}(LD^2/T^2) — optimal for a first-order method.
  • Weighted sampling: draw i according to a distribution q and correct with g = \nabla f_i/q(i); the variance is minimized when q_i \propto \|\nabla f_i\| (importance sampling, Zhao & Zhang, 2014). The stratified variant partitions the data into homogeneous strata and allocates the sampling budget where the internal variance is large — stratification reduces \sigma^2 even before parallelizing.

1. Landscape of emerging topics

Concrete scene: 50 hospital clients, 2 malicious. Gradient averaging pulls the model toward a backdoor. This chapter sets the threat model before recipes.

What breaks Three classic failures: trusting the plain mean when some clients may be Byzantine, releasing a model trained without any privacy budget, and scaling the batch without retuning the noise and the learning rate.

This module opens with the questions that structure current research and industry, in direct preparation for the M2 modules (LLMs, Explainability/Security/Privacy):

  • Scaling laws: power-law loss as a function of data/parameters/compute (Kaplan 2020, Hoffmann/Chinchilla 2022 — chapter 7); emergence of capabilities; compute as a scarce resource;
  • Distributed training of large models: data/tensor/pipeline parallelism, MoE (chapters 2 and 7);
  • Frugal fine-tuning: LoRA and adapters — the “collaboration” between a large central model and local uses (chapters 3 and 7);
  • Trustworthy? Robustness to malicious clients, privacy of training data, fairness across participants — the three guiding threads of chapters 4-6;
  • Collaborative agents: multi-agent orchestration = collaboration of models (direct link with the Reinforcement Learning and Autonomous Agents module, chapter 7, and with “graph engineering”).

1.1. From a theme to a research question

The official description of the course unit asks you to formulate your own research questions and to work on them in groups; the research project that is graded is the answer to one of them. A theme such as “Byzantine robustness” is not yet a question: a question is something that a proof or an experiment can settle, in either direction, within the time of the course.

Definition 1.1 — Well-posed research question In this module, a research question has the form “under assumptions A, does method M achieve property P, measured by the quantity Q, better or worse than the baseline B?”. It is well posed when:

  1. every term is defined in these notes or in one reference paper;
  2. both answers are possible before the work starts, so the question is falsifiable;
  3. Q can be computed in the time available — on a small model, a synthetic benchmark, or by a proof on a quadratic example;
  4. a hypothesis — the expected value or trend of Q — is written down before the experiment, so that the result is able to contradict it.

Worked example — robust aggregation meets heterogeneity Theme: Byzantine robustness (ch. 4) inside federated learning (ch. 3). “Is federated learning robust?” is too vague; “does the median protect FedAvg?” is still undecidable, because “protect” is undefined.

A well-posed version: K = 20 clients hold the losses F_k(\theta) = \tfrac12\|\theta - z_k\|^2, and f = 2 of them are Byzantine; the 18 honest optima z_k have empirical mean \bar z and are spread, coordinate by coordinate, like a Gaussian of standard deviation \zeta.

How does the error Q = \|\theta_R - \bar z\| of FedAvg with coordinate-wise median aggregation grow with \zeta, compared with the plain mean without attack (B)?

Hypothesis, derived before running anything: when the two attackers send very large values on a coordinate, the median of the 20 values becomes the average of the 10th and 11th smallest of the 18 honest values, which sits near their 10/18 \approx 0.556 quantile instead of their median.

For a Gaussian spread this quantile lies at \Phi^{-1}(0.556)\,\zeta \approx 0.14\,\zeta above the centre. We therefore predict a bias of about 0.14\,\zeta per attacked coordinate — zero when the clients agree (\zeta = 0), growing linearly with heterogeneity.

The experiment then plots Q against \zeta \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\} and checks the slope.

Why the question is worth asking: when \zeta > 0, an attacker who stays inside the honest spread looks exactly like an unusual honest client, so no aggregation rule can remove all of this bias — the measured slope tells how much a given rule concedes.

Group work and the question sheet Our suggested format (the coordinator fixes the actual one): groups of three or four; each group keeps a one-page question sheet listing the theme, the question in the form of Definition 1.1, its assumptions, the quantity Q, the baseline B, the hypothesis, the planned experiment or proof, and the reference paper. The sheet is drafted in session 2, revised as the chapters 2 to 6 supply new tools, and fixed in session 9; it becomes the introduction of the project report, and its reference paper is a natural candidate for the third paper-reading session.

2. Distributed learning

One GPU is too slow for a large model, so the gradient computation is split across K workers that must then agree on a single update. This chapter quantifies the gain (the variance of the averaged gradient falls like 1/K) and the price — communication — and presents the techniques that reduce the bytes exchanged: quantization, sparsification, asynchrony and decentralized gossip.

Definition 2.1 — Distributed SGD (synchronous workers) K workers hold data shards. At each step: each worker k computes g^{(k)} on its mini-batch, the server aggregates and broadcasts: \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^K g_t^{(k)}.

Theorem 2.2 — Linear speedup (proof via the variance) If the g_t^{(k)} are independent, unbiased, with variance \sigma^2, then \bar g_t = \frac1K \sum_k g_t^{(k)} is unbiased with \mathbb{E}\|\bar g_t\|^2 = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{K}, and Theorem 0.1 applies with \sigma \to \sigma/\sqrt K:

Key result — linear speedup \frac1T \sum_t \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \leq \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{LD^2}{T} + \frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt{KT}}\Big).

Proof. Independence + unbiasedness give \mathbb{E}\|\bar g\|^2 = \|\mathbb{E}\bar g\|^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\bar g) = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{1}{K^2}\sum_k \mathrm{Var}(g^{(k)}) = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{K}. To reach a target accuracy \varepsilon = \sigma D/\sqrt{T}, one needs T(\varepsilon) \propto \frac{\sigma^2 D^2}{K\,\varepsilon^2} distributed iterations — versus \frac{\sigma^2 D^2}{\varepsilon^2} sequentially.

Hence K times fewer iterations, each K times more expensive in computation (parallelized): this is the ideal linear speedup, valid if and only if communication is negligible — which it never is (rest of this chapter). ∎

The communication wall Sending a gradient of 10^9 parameters (4 GB in fp32) costs more than computing it. Ring all-reduce is bandwidth-optimal: each worker only exchanges \frac{2(K-1)}{K} model sizes per step. The rest of this chapter aims to reduce the bytes exchanged.

Theorem 2.3 — Quantization: QSGD (Alistarh et al., 2017) Quantize each coordinate v_i by random rounding to the grid \{0, \pm s^{-1}\|v\|_2, \dots, \pm\|v\|_2\}: the quantizer Q_s is unbiased and \mathbb{E}\|Q_s(v)\|^2 \;\leq\; \Big(1 + \min\Big\{\frac{d}{s^2}, \frac{\sqrt d}{s}\Big\}\Big) \|v\|^2.

Reading: with s = \sqrt d levels, the variance barely doubles, and the (Elias) coding of the quantized vector costs about 2.8\,d + 32 bits in total, i.e. under 3 bits per coordinate instead of 32 — up to an order of magnitude of bandwidth saved at equal training quality.

Extreme case: signSGD (1 bit per coordinate: the sign); combined with majority vote it converges under adapted assumptions (Bernstein et al., 2019). The general trade-off: bits ↔︎ variance (Theorem 2.2: more variance = less speedup).

Proposition 2.4 — Sparsification and error feedback Sending only the k largest coordinates (top-k) drastically reduces communication — but the truncated gradient is biased, and top-k alone can make training diverge. Error feedback: keep the residual, e_{t+1} = \big(g_t + e_t\big) - \mathrm{Top}_k\big(g_t + e_t\big), \qquad \text{one sends } \mathrm{Top}_k(g_t + e_t). Each “forgotten” coordinate is eventually sent once its accumulated residual enters the top-k: convergence is restored at the rate of uncompressed SGD (Seide et al. 2014 for 1-bit; analyses by Stich et al. 2018). In practice: top-k at 1% + error feedback is nearly free in quality.

Proposition 2.5 — Asynchrony and Hogwild! Synchronizing K workers means waiting for the slowest one (the “stragglers”). In asynchronous mode, each worker pushes its gradient computed on a model potentially stale by \tau steps (staleness): the bound of Theorem 0.1 degrades by a factor (1 + \tau).

Hogwild! (Recht et al., 2011): lock-free SGD on shared memory, valid for sparse problems (concurrent updates rarely collide); Lian et al. (2015): asynchronous SGD retains the 1/\sqrt T and the linear speedup up to constant staleness — asynchrony pays off when clock variance dominates.

Theorem 2.6 — Decentralized: gossip and the graph spectrum Without a server: the workers form a communication graph \mathcal{G} (adjacency matrix A, Laplacian \mathcal{L}) and exchange with their neighbors. Gossip aggregation (matrix W doubly stochastic, e.g. W = I - \gamma \mathcal{L}), \theta^{(k)} \leftarrow \sum_j W_{kj}\, \theta^{(j)}, makes all nodes converge to the average at geometric speed: \big\|\theta_t - \bar\theta \,\mathbf{1}\big\| \;\leq\; \rho(W)^t\, \|\theta_0 - \bar\theta\,\mathbf{1}\|, \rho(W) = \max_{i \neq 1} |\lambda_i(W)| = 1 - \gamma\lambda_2(\mathcal{L}) \ \ (\gamma \text{ small enough}), where \lambda_2(\mathcal{L}), the second smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian, is the spectral gap (algebraic connectivity of the graph — see the Graph ML module, spectrum chapter; chain = slow, complete graph = fast, expander = nearly optimal).

Decentralized SGD (D-PSGD, Lian et al., ICML 2017) interleaves local SGD steps and gossip: it retains the \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt T) rate of the centralized version and its linear speedup, with local exchanges instead of the global all-reduce — it would beat the centralized version in wall-clock time as soon as the graph is well connected and bandwidth is limited.

3. Federated learning

When the data belong to hospitals or sit on phones, they cannot be gathered on one server: the model must travel to the data. Each client then performs several local steps between two communications, and because the clients’ data differ, these local steps pull the model in different directions. This chapter proves this client drift on an example, and presents the corrections (FedProx, SCAFFOLD) and the personalized variants.

Algorithm 3.1 — FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) Clients \{1..K\} with local data \mathcal{D}_k that is non-iid (each has its own data, never shared). At each round:

  1. the server broadcasts \theta_r to a subset of clients;
  2. each client k performs E epochs of local SGD: \theta_r^{(k)} \approx \arg\min F_k;
  3. weighted aggregation: \theta_{r+1} = \sum_k \tfrac{n_k}{n}\, \theta_r^{(k)}.

Theorem 3.2 — Client drift: proving it on a quadratic example Heterogeneity makes clients drift: local SGD solves their own problems before aggregation. Exact computation on F_k(\theta) = \tfrac{a_k}{2}(\theta - z_k)^2 (local SGD = exact gradient, step size \eta):

Proof. One local step: \theta \leftarrow (1 - \eta a_k)\theta + \eta a_k z_k, so after E steps starting from \theta_r (geometric sum): \theta_r^{(k)} = z_k + c_k\,(\theta_r - z_k), \qquad c_k = (1 - \eta a_k)^E. The aggregation \theta_{r+1} = \frac1K\sum_k \theta_r^{(k)} is an affine iteration whose fixed point is a weighted average by the effective curvatures 1 - c_k: \theta_\infty = \frac1K\sum_k (1 - c_k)\, z_k \,\Big/\, \frac1K \sum_k (1 - c_k) . Now the global optimum of F = \frac1K\sum F_k is \theta^\star = \sum a_k z_k / \sum a_k. The two coincide only if the a_k are equal: FedAvg converges to a point that is NOT the global optimum as soon as the curvatures differ.

Numbers. Take K=2, a_1 = 1, z_1 = 0, a_2 = 10, z_2 = 1, \eta = 0.05, E = 10.

Then c_1 = 0.95^{10} \approx 0.60 and c_2 = 0.5^{10} \approx 0.001, so \theta^\star = 10/11 \approx 0.909, \qquad \theta_\infty = \frac{1 \cdot 1}{0.40 + 1} \approx 0.713 . This is a structural bias of ~20% that does not vanish as the number of rounds grows. ∎

Theorem 3.3 — Convergence of FedAvg (strongly convex setting, statement) Under (A1)-(A3) per client and bounded heterogeneity, the Theorem-0.1-style analysis applied to FedAvg (Li et al., 2020) yields, with E local steps per round of K clients: \mathbb{E}\big[F(\theta_R)\big] - F^\star \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{D^2}{\eta E R}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu^2 K E R}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\big(\Gamma\big), \qquad \Gamma = F^\star - \sum_k \frac{n_k}{n} F_k^\star \ \text{(or equivalent)}, where the heterogeneity term \Gamma > 0 measures the gap between the global optimum and the local optima: it is the analytic manifestation of Theorem 3.2.

The first two terms decrease with the rounds (communication gain: E steps for 1 exchange); the third is a bias that only a correction (SCAFFOLD) or a penalty (FedProx) can mitigate.

Proposition 3.4 — FedProx: the role of the proximal term FedProx (Li et al., 2020): each client minimizes a regularized objective \theta^{(k)} \approx \arg\min_\theta\ F_k(\theta) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|\theta - \theta_r\|^2, then aggregates. The proximal penalty:

  1. curbs the drift of Theorem 3.2 — the client stays in a neighborhood of \theta_r of radius \propto 1/\mu;
  2. makes E arbitrary (tolerance to slow or partial clients — work with what has been received);
  3. can be read as a proximal step of the global problem: in the limit \mu \to \infty, FedAvg with 1 step.

Trade-off: too large a \mu crushes local work (back to communication-slow FedSGD), too small a \mu lets clients drift.

Algorithm 3.5 — SCAFFOLD: variance correction via control variates (Karimireddy et al., ICML 2020.) The server and the clients maintain control variates c and c_k (initially zero). Local step: \theta_{j+1}^{(k)} = \theta_j^{(k)} - \eta\big(g_k(\theta_j^{(k)}) - c_k + c\big), update of the client control at the end of the round: c_k^{+} = c_k - c + \frac{\theta_r - \theta_E^{(k)}}{\eta E}, \qquad c^{+} = \frac1K \sum_k c_k^{+}.

Reading. The term -c_k + c replaces the local gradient with a “recentered” version: c_k - c estimates the mean difference between the local gradient and the global gradient — exactly the drift of Theorem 3.2. On the quadratic example above, the correction is exact: the drift disappears and the fixed point becomes \theta^\star again (exercise 8).

Result: the number of rounds to reach \varepsilon goes from \mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^2) (FedAvg, with large E) to \mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon) — the same motif as SVRG (Proposition 0.3): a control variate buys a factor of \varepsilon.

Proposition 3.6 — Personalization: Ditto and local fine-tuning When clients want different models (hospitals with distinct practices), heterogeneity becomes a resource: learn a good global model that serves as an initialization for everyone, then personalize. Ditto (Li et al., 2021) solves, per client, \min_{\theta_k}\ F_k(\theta_k) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta_k - \theta^\star_{\mathrm{glob}}\|^2 jointly with the global problem: \lambda arbitrates specialization/collaboration — and the solution improves fairness across clients (the worst client is not sacrificed for the benefit of the average client).

Pragmatic version: local fine-tuning of the federated model (standard in production, e.g. keyboards); probabilistic version: mixtures of posteriors (see ch. 8, FedEM).

4. Byzantine robustness

Averaging the clients’ gradients assumes that every client is honest; a single faulty or malicious client can move the average anywhere. This chapter defines the threat model and studies aggregation rules that resist a bounded number of arbitrary clients — coordinate-wise median, trimmed mean, Krum, geometric median — together with their guarantees and their limits in high dimension.

Definition 4.1 — Byzantine client and threat model A client is Byzantine if it returns an arbitrary gradient (crash or attack: data poisoning, model poisoning, backdoor). One distinguishes:

Failure Behavior Tolerance threshold
Crash stops responding redundancy, timeout (systems)
Byzantine (statistical) arbitrary message, honest iid gradients f < K/2 (geometric rules)
Byzantine (exact agreement) arbitrary message, no statistical assumption f < K/3 (information-theoretic bounds)

The battle is fought on the aggregation rule (GAR): replace the mean with a robust operator — the rest of the pipeline (ch. 2-3) is unchanged.

Attacks, by hand

  • Label flipping: the client trains on flipped labels — its gradient points opposite to the consensus;
  • Large-scale model poisoning: in dimension 1, honest \mu = 0.5, K = 10, f = 1 attacker sending -C with C = \tfrac{K-f}{f}\mu + 1 = 5.5. The mean becomes \mathrm{mean} = \frac{9 \times 0.5 - 5.5}{10} = -0.1 . The sign is reversed by a single client: the mean is uncontrolled as soon as f \geq 1 (norm of the bias arbitrarily large);
  • Backdoor: data with a trigger (“add word X to every sentence containing the secret sequence”); the poisoned model keeps normal accuracy on clean data and fires on the trigger — invisible in the global metric, dangerous in deployment.

Theorem 4.2 — Median and trimmed mean: guarantees in dimension 1 (proved) Let K values of which K - f honest (f < K/2) lying in an interval I = [m, M] with honest mean \bar g. Then:

    1. the coordinate-wise median lies in I, hence |\mathrm{med} - \bar g| \leq M - m;
    1. the \beta-trimmed mean (remove the \beta K largest and the \beta K smallest values, \beta K \geq f, 2\beta K < K) is a mean of values that all lie in the interval, hence lies in I.

Proof. (i) There are more than K/2 honest values: the order statistic of rank \lceil K/2 \rceil is honest or bracketed by two honest values, hence in [m, M]; since \bar g \in [m, M], the gap is at most the diameter M - m.

(ii) Every value kept after trimming is at most the (\beta K + 1)-th largest value. Among the \beta K + 1 largest values, at least one is honest (there are only f \leq \beta K Byzantine values), so this threshold is at most an honest value, hence at most M.

Symmetrically, every kept value is at least m. The kept values, Byzantine or not, all lie in [m, M], and so does their mean. ∎

Scope and limits. The guarantee is bounded where the mean is arbitrary — but it holds coordinate by coordinate: an attacker can stay “within the interval” on each coordinate while correlating its deviations to shift the global direction (dimension-by-dimension attack — the flaw exploited by El Mhamdi et al., see ch. 8).

The price is also statistical: the median and trimming use information less well than the mean (efficiency \sim 2/\pi in the Gaussian case for the median).

Algorithm 4.3 — Krum (Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Stainer, NeurIPS 2017), in detail Parameter: an upper bound f on the number of Byzantine clients, with K \geq 2f + 3.

  1. compute all the distances D(i, j) = \|g_i - g_j\|^2;
  2. for each i, score s_i = sum of the m = K - f - 2 smallest distances D(i, j) (its m nearest neighbors);
  3. return g_{i^\star}, i^\star = \arg\min_i s_i — the point most “surrounded” by close points.

Proof. Intuition for the guarantee. The K - f honest gradients are concentrated around the true gradient \nabla F (i.i.d., variance \sigma^2 per coordinate). Each honest point has at least K - f - 1 \geq m + 1 close honest neighbors, hence a score at most m times a quantile of the inter-honest distances, of the same order as \sigma^2 d.

A Byzantine point, on the other hand, has at most f close accomplices: its score includes distances to honest points, large as soon as it moves away from the cloud — unless it hides inside the honest cloud, in which case it is harmless.

The theorem of the paper formalizes this, under the assumption of (2f+2, f+1)-resilience: any mean of f+1 honest points is close to \nabla F, and 2f + 2 honest points cannot all be simultaneously “close” to the same outlying point. The selected point is then at distance \mathcal{O}(\sigma\sqrt d) from the true gradient — the order of magnitude of the stochastic noise itself: robustness has not destroyed convergence. ∎

Cost and limits. O(K^2 d) on the server (all pairwise distances); requires i.i.d. gradients — in heterogeneous federated learning (Th. 3.2), the honest points form several clusters and Krum may pick a cluster; attacks varying over time: alternating between two attack directions beats any one-shot selection — direct motivations for Bulyan (ch. 8).

Proposition 4.4 — Geometric median and Weiszfeld iterations The geometric median \hat g = \arg\min_x \sum_k \|x - g_k\|_2 is the center minimizing the sum of distances (robust: breakdown point 1/2). Weiszfeld’s algorithm (fixed point of weights inverse to the distances): x^{(j+1)} \;=\; \frac{\sum_k g_k \,/\, \|x^{(j)} - g_k\|}{\sum_k 1 \,/\, \|x^{(j)} - g_k\|}. Each iteration is a weighted barycenter where far-away points carry little weight; convergence is guaranteed (strict convexity as soon as the g_k are not aligned).

Guarantee (RGM, Chen et al., 2017): with f < K/2 and i.i.d. honest gradients of variance \sigma^2, \|\hat g - \nabla F\| = \mathcal{O}\big(\sigma\sqrt{d/K} + \tfrac{f}{K}\big) up to constant factors — the price of robustness is a factor \sqrt K on the stochastic part, plus a term in the fraction of corrupted clients.

The Byzantine curse of dimensionality The typical distance between two honest gradients grows like \sigma\sqrt d: in high dimension, the honest “cloud” is immense, and telling a malicious deviation from noise becomes impossible without an additional assumption.

Impossibility results: no rule can be simultaneously unbiased (efficient at 1/\sqrt K) and resilient to f > 0 for all distributions; minimal robust estimators (trimmed mean, geometric median) incur \Omega(\sigma\sqrt d) while “efficient” rules incur infinity. That is why the Byzantine setting tolerates d = 10^3 (aggregated gradients, cross-silo) better than d = 10^9 (raw LLM gradients).

Proposition 4.5 — Secure aggregation: pairwise masks Dual problem: the server must not even see the individual gradients (privacy, ch. 5). Bonawitz et al. (2017): each pair (k, j) shares a pseudo-random seed, and client k sends g_k + \sum_{j > k} s_{kj} - \sum_{j < k} s_{kj} . When summing over all clients, the masks cancel out exactly (s_{kj} = -s_{jk}) and the server only learns \sum_k g_k. Unexpected departures are handled by sharing the seeds via secret sharing (Shamir): the survivors reconstruct the masks of those who left.

Fundamental tension: secure aggregation masks the individual gradients… which Krum (4.3) needs; robustness and confidentiality compete — compatible schemes (robust aggregation on cryptographically masked gradients, proofs of “proximity” to the consensus) are an active research topic (ch. 8).

5. Differential privacy

A trained model can leak its training data: an attacker may tell whether a given person was in the dataset, or even extract a memorized sentence. Differential privacy turns “the output barely depends on any one individual” into a measurable guarantee. This chapter defines it, proves the composition rule and the Laplace mechanism, and builds DP-SGD, the private version of stochastic gradient descent.

Definition 5.1 — (\varepsilon, \delta)-differential privacy (Dwork) Two datasets D \sim D' are neighbors if they differ in a single individual (addition/removal; some authors: replacement, i.e. Hamming distance 2 — state the convention!). A randomized algorithm \mathcal{A} is (\varepsilon,\delta)-DP if for all D \sim D' and every event S:

Key result — differential privacy \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^\varepsilon\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(D') \in S] + \delta.

Small \varepsilon ⇒ the output barely changes if one individual changes — a computable guarantee, composable, independent of the adversary’s prior knowledge. \delta allows a rare leak (typically \delta \ll 1/n).

Proposition 5.2 — Post-processing If \mathcal{A} is (\varepsilon, \delta)-DP, any function g applied to its output (analysis, fine-tuning, publication) is too — DP survives any a posteriori transformation of the results. (Proof: the likelihood ratio of g(\mathcal{A}(D)) inherits that of \mathcal{A}(D) by change of variables — inequality of measure ratios.)

Theorem 5.3 — Sequential composition (proved, case \delta = 0) Let \mathcal{A}_1 be \varepsilon_1-DP, and let \mathcal{A}_2 — applied to D and to the result of \mathcal{A}_1 — be \varepsilon_2-DP. Then budgets add up: (\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2) \ \text{is} \ (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\text{-DP}.

Proof. For any subset S of the product of the outputs, conditioning on the first output z: \mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2)(D) \in S] = \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D) = dz]\; \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D, z) \in S_z] where S_z = \{y : (z, y) \in S\}. By DP of \mathcal{A}_2, this is \leq e^{\varepsilon_2} \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D) = dz]\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D', z) \in S_z], then by DP of \mathcal{A}_1, \leq e^{\varepsilon_2} e^{\varepsilon_1} \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D') = dz]\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D', z) \in S_z] = e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\, \mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2)(D') \in S]. ∎

Usage. T accesses that are \varepsilon-DP ⇒ (T\varepsilon)-DP: a naive linear budget. Advanced composition (Rényi DP, moments accountant, 5.5) gives \varepsilon \sim \sqrt T — it is what makes DP deep learning practical.

Theorem 5.4 — Laplace mechanism (proved via sensitivity) The \ell_1 sensitivity of a query q is \Delta = \max_{D \sim D'} \|q(D) - q(D')\|_1 . The mechanism M(D) = q(D) + (Z_1, \dots, Z_k), with Z_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon) i.i.d. (density \tfrac{1}{2b}e^{-|z|/b}), is \varepsilon-DP.

Proof. For any output z, the ratio of densities satisfies, by the triangle inequality: \frac{p_M(z \mid D)}{p_M(z \mid D')} = \exp\Big(-\tfrac{\|z - q(D)\|_1 - \|z - q(D')\|_1}{b}\Big) \;\leq\; \exp\Big(\tfrac{\|q(D) - q(D')\|_1}{b}\Big) \;\leq\; e^{\Delta/b} = e^{\varepsilon}, with b = \Delta/\varepsilon. Since the density ratio is uniformly bounded by e^\varepsilon, so is the ratio of the probabilities of any event: \varepsilon-DP. ∎

Added error. \mathbb{E}|Z_i| = b = \Delta/\varepsilon (per coordinate): the noise is proportional to the sensitivity — hence the art of DP: rewriting queries to minimize \Delta.

Gaussian: for Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I), the condition \sigma \geq \Delta_2 \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon gives (\varepsilon, \delta)-DP (proof via a Gaussian tail bound; \ell_2 noise is the right regime for training).

Algorithm 5.5 — DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) and the moments accountant

  1. clip each gradient per example: \tilde g_i = g_i / \max(1, \|g_i\|_2/C) — bounds the sensitivity: changing one individual changes the sum of the clipped gradients by at most \sim 2C;
  2. add noise to the batch: \tilde g = \frac1m\big(\sum_i \tilde g_i + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)\big) ;
  3. take the SGD step with \tilde g.

Moments accountant (statement). For a mechanism M, define the Rényi moment of order \lambda: M_M(\lambda) = \max_{D \sim D'} \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim M(D')} \big[\big(\tfrac{p_M(z \mid D)}{p_M(z \mid D')}\big)^\lambda\big] . Composition adds the moments (T steps: total moment T \cdot M_M(\lambda)), and conversion to DP gives \varepsilon \;=\; \min_\lambda \frac{T\, M_M(\lambda) - \log \delta}{\lambda}, that is, typically \varepsilon = \Theta(\sqrt T) instead of T — a quadratic gain on the budget. It is this accountant (implemented in the Opacus/TensorFlow Privacy libraries) that tracks the budget at every step of training.

Fundamental trade-off: utility \leftrightarrow privacy \leftrightarrow amount of data (the noise, fixed at C, gets averaged over large batches: DP likes large n).

Proposition 5.6 — Local vs central DP Central: a trusted curator adds the noise (the mechanisms above): good utility/privacy ratio. Local: each user randomizes before sending (no trust required — the keyboard model): the variance accumulates per user, and utility drops (randomized response for counts); the shuffle model (anonymization by cryptographic mixing) sits between the two. Rule of thumb: local for extremely sensitive data, central for research, shuffle for real systems.

Membership inference attack — how it works The adversary’s goal: decide whether the example x was in the training set. Protocol (Shokri et al., 2017):

  1. train shadow models on similar data whose member/non-member status is known;
  2. learn a small classifier separating the confidence scores of members vs non-members;
  3. apply it to the confidences of the target model.

The exploited signal: memorization — the model is more confident on its training data; the worst memorization concerns “atypical” data (canaries: Carlini et al., 2019, demonstrate verbatim extraction of inserted secrets).

DP bounds this signal: the influence of an example on any output is capped by clipping + noise; conversely, an attack rate close to chance is an empirical privacy test.

Why LLGA cares LLMs memorize training fragments (extraction attacks); DP training (DP-SGD on fine-tuning) is an active defense — and the M2 module “Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs” extends exactly this chapter. In the federated setting, DP and secure aggregation (4.5) combine: cryptographic masks against the server, DP against a posteriori adversaries. Graph confidentiality (node-DP, edge-DP) protects social networks — see the Graph ML module. Multi-agent systems (see the Reinforcement Learning module, ch. 7) raise the same question: what do interaction logs leak?

6. Collaboration, personalization, fairness

The previous chapters make collaboration possible; this one asks when it is worth it for each participant. It surveys how to personalize a shared model, when local models can be averaged at all, how to value each participant’s data, and how to keep the result fair across participants.

  • Personalization: a global model + local heads (light per-client fine-tuning); Bayesian interpretation (mixture of posteriors); multi-task formalization (Ditto, ch. 3);

  • Model fusion and mode connectivity: two networks trained from the same initialization are connected by a nearly flat path in the loss surface (low barrier along the segment — Frankle et al., 2020).

    Consequence for collaboration: local models can be averaged… provided they stay in the same basin; realigning the neuron permutations between two models is the key to recent fusions (sketch); these are the theoretical foundations of FedAvg (the averaging of chapter 3 only makes sense in this regime);

  • Data markets: valuing a data source by its marginal contribution — the Shapley value \phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(n - |S| - 1)!}{n!}\,\big[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\big], where N is the set of the n sources and v(S) the quality of the model trained on the sources of S (estimated by Monte-Carlo permutations when n is large; worked example below); data buyers/sellers, incentives for quality — game theory meets ML;

  • Vertical FL: clients hold different features about the same individuals (bank × retailer): split learning, exchanges of protected intermediate representations — a complement to the “horizontal” FL of ch. 3;

  • Fairness: performance per subgroup (demographic parity, equalized odds); in the federated setting, fairness cuts across clients; the Computational Optimal Transport module provides tools (barycenters, fairness via OT);

  • Toward multi-agent systems: when “workers” become LLM agents, collaboration becomes orchestration (roles, votes, verification) — the loop is closed with the Reinforcement Learning and Autonomous Agents module, chapter 7; the mechanisms of this module (robust aggregation, budgets, incentives) carry over directly.

Worked example — Shapley values of three data sources Three hospitals A, B, C can contribute data; v(S) is the test accuracy of the model trained on the union of the sources in S, with v(\varnothing) = 0.50 \ (\text{chance}), \qquad v(A) = v(B) = 0.70, \qquad v(C) = 0.55, v(AB) = 0.80, \qquad v(AC) = v(BC) = 0.75, \qquad v(ABC) = 0.82. With n = 3 the formula averages the marginal contribution of a source over the 3! = 6 orders in which the sources can join. For A:

  • it joins first in two orders (contribution 0.70 - 0.50 = 0.20 each);
  • it joins after B alone (0.80 - 0.70 = 0.10);
  • it joins after C alone (0.75 - 0.55 = 0.20);
  • it joins last in two orders (0.82 - 0.75 = 0.07 each).

Hence \phi_A = (0.20 + 0.20 + 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.07 + 0.07)/6 = 0.14 . By symmetry \phi_B = 0.14, and the same computation for C gives \phi_C = (0.05 + 0.05 + 0.02 + 0.05 + 0.02 + 0.05)/6 = 0.04 . The three values add up to 0.32 = v(ABC) - v(\varnothing): the Shapley value distributes exactly the total gain (efficiency).

Reading: C alone brings +0.05, but once A or B is present its data are largely redundant, and its share reflects that. For n sources the exact computation needs 2^n trainings, hence the Monte-Carlo estimate over random orders.

7. Scaling and compute

Training a large model is first a budget decision: for a fixed amount of compute, how large should the model be and on how many tokens should it be trained? This chapter states the empirical scaling laws, derives the compute-optimal allocation, and shows how mixtures of experts decouple the number of parameters from the cost per token.

Theorem 7.1 — Scaling laws (Kaplan 2020, Chinchilla 2022): formulas and reading On large corpora, the validation loss follows a power law in parameters N and tokens D (before the saturation regime): \text{Kaplan: } L(N, D) = \Big[\Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D} + \frac{D_c}{D}\Big]^{\alpha_D}, \text{Chinchilla: } L = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, with for Chinchilla \alpha \approx 0.34, \beta \approx 0.28, and E \approx 1.69 the irreducible loss (entropy of language); A, B, N_c, D_c are calibrated constants. Training compute is billed C \approx 6\,N D FLOPs (2 passes × 3 per parameter/token).

Theorem 7.2 — Compute-optimal: the Chinchilla derivation Minimize L = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta} under the budget 6 N D = C. The Lagrange conditions -\alpha A N^{-\alpha - 1} = 6\lambda D, \qquad -\beta B D^{-\beta - 1} = 6\lambda N give

Key result — compute-optimal allocation \alpha A\, N^{-\alpha} = \beta B\, D^{-\beta} \;\Rightarrow\; N^\star \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} \approx C^{0.45}, \qquad D^\star \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)} \approx C^{0.55}.

That is: N and D grow together, almost like \sqrt C, with a ratio D^\star/N^\star \approx 20 tokens per parameter (empirical value: ~1.4T tokens for 70B parameters).

Counter-intuitive but verified: Chinchilla (70B) beats Gopher (280B) trained on the same compute; the models of the Kaplan generation were undertrained (too many parameters, too few tokens per parameter).

LLGA reading: compute is the scarce resource; power laws justify budgets; “emergent capabilities” are the deviations (thresholds) beyond this smooth law — see the M2 Large Language Models module.

Worked example — sizing a compute-optimal run With the rule D^\star \approx 20\,N^\star, the budget constraint becomes C = 6ND = 120\,N^2, so N^\star = \sqrt{C/120}.

For the Chinchilla budget, C \approx 5.8 \times 10^{23} FLOPs: N^\star = \sqrt{4.8 \times 10^{21}} \approx 7 \times 10^{10} \ \text{parameters}, \qquad D^\star \approx 1.4 \times 10^{12} \ \text{tokens}. This is the 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens. Gopher spent about the same compute on a model four times larger that saw about one token per parameter: 6 \times 2.8 \times 10^{11} \times 3 \times 10^{11} \approx 5 \times 10^{23} .

In hardware terms: a current data-centre GPU delivers of the order of 10^{15} FLOP/s in bf16 at peak, and realistic runs use about 40% of it. So 5.8 \times 10^{23} FLOPs take about 1.5 \times 10^{9} GPU-seconds, i.e. roughly 400,000 GPU-hours.

That is weeks on a cluster of a thousand GPUs, which is why the allocation between N and D is decided before the run, never during it.

Proposition 7.3 — Mixture of Experts: decoupling FLOPs from parameters An MoE replaces each feed-forward layer with E “experts” and a router that activates only the top k (top-1 in Switch Transformer, Fedus et al., 2021) per token: the parameters grow with E (up to a trillion), while the FLOPs per token stay nearly constant — the Chinchilla law recalibrates on this distinction between active and latent parameters. Technical points: capacity factor (load per expert), balancing loss (preventing routing collapse), all-to-all communication (link to ch. 2).

Federating LLM training: where do we stand? The previous chapters combine into a “federated LLM”: cross-silo (ch. 3) LoRA adapters (FedIT, ch. 3), compression of exchanges (QSGD/error feedback, ch. 2), robust aggregation (ch. 4) if some silos are hostile, DP (ch. 5) for sensitive prompts, and compute-optimality (7.2) to decide where to place the parameters. 8.1. El Mahdi El Mhamdi — Byzantine tolerance for distributed ML

The guiding thread: can we learn in a distributed way when some participants lie? — a line of research that founded the field (byzantine-ML) whose chapters 2-4 this course follows.

Work 8.1 — Krum (Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Stainer, NeurIPS 2017) First aggregation rule provably tolerant to Byzantine clients for distributed SGD (Algorithm 4.3): selection of the most surrounded point, guarantee of distance \mathcal{O}(\sigma\sqrt d) to the true gradient under iid, cost O(K^2 d). Published at the moment distributed SGD was becoming the industrial standard — ~4000 citations; it is the reference that all subsequent GARs (geometric median, trimmed, Bulyan) discuss.

Work 8.2 — The Hidden Vulnerability & Bulyan (El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Rouault, ICML 2018) Negative result. Coordinate-wise rules (median, trimmed mean — Th. 4.2) are beaten by a “dimension-by-dimension” attack: the attacker stays within the honest interval on each coordinate while correlating its deviations — the global direction drifts; and any rule that selects a point (Krum!) can be forced to pick a bad point under attacks varying over time.

Constructive answer: Bulyan — iterate Krum to select K - f - 4 candidates, then coordinate-wise trimmed means on the selected candidates: the selection filters out outliers, the trimming neutralizes the remaining corrupted ones; convergence is proven at the price of a residual bias and an increased variance (the 1/\sqrt K becomes 1/\sqrt{K - 4f} up to a constant).

Methodological lesson: impossibility first — characterize what no GAR can do, then build under these bounds.

What next? The program continues: Distributed Momentum for Byzantine-resilient SGD (ICLR 2021) — making the momentum variant robust (the optimizer’s memory accumulation enlarges the attack surface, see Proposition 0.4); and more broadly the safety of distributed learning systems: interactions between robustness (ch. 4), confidentiality (secure aggregation, ch. 5) and verifiability — the “complete security” of a federated system is a multi-objective problem, not an accumulation of patches.

8.2. Aymeric Dieuleveut — stochastic acceleration and the federated setting

Work 8.3 — Optimal averaged SGD (Dieuleveut, Flammarion, Bach, JMLR 2017) Statement (finite-dimensional case). For least-squares regression (quadratic function, noise \sigma^2, dimension d, condition number \kappa), consider SGD with Polyak-Ruppert averaging and its “tail averaging” version, which discards the first iterations: \bar\theta_n = \tfrac{2}{n}\sum_{t > n/2} \theta_t . With step size \eta \propto 1/\sqrt n, it reaches \mathbb{E}\big[F(\bar\theta_n)\big] - F^\star \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{d\,\sigma^2}{n}\Big) \qquad \text{(minimax rate, without knowing } \sigma^2 \text{ or the spectrum)}, where plain SGD with a constant step size plateaus at \sigma/\sqrt n (Theorem 0.1) and would require exact knowledge of the constants to be optimal.

Idea. Averaging decouples bias and variance: the individual iterates remain noisy, but their average estimates the optimum asymptotically without bias (the noise averages out at rate 1/n, the deterministic dynamics converge fast). It is the tool that makes SGD “parameter-free” — behind the practical optimizers of the module and the optimal Nadaraya-Watson-type rates in the nonparametric setting.

Work 8.4 — Federated-EM (Dieuleveut, Fort, Moulines, Robin, NeurIPS 2021) First federated extension of the EM algorithm to models with latent variables (Gaussian mixtures, HMMs): each client computes its local E statistics, the server aggregates and performs the M step.

Result. A version with control variates (the SCAFFOLD/SVRG motif — Propositions 0.3 and 3.5!) corrects heterogeneity and variance: convergence is proven toward a neighborhood of the optimum at rate \mathcal{O}(1/R) in rounds, with radius controlled by heterogeneity and mitigated by the correction — the same theorem as 3.3, transposed to EM steps.

Applications: federated GMMs in healthcare, federated HMMs in telephony; extended by Federated Majorize-Minimization (2025), which unifies these constructions beyond the server parameter.

And also: communication Artemis (Philippenko & Dieuleveut, 2020): tight bounds for bidirectional compression (server ⇄ clients, ch. 2) in heterogeneous federated learning — when the download AND the upload are compressed, memory is needed (error feedback on both sides) to preserve the rate. The guiding thread of this work: making the guarantees of chapter 0 robust to the realities of chapters 2-3 (expensive communication, heterogeneity, statistical variance of clients).

8.3. Reading a research paper critically

The sheet devotes three sessions to reading scientific articles, with an explicit aim: learning how to read them and how to take a critical view of their content. Critical does not mean hostile: it means separating what a paper proves from what it measures and from what it merely suggests, and finding the question it leaves open. This section gives the method used in sessions 10 to 12; the papers are those of 8.1 and 8.2, all of which extend chapters 0 to 4.

Definition 8.5 — Three-pass reading (after Keshav, 2007)

  • Pass 1 (about ten minutes): title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusion; answer the “five Cs” — the category of the paper (new algorithm, lower bound, analysis, benchmark), its context (which earlier papers it builds on), whether its assumptions look correct at first sight, its contributions, and its clarity.
  • Pass 2 (about an hour): read the statements of the theorems, the algorithms, the figures and the experimental tables, but skip the proofs; write down what is unclear and which references would be needed.
  • Pass 3 (several hours, for one paper per group): rebuild the main result — re-derive the key step of the proof or re-run the key experiment — questioning every assumption as you go.

Definition 8.6 — Critique grid For each paper, answer five points in writing.

  1. Claim: the main result in one sentence, with its quantifiers (“for all f such that…”, “with probability…”).
  2. Assumptions: list them, separate those the proof needs from those that are only convenient, and name the one most likely to fail in practice.
  3. Evidence: what is proved, what is only measured, on which benchmark, against which baselines, over how many runs.
  4. Scope: what the result says nothing about.
  5. Next question: one research question in the sense of Definition 1.1 that the paper leaves open.

Worked example — the grid applied to Krum (Work 8.1) Claim: with K workers of which at most f are Byzantine and K \geq 2f + 3, the vector selected by Krum is, in expectation, positively aligned with the true gradient \nabla F, and SGD driven by it reaches a region where the gradient is small.

Assumptions: the honest gradients are i.i.d. and unbiased with variance \sigma^2 per coordinate; the bound f is known in advance; and the honest noise must be small compared with the gradient — a condition of the form c(K, f)\,\sigma\sqrt d < \|\nabla F\|. The most fragile assumption in our setting is the first one: federated clients are not identically distributed (ch. 3), so the guarantee does not transfer to FedAvg as it stands.

Evidence: a proof for the alignment and the convergence, plus experiments with simulated attackers; the experiments do not test heterogeneous clients.

Scope: the condition on \|\nabla F\| fails near any stationary point, so the guarantee only brings the iterates to a region where \|\nabla F\| is of order c(K,f)\,\sigma\sqrt d — large in high dimension (see ch. 4); and the analysis is silent on attackers who stay inside the honest cloud while correlating their deviations over many coordinates, which is precisely the gap that Work 8.2 exploits.

Next question: “does Krum keep a bounded error when honest gradients are non-i.i.d. with heterogeneity \zeta^2?” — the worked example of 1.1 with Krum in place of the median.

How the three sessions run Before each session, every student does passes 1 and 2 on the paper of the day. In class, each group fills the critique grid; one group presents its grid, another acts as the reviewer and must defend or attack each of the five points with a precise line of the paper.

Our suggested sequence (the coordinator chooses the actual papers): session 10, Krum (8.1), using the grid above as the model; session 11, the Hidden Vulnerability and Bulyan (8.2), which answers the “scope” point of Krum; session 12, averaged SGD or Federated-EM (8.3, 8.4) — or the reference paper of the group’s project, on which the group does pass 3.

Solutions — exercises 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 10, 11

1. Independence: \mathrm{Var}(\tfrac1m\sum_i g_i) = \tfrac{1}{m^2} m\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/m . For K workers with i.i.d. batches of size m, the aggregate has variance \sigma^2/(Km) — plug into Theorem 0.1 (replace \sigma by \sigma/\sqrt{Km}).

For a fixed total number of examples consumed, this gives the same error as sequentially — but each step cost a 1/K-th of the time (parallel computation): this is the speedup, valid if communication is free (Theorem 2.2 for the full version).

2. The three ingredients:

    1. convexity: f(\theta^\star) \geq f(\theta_t) + \langle\nabla f(\theta_t), \theta^\star - \theta_t\rangle ;
    1. the decomposition \mathbb{E}\|g\|^2 = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\xi\|^2, then, by smoothness + convexity, \tfrac12\|\nabla f(\theta)\|^2 \leq L(f(\theta) - f^\star) ;
    1. telescoping: \sum_t (\mathbb{E}D_t - \mathbb{E}D_{t+1}) = D_0 - \mathbb{E}D_T \leq D^2 .

If \eta > 1/L: 1 - \eta L < 0, the descent term changes sign and the recursion no longer controls anything — this analysis needs \eta < 1/L, although plain gradient descent on a quadratic only diverges beyond 2/L.

3. For E = 1 we recover exact distributed SGD: \theta_{r+1} = \frac1K\sum_k(\theta_r - \eta g_k(\theta_r)) = \theta_r - \eta \bar g(\theta_r) . For the drift: apply Theorem 3.2 (values: fixed point \approx 0.713 versus optimum \approx 0.909).

At E = 1 the bias vanishes (c_1 = c_2 = 1 - \eta, mean and optimum coincide); it grows with E — plot |\theta_\infty - \theta^\star| as a function of E.

4. The mean: if each of the f attackers sends \bar g_{\mathrm{hon}} + \frac{K}{f}\,\delta, the mean becomes \frac{(K-f)\,\bar g_{\mathrm{hon}} + f\,\bar g_{\mathrm{hon}} + K\delta}{K} = \bar g_{\mathrm{hon}} + \delta . It is shifted by exactly \delta, in any direction and by any amount.

The median: with K - f > K/2 honest values in [m, M], the median lies in [m, M] (Theorem 4.2); the gap to the honest median is at most the diameter of the honest part — bounded independently of the attack.

6. Sensitivity: adding/removing one individual changes q by at most 1, so \Delta = 1; Theorem 5.4 gives \varepsilon-DP for b = 1/\varepsilon.

Typical error: \mathbb{E}|Z| = 1/\varepsilon (one “blurred” individual at \varepsilon = 1; at \varepsilon = 0.1, ten — hence the price of strong DP on small counts).

10. Three mechanisms: apply the nesting of Theorem 5.3 twice (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3). One hundred \varepsilon_0-DP queries: 100\,\varepsilon_0 under simple composition.

Advanced composition gives roughly \varepsilon \approx \sqrt{2 \times 100 \ln(1/\delta)}\,\varepsilon_0 for small \varepsilon_0 (at \delta = 10^{-6}: \approx 53\,\varepsilon_0) — better, but still growing: DP taxes every access, definitively.

11. Exponents: \beta/(\alpha+\beta) = 0.28/0.62 \approx 0.45 for N^\star, 0.34/0.62 \approx 0.55 for D^\star.

With C = 10^{23}: the Lagrange condition gives N \approx 1.7\, D^{0.82}, and 6ND = C solves, as an order of magnitude (parametric fit), to D^\star \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12} \ \text{tokens}, \qquad N^\star \approx 1.5 \times 10^{10} \ \text{parameters}. The ratio D^\star/N^\star comes out between 20 and 80 depending on the fitting method of the paper (IsoFLOP vs parametric) — two figures, one single lesson: at a fixed budget, feed the model with tokens rather than inflating the parameters.
