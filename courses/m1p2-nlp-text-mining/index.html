0. Review: vector spaces and probabilities

This course reads text through two lenses: as vectors (a document is a point, similarity is an angle) and as random sequences (a document is drawn from a distribution, and learning means maximizing a likelihood). The review below recalls the tools of both lenses and points to the chapter where each one is used.

L3 background used

  • Vector spaces: basis, dimension, projection; cosine similarity \cos(u, v) = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} — invariant under dilation (\cos(u,v)=\cos(\lambda u, v) for \lambda > 0, which “cancels out” document lengths); the \ell_2 norm will be used to normalize TF-IDF vectors;
  • Linear algebra: singular value decomposition \mathbf{X} \approx \mathbf{U}_k \Sigma_k \mathbf{V}_k^\top — the engine of LSA (ch. 5) and the direct cousin of PCA (Machine Learning module);
  • Conditional probabilities and Bayes: \mathbb{P}(c \mid d) \propto \mathbb{P}(d \mid c)\,\mathbb{P}(c) — the Naive Bayes classifier (ch. 3) follows directly from it; the Dirichlet distribution \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha), conjugate of the multinomial — the foundation of LDA (ch. 5);
  • Markov chains (Refresher Statistics ch. 2, Reinforcement Learning ch. 0) and dynamic programming (Refresher CS 6.6): forward/backward and Viterbi (ch. 6) are Bellman recursions on a chain; TextRank (ch. 8) is a Markov chain raised to a power;
  • Likelihood, MLE and EM: word2vec (ch. 4), LDA (ch. 5), Naive Bayes (ch. 3) and the Unigram tokenizer (ch. 1) are all likelihood maximizations — by SGD, Gibbs, direct counting or EM;
  • Entropy and perplexity: for a language model, \mathrm{PPL} = \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{t} \log p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\big) — the universal quality measure of a generative model of text (M2 LLMs, ch. 3).

How to read this course Each chapter follows the same triptych:

  1. a representation of text (counts, weights, vectors, distributions, latent topics);
  2. a learning algorithm with its statistical justification;
  3. a quantified evaluation.

The thm/prop boxes contain the proven results; the proofs come at the end of each box. References of the form “module X, chapter N” point to the other courses of the master’s program.

1. The text processing pipeline

Concrete scene: 50,000 support tickets in French and English. A first pipeline lowercases the text, splits it into “words” with a regular expression and feeds TF-IDF features to a logistic regression: macro-F1 0.62. Without NFKC normalization, two encodings of “café” count as two different types; with aggressive stemming, “connection” and “connector” merge and the macro-F1 drops to 0.51. This chapter fixes the pipeline before the model.

What breaks Inconsistent train/prod tokenizer (ghost vocab); normalizing too late (Unicode duplicates); judging a classifier on overall accuracy when 3 classes dominate. All three sabotage an LLM fine-tune as much as a toy BoW.

Definition 1.1 — Standard pipeline Raw text → cleaning (HTML, case, accents, Unicode) → sentence segmentation → tokenization (splitting into units: words, subwords, characters) → lexical normalization (stemming raw root / lemmatization canonical form; stopwords) → representation (ch. 2, 4, 5) → model (ch. 3, 6, 7) → evaluation. Each stage destroys information; the engineer’s craft is deciding which information is expendable for the task at hand.

1.1. Unicode normalization and “clean” text

Definition 1.2 — Unicode normal forms The same glyph admits several encodings: “é” = one codepoint U+00E9 in NFC (canonical composition), but two (e + combining accent U+0301) in NFD. NFKC goes further: ligature “ﬁ” → “fi”, digit “²” → “2”, full-width letters → ASCII. Two strings that are visually identical but distinct in bytes break counting, deduplication and search; so we normalize before anything else (in practice NFKC, or NFC if typography and units must be preserved).

  • Case: lower() merges “Polytechnique” and “polytechnique” but also the acronym and the common word; in French, keep the accents in lowercase (“élève” ≠ “eleve”);
  • Sentence segmentation: rules (punctuation + capital letter) or learning (Punkt, Kiss & Strunk 2006); real pitfalls: “M.”, “etc.”, German abbreviations (“z. B.”), period before a closing quote. Bad segmentation wrecks ch. 8 (extractive summarization: the sentence is the unit being selected);
  • Stemming vs lemmatization: the stemmer (Porter, Snowball) cuts off suffixes by rules — fast, crude, suited to information retrieval; the lemmatizer uses a dictionary and morphology (“sont” → “être”) — slower, better for fine-grained analysis. In industry: stemmer for search, lemmatizer for analysis.

1.2. Subword tokenization: BPE, WordPiece, Unigram, SentencePiece

Why subwords Splitting on spaces works poorly: German compounds (“Donaudampfschifffahrt”), French elisions (“l’arbre”: 1 or 2 tokens?), some Asian languages write without spaces, and any closed vocabulary runs into unknown words (typos, neologisms, product references). Subword tokenizers learn, on the corpus, a fixed-size vocabulary (~32k-128k) whose concatenation reconstructs any string: frequent words = 1 token, rare words = a sequence of pieces.

Algorithm 1.3 — BPE (Byte Pair Encoding, Sennrich et al. 2016) Initial vocabulary: characters (or bytes). Iteratively: (1) count all pairs of adjacent symbols in the corpus (weighted by word frequencies); (2) merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; (3) repeat until the vocabulary \mathcal{V} reaches the target size |\mathcal{V}|. The result is an ordered set of merge rules; tokenizing a new text = applying the merges in the learned order.

Example 1.4 — Step-by-step BPE training Mini-corpus (words, frequencies): base×5, bases×4, basse×3, basique×2. Initial alphabet: b, a, s, e, i, q, u. We write each word as a sequence of symbols and count adjacent pairs:

Step Most frequent pairs Chosen merge
1 (b,a)=14, (a,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, (s,i)=(i,q)=(q,u)=(u,e)=2 ba (14; tie with (a,s), arbitrary choice)
2 (ba,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, … bas (14)
3 (bas,e)=9, (e,s)=4, (bas,s)=3, (s,e)=3, … base (9)
4 (base,s)=4, (bas,s)=3, (s,e)=3, … bases (4)
5 (bas,s)=3, (s,e)=3, … bass (3)
6 (bass,e)=3, … basse (3)

After these six merges, base, bases and basse are whole tokens, while basique is still bas+i+q+u+e (four more merges would make it whole); an unexpected word like “basiste” breaks down into a known prefix (bas, or basi once more merges are learned) followed by single characters: never an unknown token, at worst a longer sequence. This is exactly the mechanism that equips GPT (byte-level BPE since GPT-2, 50k tokens; about 100k for GPT-4) and Llama 3 (byte-level BPE, 128k tokens) — developed in M2 LLMs, ch. 1.

Variants 1.5 — WordPiece (BERT), Unigram (T5), SentencePiece

  • WordPiece: the same merge loop, but a pair’s score is \frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)\,f(y)} — we merge the pair whose relative frequency increases the most, a proxy for the likelihood gain of a unigram model (justification: merging x,y replaces two symbols with one; the gain in bits is all the greater as the collocation is over-represented relative to independence);

  • Unigram (Kudo, 2018): a generative model — a candidate vocabulary (characters + frequent substrings), each segment s has a probability p(s), a word is segmented by Viterbi by maximizing \sum_j \log p(s_j).

    Training by EM: E — for each word, the posterior distribution over its segmentations (∝ product of the p(s_j), computable by a forward dynamic program); M — p(s) = normalized expected counts; then pruning of the segments that degrade the likelihood the least, until the target size |\mathcal{V}|.

    Unlike BPE, the segmentation is probabilistic and admits several analyses (subword regularization);

  • SentencePiece: a language-independent layer that treats the sentence as a sequence of bytes, the space becoming a “▁” symbol; no pre-tokenization on spaces — essential for languages without spaces and for multilingual settings. It is the tokenizer of T5 (a Unigram model) and of Llama 1 and 2 (a 32k BPE model); Llama 3 moved to the 128k byte-level BPE of Example 1.4.

import re
from collections import Counter

# --- Toy BPE: learning merges on the mini-corpus of example 1.4 ---
words = {"b a s e": 5, "b a s e s": 4, "b a s s e": 3, "b a s i q u e": 2}
merges = []
for _ in range(8):
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, f in words.items():
        sym = w.split()
        for pair in zip(sym, sym[1:]):
            pairs[pair] += f
    if not pairs:
        break
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)          # most frequent adjacent pair
    merges.append(best)
    big = " ".join(best)
    words = {re.sub(r"(?<!\S)" + re.escape(big) + r"(?!\S)", "".join(best), w): f
             for w, f in words.items()}
print("merges:", merges[:6])
# tokenize a new word = replay the merges in the learned order
def encode(word, merges):
    sym = list(word)
    for a, b in merges:
        i, out = 0, []
        while i < len(sym):
            if i + 1 < len(sym) and (sym[i], sym[i+1]) == (a, b):
                out.append(a + b); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        sym = out
    return sym
print(encode("basiste", merges))

1.3. Stopwords, n-grams, collocations and n-gram language models

  • Stopwords: to be removed for classical text mining (they saturate the BoW); to be kept for transformers — negation (“ne…pas”) and syntax matter. The decision must be justified by the task, not by habit;
  • N-grams: sequences of n tokens; “New York” (bigram) ≠ “New” + “York”; in classification, moving from unigrams to bigrams captures compositional polarity (“pas cher”); cost: the vocabulary explodes (potentially |\mathcal{V}|^n) — hence ngram_range=(1,2) and min_df in the code of ch. 3;
  • Collocations: bigrams whose frequency exceeds what independence predicts, measured by mutual information \mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)} — a criterion reused as-is in ch. 2 to justify IDF, and in ch. 5 for topic coherence.

Counting n-grams also gives the first language model: a probability for the next word given the previous ones. It is the ancestor of the LLMs of M2, it is still the language model of classical speech recognizers (Speech Technology, ch. 1), and its central difficulty — how much probability to leave for what was never seen — never goes away.

Model 1.6 — Bigram language model with interpolated Kneser–Ney smoothing A bigram model factorizes a sentence as p(w_{1:N})=\prod_{t} p(w_t\mid w_{t-1}). The maximum-likelihood estimate c(v,w)/c(v) gives probability zero to every bigram absent from the training corpus, so a single unseen bigram makes a whole test sentence impossible.

Interpolated Kneser–Ney (Kneser & Ney, 1995; Chen & Goodman, 1999) subtracts a fixed discount D \in (0, 1], typically 0.75, from every seen count and gives the freed mass to a continuation distribution:

Key result — interpolated Kneser–Ney bigram p_{\mathrm{KN}}(w\mid v)=\frac{\max\big(c(v,w)-D,\,0\big)}{c(v)}+\lambda(v)\,p_{\mathrm{cont}}(w), \qquad \lambda(v)=\frac{D\,N_{1+}(v\,\bullet)}{c(v)}, \qquad p_{\mathrm{cont}}(w)=\frac{N_{1+}(\bullet\,w)}{N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet)}

Here N_{1+}(v\,\bullet) is the number of distinct words seen after v, N_{1+}(\bullet\,w) the number of distinct words seen before w, and N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet) the number of distinct bigram types.

Reading: the continuation probability asks “in how many contexts does w appear?”, not “how often?”. “Francisco” is frequent but appears almost only after “San”, so it receives little continuation mass and is not predicted after unrelated words.

Proof. The model is a distribution. Let c(v) count the occurrences of v followed by a word. Every seen bigram has c(v,w)\ge 1\ge D, so \sum_w \frac{\max\big(c(v,w)-D,\,0\big)}{c(v)}=\frac{c(v)-D\,N_{1+}(v\,\bullet)}{c(v)}=1-\lambda(v), and the second term adds \lambda(v)\sum_w p_{\mathrm{cont}}(w)=\lambda(v). The total is 1. ∎

Example — counts from a training corpus:

  • “the” is followed by a word 10 times, by 4 distinct words, 3 times by “cat” and never by “dog”;
  • the corpus has 200 bigram types; “cat” follows 5 distinct words and “dog” follows 20;
  • with D=0.75, the back-off weight is \lambda(\text{the})=0.75\cdot 4/10=0.3.

Then p_{\mathrm{KN}}(\text{cat}\mid\text{the})=\frac{2.25}{10}+0.3\cdot\frac{5}{200}=0.2325, \qquad p_{\mathrm{KN}}(\text{dog}\mid\text{the})=0+0.3\cdot\frac{20}{200}=0.03 .

Definition 1.7 — Perplexity On a held-out text of N tokens, \mathrm{PPL}=\exp\Big(-\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p(w_t\mid w_{<t})\Big), the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood (the cross-entropy, in nats).

It reads as an effective branching factor: a model that is uniform over \mathcal{V} has perplexity |\mathcal{V}|, and a model with perplexity 50 is as uncertain, on average, as a uniform choice among 50 words. Lower is better. Two perplexities are comparable only with the same tokenizer and the same test text: a subword model and a word model do not count the same N.

Example: four test tokens predicted with probabilities 0.25, 0.5, 0.1 and 0.2 have a mean log-probability of -1.498, so \mathrm{PPL}=e^{1.498}\approx 4.47 .

2. Bag of words, TF-IDF and BM25

Most models cannot read strings: they need vectors. This chapter turns each document into a vector of word counts, reweights the counts so that informative words dominate (TF-IDF), and derives BM25, the scoring function that still powers search engines.

Definition 2.1 — Bag of words (BoW) A document = a vector of counts over the vocabulary \mathcal{V} (order lost): d \in \mathbb{N}^{|\mathcal{V}|}. Documents × terms matrix \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{N}^{n \times |\mathcal{V}|} — extremely sparse (a few hundred non-zeros per document across tens of thousands of columns; sparse representation, cf. Refresher CS).

Definition 2.2 — TF-IDF

Key result — TF-IDF weight \mathrm{tfidf}(t, d) = \mathrm{tf}(t, d) \times \underbrace{\log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)}}_{\mathrm{idf}(t)},

\mathrm{tf} = frequency of the term in the document, \mathrm{df} = number of documents containing the term, n = number of documents. It penalizes omnipresent terms and rewards discriminative ones.

L2 weighting: we post-normalize \tilde d = d / \|d\|_2, so that the dot product is directly the cosine similarity — this is what TfidfVectorizer does by default.

Proposition 2.3 — IDF = pointwise mutual information Let us choose a document D uniformly among the n, and let T denote the event “D contains the term t”.

Then for any document d containing t: \mathrm{PMI}(T\,;D=d) \;=\; \log\frac{\mathbb{P}(T,\,D=d)}{\mathbb{P}(T)\,\mathbb{P}(D=d)} \;=\; \log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)} \;=\; \mathrm{idf}(t).

In other words: IDF is exactly the pointwise mutual information between “the event t occurs” and the choice of this particular document. A term present everywhere identifies no document (zero PMI); a rare term strongly identifies the documents that contain it.

Proof. \mathbb{P}(D=d)=1/n and \mathbb{P}(T)=\mathrm{df}(t)/n, so \mathbb{P}(T,D=d)=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]. Substituting: \mathrm{PMI} = \log\frac{\mathbf{1}[t\in d]/n}{(\mathrm{df}/n)(1/n)} = \log\frac{n\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]}{\mathrm{df}}, which equals \mathrm{idf}(t) if t \in d (and -\infty otherwise). ∎

Common variants (all useful in practice)

  • Sublinear TF: 1+\log \mathrm{tf} — “3 occurrences do not signal the topic 3× more than a single one”;
  • Boolean TF: \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{tf}>0] — robust to highly repetitive documents (spam, bots);
  • Smoothed IDF: \log\frac{1+n}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1 (the scikit-learn variant) — avoids blow-ups for \mathrm{df}=n;
  • Composite schemes (SMART nomenclature): different weighting on the document side (e.g. L2 of log-tf × idf) and on the query side (e.g. plain cosine); the performance of a search engine is often tuned here, before any model.

Model 2.4 — BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza) and its probabilistic reading A direct heir of probabilistic information retrieval (the Robertson–Sparck Jones 2-Poisson framework): a document is a mixture of “informative / non-informative” for the query.

The number of occurrences x of the term follows a Poisson law of intensity \mu_1 in the documents that are really about the term (“elite” documents) and of intensity \mu_0 \ll \mu_1 in the others, and relevance is only correlated with eliteness.

The resulting weight of x is the log-odds of relevance under this mixture: it is 0 at x = 0, increasing, and saturating as x grows (for a single pair of Poisson laws, the log-likelihood ratio \;x\log\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_0}-(\mu_1-\mu_0)\; would grow linearly; it is the mixture that bounds it).

BM25 approximates this shape:

Key result — the BM25 score \mathrm{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)\cdot \frac{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)+k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)},\qquad \mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)=\log\frac{n-\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5}{\mathrm{df}(t)+0.5},

where |d| is the length of d in tokens, \mathrm{avgdl} the average length over the corpus, and k_1 \in [1.2, 2], b = 0.75 the usual defaults of the two tuning parameters.

Proof. Two limits to know by heart.

  • Saturation: as \mathrm{tf}\to\infty, the factor tends to k_1+1 (bounded); at \mathrm{tf}=0 it equals 0.
  • Half-saturation: it reaches half of its maximum when \mathrm{tf}=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl}) — k_1 tunes the saturation speed, b the length penalty (b=0: no normalization; b=1: proportional).

These are exactly the two flaws of raw TF-IDF that BM25 corrects: unbounded linear growth on the tf side, and no handling of document length. ∎

Proposition 2.5 — Hashing trick for giant vocabularies For huge or a priori unbounded |\mathcal{V}| (tweet streams, logs): hash each term h(t) \in \{1,\dots,D\} with a random sign s(t)\in\{\pm1\}, and set \tilde x_i = \sum_{t:\,h(t)=i} s(t)\,x_t. Then \mathbb{E}\,\langle \tilde x,\tilde y\rangle = \langle x,y\rangle — collisions cancel out in expectation.

Proof. We have \tilde x^\top\tilde y = \sum_{t,t'} s(t)s(t')\,x_t y_{t'}\,\mathbf{1}[h(t)=h(t')] and \mathbb{E}[s(t)s(t')]=\mathbf{1}[t=t'] (independent signs); what remains is \sum_t x_t y_t.

The standard deviation of the collision term decreases like 1/\sqrt{D}: with D\sim 2^{20}, the approximation is excellent for classification. ∎

Geometry of documents Each document becomes a point in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}; we measure similarities (cosine), classify (Naive Bayes, SVM), cluster (k-means). The limitation of BoW: bag = no order and no semantics; “the cat eats the mouse” ≈ “the mouse eats the cat”.

Embeddings (ch. 4) and transformers (ch. 7) answer this. BM25 remains the baseline of any retrieval system — including RAG (M2 LLMs, ch. 7, where it serves as a reference point for dense retrievers).

2.1. Search engines: inverted index and ranking evaluation

Information retrieval, named first among the applications of the official description, needs two more ingredients than a scoring function: a data structure that avoids scoring every document, and a way to measure whether the ranking is good.

Definition 2.6 — Inverted index For each term t, the index stores a posting list of pairs (\text{doc id}, \mathrm{tf}(t, d)) sorted by document id, together with \mathrm{df}(t) and the document lengths |d|.

A query q reads only the posting lists of its own terms, at cost \sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{df}(t) instead of n\,|q|; scores (TF-IDF, BM25) are accumulated per document and the top k kept in a heap.

Because the BM25 term factor is bounded by k_1 + 1 (Model 2.4), each term has an upper bound on its contribution, and dynamic pruning (MaxScore, WAND) skips documents that cannot enter the current top k.

Posting lists store the gaps between consecutive ids with variable-length codes; by Zipf’s law most terms are rare, so most lists are short.

Definition 2.7 — Ranking evaluation For a query with R relevant documents and a ranked list:

  • Precision@k = \#\{\text{relevant in the top } k\}/k;
  • Recall@k = \#\{\text{relevant in the top } k\}/R;
  • average precision \mathrm{AP} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{k\,:\,\text{doc at rank } k \text{ relevant}} \mathrm{P@}k, and MAP its mean over queries;
  • reciprocal rank 1/\text{rank of the first relevant document} (MRR over queries).

With graded relevance \mathrm{rel}_i: \mathrm{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\mathrm{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \qquad \mathrm{nDCG@}k = \frac{\mathrm{DCG@}k}{\mathrm{IDCG@}k} \in [0, 1], where IDCG is the DCG of the ideal ordering. These are also the metrics of the retrievers of RAG systems (M2 LLMs, ch. 7) and of the recommenders of section 8.5.

Example 2.8 — Evaluating one ranked list A collection has R = 3 relevant documents for a query, and the engine returns five documents judged (relevant, not, relevant, not, relevant).

  • Precision: \mathrm{P@}1 = 1, \mathrm{P@}3 = 2/3, \mathrm{P@}5 = 3/5.
  • Average precision: \mathrm{AP} = \frac13\big(1 + \frac23 + \frac35\big) \approx 0.756 ; the reciprocal rank is 1.
  • With graded judgments (3, 0, 2, 0, 1): \mathrm{DCG@}5 = \frac{7}{1} + \frac{3}{\log_2 4} + \frac{1}{\log_2 6} \approx 7 + 1.5 + 0.387 = 8.887 ;
  • the ideal order (3, 2, 1, 0, 0) gives \mathrm{IDCG@}5 = 7 + \frac{3}{\log_2 3} + \frac{1}{2} \approx 9.393 .

So \mathrm{nDCG@}5 \approx 0.946.

Swapping the documents at ranks 2 and 3 gives (3, 2, 0, 0, 1) and \mathrm{nDCG@}5 \approx 9.280/9.393 \approx 0.988, the best single swap but not yet the ideal list: the grade-1 document at rank 5 must also move up to rank 3, which takes a second swap.

3. Text classification

Deciding which category a document belongs to — spam or not, which team should handle a ticket, positive or negative review — is the most common NLP task in industry. This chapter builds the two reference classifiers on the bag-of-words vectors of ch. 2, Naive Bayes and regularized logistic regression, together with the evaluation practice that goes with them.

Theorem 3.1 — Multinomial Naive Bayes, derived With the (false!) assumption of word independence given the class, \mathbb{P}(d\mid c)=\prod_{t\in d}\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)^{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)}, and Bayes: \hat c = \arg\max_c \Big[\log \mathbb{P}(c) + \sum_{t\in d}\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\log \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)\Big].

MLE of the parameters: \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}}{n_c}, where n_{t,c} is the number of occurrences of t in the documents of class c and n_c = \sum_t n_{t,c}, and \mathbb{P}(c)=\frac{N_c}{N} (N_c documents of class c among N).

Laplace smoothing: \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|\mathcal{V}|}.

Proof.

  1. The MLE comes from maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood under the constraint \sum_t \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=1 (Lagrange multipliers — relative counts).
  2. Problem: a word seen at test time but never in class c gives \log 0 = -\infty — a single rare word wipes out the whole class, and on small corpora this happens constantly.
  3. Laplace smoothing is the posterior mean under a uniform Dirichlet(1,\dots,1) prior: the posterior density \propto \prod_t \pi_t^{n_{t,c}}\cdot\prod_t\pi_t^{1-1} is that of a Dirichlet(n_{1,c}+1, \dots, n_{|\mathcal{V}|,c}+1), whose mean is \frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|\mathcal{V}|} (equivalently, it is the MAP under a Dirichlet(2,\dots,2) prior) — the exact Bayesian link of ch. 0. ∎

Why NB works despite an absurd assumption Conditional independence is massively violated (“New” predicts “York”). But the decision (argmax) is often correct even when the probabilities are badly calibrated: it is enough for the right candidate to win, not for its score to be right (the theory of Domingos & Pazzani). Hence NB’s status: bad probabilities, good baseline — always the first one in a text project, because it trains in seconds and sets the performance floor.

Sparse logistic regression: why it works in high dimension On TF-IDF, \#\text{features} \gg \#\text{docs} (10⁵ features for 10³ documents): the risk of overfitting seems maximal, yet the regularized linear model (L2, or L1 to select) generalizes very well. Three reasons:

  1. in high dimension the vectors are almost orthogonal — examples are naturally separable, and regularization suffices to pick a “sober” separator;
  2. IDF weighting has already done a large share of the statistical work (prop. 2.3);
  3. discriminative unigrams/bigrams are exactly the features a linear model weights well.

State of the art of the “classical” era until 2018 (Joulin et al., fastText); still formidable below 1,000 labeled examples.

  • Calibration: when the probability is consumed (business thresholds, ticket routing), recalibrate — Platt (logistic regression on the scores) or isotonic; diagnostic: reliability curve (predicted vs observed reliability);
  • Imbalanced classes: class weighting (class_weight="balanced"), optimal threshold on the PR curve (not ROC); metric: macro-F1 (per-class average — every class counts equally) rather than accuracy (which flatters the majority); per-intent confusion matrix for chatbots;
  • Networks: sentence CNNs (Kim, 2014) and BiLSTMs over the word embeddings of ch. 4; then transformer fine-tuning (ch. 7). Empirical learning curves: linear+TF-IDF wins below ~10²-10³ examples, the transformer above;
  • Zero-shot / few-shot with an LLM: zero labeled examples, or k examples in the prompt; the mechanism is stated in the box of section 7.2 and studied in depth in M2 LLMs (ch. 4.0, prompting and in-context learning). An excellent exploratory starting point and a help for labeling (active learning), rarely the final solution in production (cost, latency, confidentiality).

4. Word embeddings

In a bag of words, “car” and “automobile” are as different as “car” and “banana”: every word is its own orthogonal axis. Word embeddings replace these axes by dense vectors of a few hundred dimensions, learned from the contexts in which words appear, so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013): distributional hypothesis “A word is characterized by the company it keeps” (Firth, 1957). Each word has two vectors: a center vector v_w, used when w sits at the current position, and a context vector u_c, used when it appears in the window of another word.

Skip-gram: for each position t with center word w_t, maximize the log-likelihood of the context words c within a window, \sum_{t}\sum_{c\in \mathrm{window}(t)} \log p(c\mid w_t), \qquad p(c\mid w)=\frac{\exp(u_c^\top v_w)}{\sum_{c'\in \mathcal{V}}\exp(u_{c'}^\top v_w)} — a softmax over the |\mathcal{V}| words of the vocabulary at each position: \mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|) per example, prohibitive. CBOW: symmetric (predict the word from the average of its context).

Theorem 4.1 — Negative sampling: the derived loss For one observed pair (center w, context c), we draw k “negatives” n_1,\dots,n_k \sim p_n,\qquad p_n(x)\propto \mathrm{freq}(x)^{3/4} (the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4) and replace the softmax with k+1 binary classifications “is this (center, word) pair a true context pair?”. The loss, to be minimized, is \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\log\sigma(u_c^\top v_w) - \sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\sigma(-u_{n_i}^\top v_w), at a cost of \mathcal{O}(k) instead of \mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|). Averaged over the draws, the sum over negatives is k\,\mathbb{E}_{n\sim p_n}\big[\log\sigma(-u_n^\top v_w)\big].

The gradient in v_w: \nabla_{v_w}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\big(1-\sigma(u_c^\top v_w)\big)\,u_c \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(u_{n_i}^\top v_w)\,u_{n_i}.

Reading: a descent step moves v_w toward the context vector u_c, with a force 1-\sigma that vanishes once the pair is well predicted, and away from each negative, with a force equal to its current score \sigma — a negative that the model wrongly scores high (often a semantically close word!) receives the largest correction.

After training, the center vectors are kept as the word embeddings. The rest of the chapter writes z_w for the embedding of w whatever the method (here z_w = v_w).

Theoretical interpretation: negative sampling is a form of NCE (noise contrastive estimation, Gutmann & Hyvärinen 2010; cf. Goldberg & Levy 2014).

Two settings that matter (and why)

  • Frequency subsampling: very frequent words (“the”, “of”) drown the signal; they are discarded with probability p_{\mathrm{discard}}(w)=1-\sqrt{t/f(w)} (threshold t\sim10^{-5} to 10^{-3}) — the signal per pair stays intact and the computation is sped up;
  • The 3/4 exponent: flattening the frequencies boosts rare negatives, which are the most informative ones (frequent negatives are trivial to repel). The same exponent reappears in the weighting function of GloVe below: an empirical compromise between signal and coverage.

GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014): least squares on cooccurrences Let X_{ij} be the number of cooccurrences (words i,j) in the corpus. GloVe factorizes \log X_{ij} with a weighted bilinear model: J=\sum_{i,j} f\!\left(X_{ij}\right)\big(z_i^\top \tilde z_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2,\qquad f(x)=\min\!\Big[\big(x/x_{\max}\big)^{3/4},\,1\Big], where f cuts the weight of rare cooccurrences (noisy, possibly zero; one cannot take \log 0) and saturates for frequent ones. The biases b_i,\tilde b_j absorb the marginal frequencies.

Deep connection: skip-gram with negative sampling is an implicit factorization of a shifted PMI matrix (Levy & Goldberg, NeurIPS 2014). When the dot products are unconstrained, the optimum of the expected loss of Theorem 4.1 is u_c^\top v_w=\mathrm{PMI}(w,c)-\log k, where the PMI uses the noise distribution p_n(c) in place of the context frequency P(c). GloVe does it explicitly, and globally (the whole matrix at once) rather than locally (sampled windows).

fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017): subwords A word = the sum of the vectors of its character n-grams (3 to 6): z_w = \sum_{g\in G_w} z_g. Consequences: an out-of-vocabulary word still receives a vector (the sum of its n-grams), and typos and rich morphology (German: compounds; French: conjugations) are absorbed. The same team shows that “fastText + bigrams” is a formidable classification baseline (cf. ch. 3).

4.1. Evaluating embeddings

  • Lexical similarity: rate word pairs (SimLex-999, WordSim-353), correlate with the cosine — metric: Spearman correlation;
  • Analogies: precision@1 on z_b - z_a + z_c \approx z_d excluding \{a,b,c\} from the search (the Mikolov protocol): “king − man + woman ≈ queen”. Caution: analogy accuracy is sensitive to word frequency;
  • Downstream: the real evaluation is the final task (ch. 3).

Proposition 4.2 — SIF sentence embeddings (Arora et al., TACL 2017) Sentence = weighted average with a(w)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(w)}, where p(w) is the relative frequency of w in the corpus (an anti-frequency weight; \alpha\sim10^{-3}), then removal of the first principal component of the corpus: \bar v(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{w\in S}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(w)}\,z_w,\qquad v(S) = \bar v(S) - u_1 u_1^\top \bar v(S), with |S| the number of words of S and u_1 the first singular vector of the matrix whose rows are the \bar v(S) of all the sentences of the corpus.

Proof. Why remove u_1: in the generative model of Arora et al., each sentence is emitted around a “discourse vector” c_S, but a common vector c_0 (syntax, function words) biases all the sentences of the corpus — that is exactly the direction of greatest variance, hence u_1.

The orthogonal projection removes this shared nuisance term; on Semantic Textual Similarity (STS), this single removal gains several correlation points and makes this baseline “simple but hard to beat” without a trained encoder. It is the direct ancestor of SBERT embeddings (ch. 7). ∎

Bias in embeddings — the WEAT test (Caliskan et al., 2017) Two sets of target words X,Y (e.g. professions) and two attributes A,B (e.g. male/female first names). Association score s(w)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\cos(z_w,z_a)-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}\cos(z_w,z_b); test statistic t = \frac{\mathrm{mean}_{x\in X}\,s(x)-\mathrm{mean}_{y\in Y}\,s(y)} {\mathrm{sd}_{w\in X\cup Y}\,s(w)}, significance by permutations of the targets.

On GloVe/word2vec: scientific professions associate with the male attribute, artistic ones with the female — the corpus contains these stereotypes, and the geometry encodes them.

Mitigation (Bolukbasi et al., 2016): estimate the bias direction (PCA of the “he/she” pairs…), neutralize neutral words (remove their projection onto this direction) and equalize the pairs — we will see the “representation → bias → correction” pattern again in M2 (LLM safety, ch. 9 of the LLMs module).

Text ↔︎ graphs, the other LLGA bridge word2vec on random walks yields DeepWalk/node2vec (Graph ML module, ch. 3); conversely, a Graph of Words (nodes = words, edges = cooccurrence within a window) enables text mining with graph tools — this is a research axis of the course’s professor, detailed in ch. 9: the words ↔︎ graphs loop is a signature of the master’s program.

5. Topic models

A corpus of thousands of documents is too large to read, but it is usually about a few dozen themes. Topic models recover these themes without any labels: each topic is a probability distribution over words, and each document a mixture of topics.

Model 5.1 — LDA (Blei, Ng & Jordan, 2003): the full generative model For each document d and each position i of the corpus: \theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}}),\quad z_{d,i}\mid\theta_d \sim \theta_d,\quad \varphi_k\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}}),\quad w_{d,i}\mid z_{d,i},\varphi \sim \varphi_{z_{d,i}}. A document is a mixture of K topics; each word is drawn from the topic of its position. The hyperparameters carry the subscript LDA because \alpha_t and \beta_t denote the forward and backward variables of the HMM in ch. 6.

The likelihood p(\mathbf{w}) sums over all assignments z — intractable (K^{\#\mathrm{words}} terms): hence the variational Bayes inference (original paper) or Gibbs sampling (practical usage).

Connected reminders: EM from the ML module (variational inference is its Bayesian version); Markov chains (Refresher Statistics ch. 2, Reinforcement Learning ch. 0 — the sampler is a chain whose stationary law is the posterior; geometric convergence under conditions).

Theorem 5.2 — Collapsed Gibbs sampling: the update equation By integrating out \theta,\varphi (Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy), the law of an assignment z_i given everything else depends only on counts:

Key result — collapsed Gibbs update for LDA \mathbb{P}(z_i = k \mid z_{-i},\, w) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{k,w_i}^{-i}+\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}}}{n_k^{-i}+|\mathcal{V}|\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}}}}_{\text{does topic \(k\) like word \(w_i\)?}}\;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{d,k}^{-i}+\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}}}{n_d^{-i}+K\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}}}}_{\text{does document \(d\) like topic \(k\)?}}

where n_{k,w} = number of times topic k has emitted w, n_{d,k} = topic k in d.

The exponent -i means “without the current position”.

Each z_i is sampled sequentially, over thousands of passes; the topics emerge by reinforcement (the rich-get-richer property of Dirichlets).

Proof. By conjugacy, the marginal of the corpus factorizes into multivariate beta functions: p(w,z\mid\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}},\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}})=\prod_k \frac{B(n_{k,\cdot}+\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}})}{B(\beta_{\mathrm{LDA}})} \prod_d \frac{B(n_{d,\cdot}+\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}})}{B(\alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}})}.

The conditional \mathbb{P}(z_i=k\mid\cdot) is the ratio p(w,z_i=k,z_{-i})/p(z_{-i},w_{-i}): all the factors not touching position i cancel out, by a property of beta ratios: \frac{B(u+e_k)}{B(u)}=\frac{u_k}{\sum_j u_j} . This leaves the product of the two predictive factors above. ∎

Choosing the hyperparameters and K Small \alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}} = pure documents (few topics per document); small \beta_{\mathrm{LDA}} = pure topics (distinctive words). The asymmetric version (a non-uniform vector \alpha_{\mathrm{LDA}}) lets a “noise” topic emerge. K is chosen by validation (held-out perplexity) or by coherence — see below; the two criteria do not rank models in the same order, an important result of text mining.

Evaluation 5.3 — Perplexity vs coherence Perplexity (held-out): \mathrm{PPL}=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum \log p(w_{\mathrm{test}})\big) with p(w\mid d)=\sum_k \theta_{d,k}\varphi_{k,w} — but it correlates poorly with human judgment (Chang et al., 2009: models with lower perplexity may produce less interpretable topics).

NPMI coherence: for the M most probable words of each topic, average \mathrm{NPMI}(u,v)=\frac{\log\dfrac{P(u,v)}{P(u)P(v)}}{-\log P(u,v)} \in [-1,1] over all pairs (probabilities estimated from document cooccurrences): it measures the actual collocation of the topic’s words — well correlated with humans.

The engineer’s rule: perplexity for predictive capacity, NPMI/C_v for interpretability.

Proposition 5.4 — LSA: truncated SVD as the PCA of text On the TF-IDF matrix \mathbf{X}: \mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\mathbf{V}_k^\top ; each row of \mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k gives one document’s coordinates in the latent space (the rows of \mathbf{X} are the documents). The k dimensions are the directions of maximal second moment — not of variance, since \mathbf{X} is not centered — and the coordinate columns are orthogonal, which makes them uncorrelated only after centering. The columns of \mathbf{V}_k give the topics as signed word loadings (unlike LDA’s distributions, they can be negative).

Key differences: LSA is linear, deterministic, fast; LDA is generative, models the counts, yields interpretable mixtures — but LSA captures weighted cooccurrence and is often sufficient for document similarity.

Link with PCA: PCA = SVD of the centered-scaled matrix (ML module); LSA = SVD of the TF-IDF matrix (no centering: the “mean” of text makes no sense).

  • After LDA: BERTopic (embeddings + clustering + c-TF-IDF) for topics on contemporary corpora — consistent with the “embeddings, ch. 4” pipeline; for the industrial use of topics: monitoring, compliance, e-discovery, detection of emerging themes;
  • Word Sense Induction: applying clustering to all the occurrences of a single word (BERT contexts) reveals its senses — the joint Vazirgiannis–Buscaldi paper of ch. 9 does exactly that, with mutual information maximization;
  • Topic models on graphs (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018): the idea extended to networks (see the Graph ML module).

6. Sequences and relations: NER, HMM, CRF, relation extraction

Some tasks need a label for every word rather than for the whole document: finding the names of persons and companies in a sentence, or the grammatical category of each word. Neighboring labels depend on each other (an entity that begins must continue), so this chapter uses models of the whole label sequence: hidden Markov models, then conditional random fields.

Definition 6.1 — Sequence labeling Assign a label to each token: POS (grammatical category) or NER (named entities: PER, ORG, LOC…) in BIO (“Begin/Inside/Outside”): “Kim” = B-PER, “Noel” = I-PER. NER is the gateway to building knowledge graphs (entity + relation extraction).

HMM: forward, backward, Viterbi — the recurrences derived HMM: hidden states z_t (labels), emissions \mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z_t), transitions \mathbb{P}(z_t\mid z_{t-1}). Notation: \alpha_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_1,\dots,w_t,\,z_t=z) (forward) and \beta_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_{t+1},\dots,w_T\mid z_t=z) (backward): \alpha_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z)\sum_{z'}\alpha_{t-1}(z')\,\mathbb{P}(z\mid z'), \qquad \beta_t(z)=\sum_{z'}\mathbb{P}(z'\mid z)\,\mathbb{P}(w_{t+1}\mid z')\,\beta_{t+1}(z').

Proof.

  • Forward: condition on z_{t-1}. \alpha_t(z)=\sum_{z'}\mathbb{P}(w_{1:t},z_{t-1}=z',z_t=z) =\sum_{z'}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(w_{1:t-1},z_{t-1}=z')}_{\alpha_{t-1}(z')} \,\mathbb{P}(z\mid z')\,\mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z) — by the Markov property, given z_{t-1} the pair (z_t, w_t) does not depend on the earlier observations.
  • Backward: symmetric, conditioning on z_{t+1}.

Uses: likelihood \mathbb{P}(w_{1:T})=\sum_z \alpha_T(z); marginal posteriors \mathbb{P}(z_t\mid w)=\alpha_t(z)\beta_t(z)/\mathbb{P}(w) — this is the E-step of an EM to train the HMM (ML module). All in \mathcal{O}(T K^2). ∎

Algorithm 6.2 — Viterbi: optimality by substructure The most likely label path satisfies the recursion \delta_t(z) = \max_{z'} \big[\delta_{t-1}(z')\, \mathbb{P}(z \mid z')\big]\ \mathbb{P}(w_t \mid z),\qquad \delta_1(z)=\mathbb{P}(z)\mathbb{P}(w_1\mid z), and the optimal labeling is read off by backtracking the argmaxes — \mathcal{O}(T K^2) instead of \mathcal{O}(K^T).

Proof. Optimality: any optimal path arriving at (t,z) has an optimal prefix up to (t-1,z') — otherwise replacing the prefix with a better one increases the total probability (exchange argument: the future factors depend only on z, Markovian). So it suffices to follow the best predecessor: this is Bellman (RL module, ch. 2) on a Markov chain, with max instead of expectation. ∎

CRF (Lafferty et al., 2001): the discriminative approach An HMM models \mathbb{P}(w,z) jointly (generative); a CRF models \mathbb{P}(z\mid w) directly: \mathbb{P}(z\mid w)=\frac{\exp\big(\sum_{t}\theta^\top f(z_{t-1},z_t,w,t)\big)}{Z(w)}, \qquad \nabla_\theta \log\mathbb{P}(z^\star\mid w) = F(z^\star, w)-\mathbb{E}_{p(z\mid w)}\big[F(z, w)\big] (with z^\star the gold labeling and F(z, w) = \sum_t f(z_{t-1}, z_t, w, t) the summed features) — arbitrary features over the whole sequence: word identity, suffixes, capitalization, shape of the neighbor, hyphen, position…, plus transition features z_{t-1}\to z_t (which forbid O→I-PER: an entity start must be B).

Inference: forward-backward for Z(w) and the feature expectation; Viterbi for prediction.

The gain over HMM: no “waste” in modeling the words, rich features, arbitrary interactions — long the state of the art for NER (CoNLL-2003).

BIO decoding and entity-level NER evaluation Per-token predictions must be converted into spans: an entity = a B-X (I-X)* sequence. Entity evaluation (not token): an entity is correct iff both type and exact boundaries match; \mathrm{P}=\frac{\#\text{correct}}{\#\text{predicted}},\qquad \mathrm{R}=\frac{\#\text{correct}}{\#\text{gold}},\qquad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}. Token-level F1 (lenient about boundaries) always overestimates entity-level F1 — always report entity-level F1 (the conlleval standard). Industrial reflex: audit boundary errors (“Banque de France” truncated) separately from type errors (PER vs ORG).

Today Transformers label token by token (per-token softmax + BIO decoding; the head is often a linear-chain CRF or a simple softmax with BIO constraints in post-processing); the spaCy and transformers libraries (the ner pipeline) deliver the state of the art in a few lines; the lab builds an entity-relation extractor that feeds the mini-GraphRAG of the Graph ML module — ch. 8 of this course for the full pipeline.

7. Transformers for NLP

The models of the previous chapters give each word one vector whatever its context, and read a sentence through a short window at best. Transformers compute for each token a representation that depends on the whole sentence; pretrained once on a large corpus, they are then adapted to each task with little labeled data. This chapter applies the architecture of the Deep Learning module to text.

  • Architecture: carried over from the Deep Learning module ch. 6 (multi-head attention \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V, residuals, LayerNorm, encoder/decoder); here we apply it to text — the M2 LLMs course (ch. 2) will revisit the architecture in depth (RoPE, GQA, MoE…);

BERT (Devlin et al., 2019): MLM + NSP in detail Bidirectional encoder, pretrained on two tasks.

  1. Masked LM: 15 % of tokens drawn; among them 80 % replaced by [MASK], 10 % by a random word, 10 % unchanged; the original token is predicted (output: per-position classification). The mixing avoids a pretraining/fine-tuning mismatch (the model will never see [MASK] downstream again) and forces a robust representation.
  2. Next Sentence Prediction: classify whether two sentences follow each other (the pair [CLS] A [SEP] B) — a weak task, abandoned by RoBERTa in favor of more data.

Result: [CLS] = sentence representation; one model, dozens of tasks via a simple head layer.

GPT (Radford et al.) and T5 (Raffel et al., 2019) GPT: causal decoder — \mathcal{L}=-\sum_t\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t}), pretraining = predicting the next token; generative by construction, zero task-specific architecture (this is the family extended in M2).

T5: everything in text-to-text — span corruption (masking continuous fragments and regenerating them), every task (classification, translation, QA…) formulated as textual input→output; it unifies MLM and CLM.

Protocol 7.1 — Full fine-tuning (the stable recipe)

  1. Head: a linear layer \mathrm{Linear}(d, K) on [CLS] (classification) or per position (NER, POS);
  2. Optimizer: AdamW, learning rate \{2\times10^{-5}, 3\times10^{-5}, 5\times10^{-5}\} — a short grid, fine-tuning is sensitive but flat around the optimum; linear warmup for 10 % then linear decay;
  3. Epochs: 2 to 4 (beyond that: brutal overfitting on small corpora — watch the dev set); batch 16-32;
  4. Small corpora (<1k): freeze the lower layers, layer-wise decay (layer-wise lr decay), or adapters/LoRA (added parameters << 1 % — revisited in the M2 ML Ops module, ch. 7);
  5. Cost: distillation (DistilBERT: 40 % fewer parameters, 60 % faster at inference, ~97 % of the performance of BERT), int8 quantization — the performance/cost trade-off is an engineering criterion, deepened in M2 (LLMs ch. 6, ML Ops).

Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019): the triplet loss derived To encode sentences in a comparable space (search, clustering, RAG): siamese BERT + pooling (mean), trained with a triplet loss on (anchor a, positive p, negative n): \mathcal{L}=\max\big(0,\ d(a,p)-d(a,n)+\varepsilon\big).

Proof. If the margin is respected (d(a,p)+\varepsilon\le d(a,n)), the loss is zero: nothing to learn. Otherwise, the gradient \nabla = \nabla d(a,p)-\nabla d(a,n) pulls the anchor toward the positive and pushes it away from the negative; with this hinge loss, the size of the update does not depend on how much the margin is violated.

Crucial point: the quality depends on the negatives (mini-batch: the other pairs provide hard negatives — this is what shapes the space). This is the kind of embeddings that indexes the corpora of RAG systems — detailed in M2 LLMs ch. 7 (dense retrieval, reranking). ∎

French: CamemBERT, FlauBERT, JuriBERT CamemBERT (Martin et al., 2020): RoBERTa retrained on 138 GB of French text — the reference French model; FlauBERT: an academic French BERT; JuriBERT (Douka et al., 2021 — research from this course, ch. 9): an adaptation to legal French, where the vocabulary (doctrine, grievances, appeal) confuses general models. Multilingual: mBERT, XLM-R (100+ languages, shared embeddings — at the price of capacity diluted per language).

8. Industrial text mining

This chapter assembles the building blocks of ch. 1-7 into products: summaries, keywords, deduplication, ticket classification — the real workloads of NLP in industry, together with their engineering pitfalls.

8.1. TextRank: extractive summarization by PageRank

TextRank (Mihalcea & Tarau, 2004)

  1. Sentence → node; similarity between sentences S_i,S_j (overlap of filtered words): w_{ij}=\frac{|\{t\in S_i\}\cap\{t\in S_j\}|}{\log|S_i|+\log|S_j|} (|S_i| = number of words of S_i);
  2. symmetric weights → random walk;
  3. score: \mathrm{TR}(u)=\frac{1-d}{N}+d\sum_{v\to u}\frac{w_{vu}}{\sum_{v'}w_{vv'}}\,\mathrm{TR}(v), iterated from the uniform distribution (matrix power); geometric convergence to the unique stationary distribution (Perron–Frobenius: primitive stochastic matrix — a Markov chain reminder, ch. 0);
  4. the k top-scoring sentences = the summary (reordered chronologically).

The same algorithm on the graph of words (cooccurrence window) yields keyword extraction — “PageRank for text”.

Proof. The recursion reads \pi^{(k+1)}=\big[(1-d)\tfrac{\mathbb{1}}{N} +d\,M\big]\pi^{(k)} with M the weighted transition matrix: the operator is contracting (factor d<1), hence geometric convergence at rate d — typically settling in ~20-30 iterations for d=0.85. ∎

TextRank keeps the letter d for its damping factor, and d = 1 - \alpha where \alpha is the site-wide teleport probability.

8.2. Keyword extraction: RAKE, YAKE, KeyBERT

  • RAKE (Rose et al., 2010): split on stopwords/punctuation → candidate phrases; a word’s score = \deg(w)/\mathrm{freq}(w) (degree in the cooccurrence graph within the candidates) + bigram frequency; a phrase’s score = the sum of its words. Fast, no external corpus, sensitive to stopwords;
  • YAKE (Campos et al., 2020): unsupervised statistical features per word — frequency, dispersion of positions (a keyword appears everywhere), case (a capital letter mid-sentence = signal), redundancy of variants (edit distance) — an aggregated score, with left/right windows for context; robust and multilingual;
  • KeyBERT (Grootendorst, 2020): extract candidates (n-grams), embed them (ch. 3), keep the ones closest to the document — and diversify with MMR: \mathrm{MMR}=\arg\max_{c\,\in\,C\setminus S}\ \Big[\lambda\cos(c,d)-(1-\lambda)\max_{s\in S}\cos(c,s)\Big], which adds to the set S the candidate that is relevant and not very redundant; the same “embeddings + MMR” pattern returns in RAG (M2, ch. 7).

Extractive vs abstractive Extractive (TextRank; BERT: ranking sentences): attributable, hallucination-free, but redundant and choppy. Abstractive (generating): fluent, but requires a generative model and a faithfulness evaluation — this is the territory of M2 (LLMs ch. 7-8) and of the M2 instructor’s research (meeting summarization, cf. ch. 9). In industry: extractive remains the trusted solution when every sentence must be traceable to its source.

8.3. Deduplication at scale: MinHash + LSH

Proposition 8.1 — MinHash estimates the Jaccard similarity For uniform h,

Key result — MinHash collision probability \mathbb{P}\big(\min_{x\in A} h(x)=\min_{y\in B} h(y)\big) = J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}.

Stacking m hashings → a signature \mathrm{sig}(A)\in\{0,\dots,2^{64}\}^m, and the collision frequency estimates J.

Proof. Let z be the element of A\cup B minimizing h (a.s. unique for uniform h). Then \min h(A)=\min h(B) iff z\in A\cap B.

Now z is uniform over A\cup B (permutation invariance of hashings), hence the probability \frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}. ∎

LSH (Locality-Sensitive Hashing): bands and rows The signature is split into b bands of r rows; two documents are candidates if an entire band coincides. Detection probability: \mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{detect}}(J)=1-\big(1-J^{\,r}\big)^{b} — a steep sigmoid: with r=5,\ b=20: J=0.5\to 47\,\%, J=0.8\to 99.96\,\%.

The accepted/rejected boundary is “chosen” through (r,b) — this is the exact deduplication mechanism used on LLM pretraining corpora (M2 LLMs, ch. 3, where it is detailed with scale figures; lab implementation: datasketch).

8.4. Complete case: support ticket classification

Case study — 50,000 tickets, 40 categories, 3 languages

  1. Data: tickets (subject + body), heavily imbalanced categories (software ~30 %, licensing ~0.2 %); fragmentary annotation, noisy history;
  2. Preprocessing: NFKC (ch. 1), language detection, replacement of product references/numbers with generic tokens (<REF>, <DATE>) — the highest-impact step: tickets are 30 % codes;
  3. Baseline: TF-IDF (unigrams, sublinear) + weighted logistic regression → macro-F1 ≈ 0.72 — the bar to beat;
  4. Model: fine-tuned CamemBERT/multilingual (protocol 7.1) → macro-F1 ≈ 0.82; the main gain is on rare classes;
  5. Errors: confusion matrix — “login” vs “password” systematically confused (a fuzzy semantic boundary for humans too); solution: business-class merging or a rejection threshold (confidence < τ → human queue);
  6. Production: drift (new products → OOV): input distribution monitoring, quarterly retraining, active learning on rejected tickets (the most uncertain = the most informative).
  • Question answering and RAG: retriever (BM25 — the direct descendant of ch. 2 — and/or dense; hybrid search) + generator; the reference module is M2 LLMs (ch. 7); the capstone lab builds a graph version of it;

8.5. Recommendation systems

The official description names recommendation systems next to retrieval as a major industrial use. A recommender is a search engine without a query: it ranks items for a user, and text is one of its two sources of signal — the other being the behaviour of other users.

Definition 8.2 — Content-based and collaborative recommendation Users u, items i, and a very sparse interaction matrix R \in \mathbb{R}^{|U| \times |I|} (ratings, clicks, purchases).

Content-based: represent each item by a text vector x_i (TF-IDF of ch. 2, or a sentence embedding of ch. 7), build the user profile p_u = \sum_{i \in I_u} r_{ui}\, x_i \big/ \sum_{i \in I_u} r_{ui} from the items the user liked, and score \cos(p_u, x_i). It handles new items (their text exists before any click) but over-specializes.

Collaborative filtering ignores content and exploits the agreement between users: user-based neighbours predict \hat r_{ui} = \bar r_u + \frac{\sum_v \mathrm{sim}(u, v)(r_{vi} - \bar r_v)}{\sum_v |\mathrm{sim}(u, v)|} over the most similar users v who rated i (item-based neighbours are symmetric and more stable in practice).

Model 8.3 — Matrix factorization (Koren, Bell & Volinsky, 2009) Model \hat r_{ui} = \mu + b_u + b_i + p_u^\top q_i with latent factors p_u, q_i \in \mathbb{R}^k, and fit only the observed pairs \Omega: \min \sum_{(u,i) \in \Omega} \big(r_{ui} - \hat r_{ui}\big)^2 + \lambda\big(\|p_u\|^2 + \|q_i\|^2 + b_u^2 + b_i^2\big).

SGD update for one observed pair, with error e_{ui} = r_{ui} - \hat r_{ui}:

Key result — SGD step of matrix factorization p_u \leftarrow p_u + \eta\,(e_{ui}\, q_i - \lambda\, p_u), \qquad q_i \leftarrow q_i + \eta\,(e_{ui}\, p_u - \lambda\, q_i).

Proof. The loss of one pair is \ell = e_{ui}^2 + \lambda\|p_u\|^2 + \lambda\|q_i\|^2 (biases treated the same way). Since \partial \hat r_{ui}/\partial p_u = q_i, \nabla_{p_u}\ell = -2\,e_{ui}\,q_i + 2\lambda\,p_u, \qquad \nabla_{q_i}\ell = -2\,e_{ui}\,p_u + 2\lambda\,q_i (symmetrically); a gradient step with the factor 2 absorbed into \eta gives the updates.

ALS alternative: with Q fixed, the problem in p_u is a ridge regression on the items rated by u, solved in closed form p_u = (Q_u^\top Q_u + \lambda I)^{-1} Q_u^\top r_u — independently and in parallel for all users, then the roles swap. ∎

The model is the truncated SVD of LSA (ch. 5) adapted to a matrix with missing entries — a plain SVD would treat them as zeros. Implicit feedback (clicks) uses confidence weights on all entries (Hu, Koren & Volinsky, 2008).

Hybrid models tie the item factor to its text, q_i = W x_i + \varepsilon_i, which gives a factor to items nobody has interacted with yet; industrial two-tower retrievers push this further, with a user encoder and an item text encoder whose dot product is searched by approximate nearest neighbours.

Example 8.4 — One SGD step, computed Setting:

  • k = 2, \mu = 3.5, b_u = 0.2, b_i = -0.1;
  • p_u = (0.5, 0.1), q_i = (0.4, 0.3);
  • an observed rating r_{ui} = 5, \eta = 0.1, \lambda = 0.05.

Prediction: p_u^\top q_i = 0.23, so \hat r_{ui} = 3.83 and e_{ui} = 1.17.

Updates (each using the old values): \begin{aligned} p_u &\leftarrow (0.5, 0.1) + 0.1\,(0.443, 0.346) = (0.544, 0.135),\\ q_i &\leftarrow (0.4, 0.3) + 0.1\,(0.565, 0.102) = (0.457, 0.310),\\ b_u &\leftarrow 0.2 + 0.1\,(1.17 - 0.01) = 0.316,\\ b_i &\leftarrow -0.1 + 0.1\,(1.17 + 0.005) \approx 0.018. \end{aligned}

New prediction: 3.5 + 0.316 + 0.018 + 0.290 \approx 4.12 — the error went from 1.17 to about 0.88 in one step.

Recommenders are evaluated offline with the ranking metrics of Definition 2.7 (Recall@k, nDCG@k) on a temporal split, watching the popularity bias; the user–item graph view (PinSage) is in the Graph ML module.

8.6. Web advertising and social media analysis

The other two industrial markets named by the description — web marketing/advertising and social network analysis — are text problems at their core: matching queries to ads, and reading short, noisy posts at scale.

Definition 8.5 — Sponsored search and click-through-rate prediction For a search query q, the engine retrieves the ads whose advertiser keywords match q — exact, phrase or broad match, the last one relying on query rewriting and embeddings (ch. 4, 7) — then ranks them by expected value \mathrm{bid} \times \hat p(\text{click} \mid q, \text{ad}, \text{context}).

The click model is typically a logistic regression \hat p = \sigma(w^\top \phi(q, \text{ad}, \text{user})) over billions of sparse features — query terms, ad terms and their crosses — mapped by the hashing trick (Proposition 2.5) and trained online with L1 regularization (FTRL-Proximal, McMahan et al., 2013).

Because \hat p multiplies a price, calibration (ch. 3) is a business requirement, not a refinement.

Example: ad A bids 2.0 with \hat p = 0.02, ad B bids 1.2 with \hat p = 0.05; expected values 0.04 and 0.06 — B is shown first despite the lower bid.

Definition 8.6 — Social media text and trend detection Posts are short, noisy and multilingual, full of hashtags, mentions, emojis and URLs. Normalization (ch. 1) replaces mentions and URLs by placeholders, splits hashtags (#GenoaFlood → “genoa flood”) and keeps emojis as tokens, since they carry sentiment.

Typical tasks are sentiment and emotion classification (ch. 3, or a tweet-pretrained encoder, ch. 7), stance and rumour detection, and event or trend detection: for a term t with counts c_t(\tau) per time window, flag a burst when z = \big(c_t(\tau) - \bar c_t\big)/s_t exceeds a threshold, with \bar c_t, s_t the mean and standard deviation over past windows (Poisson or Kleinberg burst models refine this for small counts).

Joining the text with the follower or retweet graph — communities, influence by PageRank — is social network analysis proper, developed in the Graph ML module; Buscaldi’s flood-tweet study (ch. 9) is a research instance.

Example 8.7 — Detecting a burst A hashtag was used 12, 9, 11, 10, 8, 10 times in the last six hours: mean \bar c = 10, sample variance \frac{4 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 0}{5} = 2, s \approx 1.41.

In the current hour it appears 31 times: z = 21 / 1.41 \approx 14.8, far above a threshold of 3 — a burst, which the monitoring pipeline then hands to keyword extraction (8.2) and classification to describe the event.

Exercises

  1. ★ TF-IDF on paper. On 3 given documents, compute by hand the TF-IDF matrix (natural log) of the reduced vocabulary, then the cosine similarity of each pair; verify with TfidfVectorizer (watch out for L2 normalization).

    Proof. Solution (method):

    1. count tf(t,d);
    2. df(t) over the 3 documents, then \mathrm{idf}=\log(3/\mathrm{df}) (0 for a term present everywhere — it disappears);
    3. multiply;
    4. normalize each row \tilde d=d/\|d\|_2, so that \cos(d_i,d_j)=\tilde d_i^\top \tilde d_j — this is what sklearn returns;
    5. classic pitfall: sklearn uses the smoothed IDF \log\frac{1+n}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1; pass smooth_idf=False to recover the formula from the course. ∎

  2. ★ IDF = PMI. Rederive proposition 2.3 in the cases \mathrm{df}(t)=n (term everywhere) and \mathrm{df}(t)=1 (unique term); deduce why L2 weighting alone is not enough to squash omnipresent words.

    Proof. Solution:

    • \mathrm{df}=n \Rightarrow \mathrm{idf}=\log 1=0 — the term discriminates no document (zero PMI);
    • \mathrm{df}=1 \Rightarrow \mathrm{idf}=\log n — the term perfectly identifies its document.

    The L2 norm rescales documents, not columns: a frequent word keeps a high tf in all documents — only IDF (a per-column weight) squashes it. ∎

  3. ★ BPE. Redo example 1.4 with the corpus stage×3, stages×2, stand×4 and list the 5 first merges; what happens if we add stand×40?

    Proof. Solution (start): initial pairs (s,t)=9, (t,a)=9, (a,g)=5, (a,n)=4… — the initial (s,t)/(t,a) tie is broken arbitrarily; with stand×40, the pair (a,n) reaches 44 and stand becomes a token before the other roots — this is BPE’s lesson: the vocabulary encodes the corpus frequencies, not linguistics. ∎

  4. ★ Analogies and bias. With pretrained embeddings (GloVe fr), test 10 analogies and measure the success rate; compute the WEAT statistic on two sets of professions × two lists of first names; implement neutralization (projection removal) and measure the effect on both scores.

  5. ★ Viterbi. Implement Viterbi for a toy POS tagger (2 labels, 5-word vocabulary); verify optimality against exhaustive enumeration; complexity \mathcal{O}(TK^2); modify the proof of thm 6.2 for the case of paths with zero weights.

  6. ★ MinHash/LSH. For r=5, b=20, plot \mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{detect}}(J)=1-(1-J^r)^b and locate the “threshold”; which pair (r,b) at m=256 gives a threshold close to J=0.7?

    Proof. Solution: the sigmoid goes from ~0.1 to ~0.9 between J\approx0.35 and J\approx0.65 — threshold \approx(1/b)^{1/r}\approx 0.55.

    To target 0.7 one needs b \approx 0.7^{-r} with rb \le 256.

    Then r=9, b=25 gives (1/25)^{1/9}\approx 0.70 with 225 of the 256 hashes (while r=8, b=32 uses all 256 but puts the threshold near 0.65).

    Keep the rule of thumb: threshold \approx (1/b)^{1/r}, and a larger m makes the curve steeper. ∎

  7. ★★ BM25. Show that the tf factor of BM25 is increasing, bounded by k_1+1, and reaches half its value at \mathrm{tf}=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl}); comment on the effect of b=0.9 on a corpus of documents with widely varying lengths.

    Proof. Solution: set g(x)=\frac{x(k_1+1)}{x+c} with c=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl})>0. Then g'(x)=\frac{c(k_1+1)}{(x+c)^2}>0, \qquad g\to k_1+1, \qquad g(x)=\frac{k_1+1}{2} \Leftrightarrow x=c .

    High b: long documents see their c increase → slower saturation and a stronger penalty — suited when length signals richness; dangerous on heterogeneous mixtures of short/long documents. ∎

  8. ★★ Naive Bayes. Derive the MLE estimator of \mathbb{P}(t \mid c), show the problem of zero probabilities and justify Laplace smoothing as the posterior mean under Dirichlet(1,…,1) (theorem 3.1); implement and compare against MultinomialNB on 20 Newsgroups; test the effect of α on calibration.

    Proof. Solution (core): Lagrangians → relative counts; a test word absent from the class → \log 0: the class is eliminated; the uniform prior adds a “pseudo-count” of 1 to every term → the smoothed formula; α>1 squashes differences (less overconfident), α<1 sharpens them (more discriminative, less calibrated). ∎

  9. ★★ Skip-gram. Write the negative sampling loss with center vector v_w and context vector u_c (theorem 4.1), \log \sigma(u_c^\top v_w) + \sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{n_k \sim p_n}\log \sigma(-u_{n_k}^\top v_w); compute the gradient in v_w and interpret it (who is attracted, who is repelled, with what force); implement on a small corpus and check that words with similar contexts have close embeddings.

Descent moves the center toward the context with weight (1-\sigma), and moves it away from the negatives with weight \sigma. Check: cosine neighborhood of “graphe” ≈ {réseau, nœud, arête}. ∎
