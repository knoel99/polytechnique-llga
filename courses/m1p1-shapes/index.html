0. Refresher: convexity, least squares, eigenvalues

L3 prerequisites mobilized

  • Convex hull: convex hull of P = smallest convex set containing P, described as the set of barycenters \sum_i \lambda_i p_i, \lambda_i \geq 0, \sum \lambda_i = 1; its faces are obtained by linear programs — chapter 3 uses it via the paraboloid lift;
  • Least squares: minimizing \\|Ax - b\\|^2 is equivalent to the normal equations A^\top\!A x = A^\top b; geometric interpretation: orthogonal projection onto the image of A — used in chapter 6 (quadrics) and in reconstruction (chapter 4);
  • Eigenvalues and eigenvectors: a real symmetric matrix is diagonalizable in an orthonormal basis; quadratic forms x^\top A x and their constrained minimization — the heart of chapter 8 (spectrum);
  • Fourier series on [0, 2\pi]: orthogonality of sines/exponentials — the smoothing of chapter 5 is understood as low-pass filtering mode by mode;
  • A bit of topology: homeomorphism, connected components, “number of holes” — formalized in chapter 9 by the Euler characteristic and homology.

Proposition 0.1 — Three objects, one duality For a finite point set P \subset \mathbb{R}^2 in general position, the following objects define one another:

  • the convex hull \mathrm{conv}(P) (faces = “extreme” points);
  • the Voronoi diagram (cells of nearest points, chapter 2);
  • the Delaunay triangulation (triangles with empty circumcircles, chapter 3).

Theorem 3.4 will show that the Delaunay is the image of the lower part of the convex hull of the points lifted onto the paraboloid — duality becomes a theorem of elementary linear algebra (a plane cuts a parabola).

1. Representing a shape: meshes and half-edges

Concrete scene: a 3D reconstruction pipeline that “works” in the lab and fails as soon as cloud density changes. This chapter fixes the representations.

What breaks Confusing point cloud / mesh / volumetric; omitting normals; smoothing without preserving sharp edges.

Definition 1.1 — Four families of representations

  • Explicit — point cloud: \{p_i\}_{i=1}^n \subset \mathbb{R}^3, the natural output of sensors (LiDAR, depth); no connections, no reliable normals;
  • Explicit — mesh: vertices V, edges E, faces F (most often triangles); this is the lingua franca of rendering;
  • Implicit: the shape is \{x : f(x) = 0\} for a scalar field f (SDF, potential, indicator); union/intersection trivial, sampling non-trivial (chapter 4);
  • Volumetric / parametric: voxels (grids) and parametric surfaces u \mapsto x(u, v) — NURBS patches of modelers, chart atlases of remeshing (chapter 6).

Definition 1.2 — Clean meshes (manifold, watertight, orientable) A triangulated mesh is a manifold surface if: (i) each edge is shared by at most 2 faces; (ii) the faces around each vertex form a single cycle (the “umbrella”). It is watertight (closed) if each edge has exactly 2 faces — it then bounds a volume; orientable if the face normals can be chosen consistently (any watertight mesh embedded without self-intersection is). These properties are prerequisites for the operators of chapters 5 to 9; real clouds violate them — hence chapter 4.

Proposition 1.3 — The half-edge structure The half-edge data structure orients each edge into two half-edges (u \to v) and (v \to u) and stores, for each half-edge h: its opposite (\mathrm{opp}(h)), the face to its left (\mathrm{face}(h)), the next half-edge of that face (\mathrm{next}(h)) and its origin vertex (\mathrm{org}(h)). Then:

  • the tour of a face is obtained by following \mathrm{next}; the tour of a vertex (its umbrella) by composing \mathrm{opp} \circ \mathrm{next};
  • the 1-ring neighborhood of a vertex — the basis of smoothing (ch. 5), curvature (ch. 7) and the convolutions of Geometric Deep Learning — is traversed in O(\deg);
  • memory cost: 2 half-edges per edge × 3 pointers ≈ 48 bytes/edge — versus ~4 bytes/edge for the raw “faces” table; this is the price of constant-time neighbor traversal.

Example 1.4 — The tetrahedron in tables V = 4, E = 6, F = 4. Face tables: (0,1,2), (0,3,1), (1,3,2), (2,3,0) — checking the orientation: the normal (p_1 - p_0) \times (p_2 - p_0) points outward for each face. Half-edges: 12 in total, each pair (i,j)/(j,i) matched. Euler parity: V - E + F = 4 - 6 + 4 = 2 = 2 - 2g ⟹ genus g = 0 — the tetrahedron is topologically a sphere (chapter 9). Lab reflex: after every mesh load, check V - E + F, edges with 1 face (boundaries) and with 3+ faces (non-manifold) — two lines with hash tables.

On the right side of the boundary In industrial practice (CGAL, libigl, OpenMesh), one distinguishes the combinatorial mesh (tables, half-edges) from the geometric mesh (3D positions); two isomorphic meshes share the whole theory of chapters 5–9, independently of the positions. The algorithms of this module are first written on the combinatorics (topology), then embedded in the positions — the same discipline as for the graphs of the Machine Learning module.

Definition 1.5 — Smooth 2D curves: polynomial, Hermite and spline interpolation Before surfaces, parametric curves t \mapsto c(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2: this is how one draws on a machine (fonts, outlines, tool trajectories). Three ways of generating them from data:

  • Polynomial interpolation: through n+1 points passes a unique polynomial of degree \leq n (Lagrange). But beyond a few points, the Runge phenomenon makes the polynomial oscillate between the nodes: one only uses it piecewise;
  • Hermite interpolation: one imposes at each node a position and a tangent — the piecewise cubic Hermite H(t) = h_{00}(t)\,p_0 + h_{10}(t)\,m_0 + h_{01}(t)\,p_1 + h_{11}(t)\,m_1 (basis polynomials h of degree 3) joins the points with the desired tangents: the control that keyframing exploits in animation;
  • Splines: piecewise polynomials of degree k, joined with k-1 continuous derivatives at the nodes. The natural cubic spline minimizes \int \\|c''(t)\\|^2 \mathrm{d}t among interpolating curves — the bending energy of a drafting spline (the word comes from there); its computation reduces to a tridiagonal system, and B-splines provide the locally supported basis — of which the NURBS of definition 1.1 are the rational generalization to surfaces.

Definition 1.6 — Bézier curves and the de Casteljau algorithm A Bézier curve of degree n is carried by control points b_0, \dots, b_n via the Bernstein polynomials: c(t) \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{n} B_i^n(t)\, b_i, \qquad B_i^n(t) = \binom{n}{i} t^i (1-t)^{n-i}, \;\; t \in [0,1]. Properties: c(0) = b_0, c(1) = b_n, end tangents n(b_1 - b_0) and n(b_n - b_{n-1}), and the curve lives in the convex hull of its control points (the B_i^n form a partition of unity) — hence the “handle” control of drawing software. The de Casteljau algorithm evaluates c(t) by successive linear interpolations b_i^{r} = (1-t)\,b_i^{r-1} + t\,b_{i+1}^{r-1} up to b_0^n = c(t): numerically stable (no subtraction of large terms, unlike the expanded Bernstein form), it also produces the subdivision at the point t — the intermediate points b_0^0, \dots, b_0^n and b_0^n, \dots, b_n^n form two control sets whose two Bézier curves rejoin exactly at c(t). This iterated refinement is the matrix of the subdivision surfaces of chapter 6 (def. 6.5) and the natural bridge to the curves of the animation modules.

2. Voronoi diagrams and duality

Definition 2.1 — Voronoi cell For a finite set of sites P = \{p_1, \dots, p_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d, the Voronoi cell of p_i is \mathcal{V}(p_i) = \big\{\, x \in \mathbb{R}^d \;:\; \\|x - p_i\\| \leq \\|x - p_j\\| \ \ \forall j \,\big\} = \bigcap_{j \neq i} H_{ij}, where H_{ij} is the half-space of points closer to p_i than to p_j (delimited by the perpendicular bisector plane of [p_i, p_j]). The Voronoi diagram \mathrm{Vor}(P) is the partition \bigcup_i \mathcal{V}(p_i).

Proposition 2.2 — Three immediate properties (i) Each cell is a convex polytope (finite intersection of half-spaces). (ii) The cells with non-empty interior form a partition of space; every boundary point is at equal distance from at least two sites. (iii) A Voronoi vertex (point where d+1 cells meet in general position) is the center of a sphere passing through these d+1 sites and containing no other site in its interior.

Proof. (i) By the very definition, as the intersection of the H_{ij}. (ii) For every x, the set of nearest sites is non-empty; x is interior to its cell if it is strictly closer to a single site. (iii) A vertex v belongs to the cells of p_{i_0}, \dots, p_{i_d} (exactly d+1 in general position): \\|v - p_{i_k}\\| = \rho for all k — hence v is the center of a sphere of radius \rho passing through these sites. If a site p_j were at distance \< \rho, then v would belong to the cell of p_j, contradicting general position. ∎

Definition 2.3 — Complexity In dimension 2, a diagram of n sites has at most 2n - 5 vertices and 3n - 6 edges (planar — Euler’s formula); in dimension d, the worst-case complexity is \Theta(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil}) — already quadratic in dimension 4: high-dimensional Voronoi diagrams are expensive explicit objects, manipulated via their dual graphs (chapter 3) or by queries (kd-trees).

Example 2.4 — Three sites, complete computation Sites p_1 = (0,0), p_2 = (4,0), p_3 = (0,3). Bisector planes: for [p_1 p_2]: x = 2; for [p_1 p_3]: y = 1.5; for [p_2 p_3]: \\|x - p_2\\|^2 = \\|x - p_3\\|^2 gives 8x - 6y = 7. The Voronoi vertex is the circumcenter of the triangle: intersection of x = 2 and y = 1.5, that is (2,\, 1.5), at distance r = \sqrt{2^2 + 1.5^2} = 2.5 from the three sites — verified: \\|(2,1.5) - (4,0)\\| = \\|(2,1.5) - (0,3)\\| = 2.5 ✓. The Voronoi edge between \mathcal{V}(p_1) and \mathcal{V}(p_2) is the ray of x = 2 starting from (2, 1.5) downward. The whole geometry of the chapter is already there: perpendicular bisectors, circumcircle, triangle/vertex duality.

Immediate applications The Voronoi diagram answers the nearest neighbor query by cell location; in robotics, the path “farthest from obstacles” follows the medial axis (Voronoi edges); in imaging, segmentation by Voronoi diagram of markers (these are the dual watersheds); Voronoi tessellations sample textures; and the generalization \mathcal{V}_S(p) = \{x : d(x, p) \leq d(x, S)\} (diagrams of complex sites — segments, disks) drives manufacturing robotics.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import Voronoi, Delaunay

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
P = rng.uniform(0, 1, (60, 2))

vor = Voronoi(P)          # Voronoi diagram
dela = Delaunay(P)        # dual triangulation

# --- numerical verification of the empty circle criterion (chapter 3) ---
def circumcenter(a, b, c):
    d = 2 * (a[0]*(b[1]-c[1]) + b[0]*(c[1]-a[1]) + c[0]*(a[1]-b[1]))
    ux = ((a[0]**2+a[1]**2)*(b[1]-c[1]) + (b[0]**2+b[1]**2)*(c[1]-a[1])
          + (c[0]**2+c[1]**2)*(a[1]-b[1])) / d
    uy = ((a[0]**2+a[1]**2)*(c[0]-b[0]) + (b[0]**2+b[1]**2)*(a[0]-c[0])
          + (c[0]**2+c[1]**2)*(b[0]-a[0])) / d
    return np.array([ux, uy])

worst = np.inf
for tri in dela.simplices:                 # for each Delaunay triangle
    a, b, c = P[tri]
    o = circumcenter(a, b, c); r = np.linalg.norm(a - o)
    dmin = min(np.linalg.norm(P[j] - o) for j in range(len(P))
               if j not in tri)
    worst = min(worst, dmin - r)           # must remain >= 0 (margin)
print("minimal empty circle margin:", round(worst, 6))   # ~1e-2 or more

# --- Lloyd relaxation: toward a centroidal diagram ---
for _ in range(20):
    vor = Voronoi(P)
    for i, r_idx in enumerate(vor.regions[vor.point_region]):
        if r_idx and -1 not in r_idx:
            poly = vor.vertices[r_idx]
            c = poly.mean(axis=0)           # approx. of the cell centroid
            P[i] = 0.8 * P[i] + 0.2 * c     # damped displacement
# result: sites almost coincident with the centroids of their cells

3. Delaunay triangulations: the empty circle criterion

Definition 3.1 — Delaunay triangulation A triangulation T of P (maximal for edge inclusion) is Delaunay if for each triangle abc \in T, the open circumdisk contains no point of P: \forall\, abc \in T,\quad \mathrm{disk}(a, b, c) \cap P = \varnothing \qquad \text{(empty circle criterion)}. In dimension d, “triangle” is replaced by a simplex of dimension d and “disk” by ball. If P is in general position (no 4 cocircular points), the Delaunay is unique — it is the dual graph of the Voronoi diagram: one Voronoi vertex per triangle, one Voronoi edge per Delaunay edge.

Theorem 3.2 — Local criterion by angles (flip lemma) Let a b c d be a strictly convex quadrilateral triangulated by the diagonal ac (triangles abc and acd). Then: d \in \mathrm{disk}(a, b, c) \iff \angle abc + \angle adc \> \pi. In that case, the circumcircles of abc and acd contain d and b respectively (violations of the criterion), and the flip (replacing ac by bd) locally restores the criterion and strictly increases the smallest of the quadrilateral’s six angles.

Proof. (i) Cocircular criterion. For a chord [a c], the set of points that see [a c] under a given angle is an arc of a circle (inscribed angle theorem): if b is on the circle \Gamma of diameter… more precisely, let \Gamma denote the circumcircle of a, b, c; the point d, opposite to b with respect to the line (ac), sees the chord [ac] under the angle \pi - \angle abc if it is on \Gamma. If d enters strictly inside the disk (while staying on the side opposite to b), the angle under which it sees [ac] increases — the inscribed angle grows as the point approaches the chord along the circle, and the disk is the set of points “closer to the chord” than the arc: \angle adc \> \pi - \angle abc, that is \angle abc + \angle adc \> \pi. Conversely, if d exits the disk, \angle adc \< \pi - \angle abc. (ii) Symmetry. By exchanging the roles (b seen from triangle acd), the same inequality shows that b is in the circumdisk of acd exactly in the same case: the two violations go together. (iii) Angles. The four “flank” angles of the quadrilateral (at the vertices, off the diagonal) all reappear, unchanged, in one or the other of the triangulations. Before the flip, the angles opposite the diagonal are \angle abc and \angle adc, of sum \> \pi (hence one of the two \> \pi/2); after the flip, they are \angle bad and \angle bcd, of sum \< \pi. By applying point (i) to the two new pairs of triangles, each of the six angles of the new triangulation is strictly larger than the smallest of the angles opposite the old diagonal — hence the increase of the minimum. ∎

Theorem 3.3 — The Delaunay maximizes the smallest angle Among all triangulations of P, the Delaunay triangulation (unique in general position) is the one that maximizes the vector of angles sorted in increasing order, for the lexicographic order — in particular its smallest angle is maximal. This is what makes it indispensable in scientific computing: avoiding “flattened” triangles (large conditioning numbers for finite elements, chapter 5).

Proof. (Complete sketch.) Start from an arbitrary triangulation and apply the flips of Lemma 3.2 as long as an empty circle violation exists. Each flip strictly increases the sorted angle vector (Lemma 3.2); there are only finitely many triangulations, so the process stops on a triangulation without violation: it is Delaunay (local criterion ⟹ global: any triangulation whose triangles all have the empty circle is the Delaunay). The standard exchange argument between triangulations (any pair of triangulations is linked by a sequence of flips) shows that any lexicographic maximum is without violation: the optimum is the Delaunay. ∎

Theorem 3.4 — Lifting onto the paraboloid: Delaunay = lower convex hull Via the lift \ell(x, y) = (x, y, x^2 + y^2), a triangle abc of P has its circumcircle empty if and only if the facet \ell(a)\ell(b)\ell(c) belongs to the lower convex hull of the lifted points. The Delaunay is therefore computable by any 3D convex hull algorithm — and its construction is in fact a linear optimization problem.

Proof. Let c be the center and r the radius of the circumcircle of abc. For any point p of the plane, \|p - c\|^2 = r^2 rewrites as x_p^2 + y_p^2 = 2 c \cdot p + (r^2 - \|c\|^2), that is to say: the lifted point \ell(p) is on the plane \Pi : z = 2c_x x + 2c_y y + (r^2 - \|c\|^2) exactly when p is on the circle, above (z \> \Pi(p)) exactly when \|p - c\| \> r (exterior of the disk), below exactly when p is in the open disk. The three lifted vertices of abc are on \Pi; the circumdisk is empty ⟺ no lifted point is under \Pi ⟺ the facet carries the lower hull. ∎

Definition 3.5 — The Bowyer–Watson algorithm Incremental insertion: starting from the Delaunay of a large enclosing triangle, insert p: (1) find the triangle containing it; (2) collect the “cavity” of triangles whose circumcircle contains p (it is connected and star-shaped with respect to p); (3) remove it and retriangulate the holed polygon by the edges from p to the boundary vertices of the cavity. Expected cost O(n \log n) for a random insertion order (each insertion costs O(\log n) on average for locating + small cavity), O(n^2) in the worst case. The test “circumcircle contains p” is done in exact arithmetic by the incircle determinant \mathrm{det}\begin{pmatrix} a_x & a_y & a_x^2 + a_y^2 & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ p_x & p_y & p_x^2 + p_y^2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} — positive sign ⟺ p in the disk (it is the “under the plane” test of Theorem 3.4!).

Constraints and higher dimensions To force given edges (part boundaries, faults), the constrained Delaunay (CDT) reintroduces the required edges then flips without violating the constraints; the standard for finite element meshing (with Ruppert refinement to bound the angles). In dimension d, this whole chapter generalizes (simplices, balls, hulls in dimension d+1): the geometric tomography of chapter 10 exploits these structures in 3D to reconstruct solids from cross-sections — Delaunay simplices “fly to the rescue” of sections.

4. Sampling and reconstruction of surfaces

Definition 4.1 — Sampling a surface: density and spacing For a compact surface S, a set P \subset S is an \varepsilon-sample if every point of S is at distance \< \varepsilon from P, with \varepsilon small compared to the reach (distance to the Medial Axis; it encodes both curvature and thickness). “Poisson-disk” sampling (no pair of points closer than \delta, every point covered within \delta') is the implicit model of scanners; the Lloyd relaxation (code of chapter 2) pushes a cloud toward this quasi-uniform structure.

Proposition 4.2 — Linear approximation error For a surface of bounded curvature K \leq \kappa, locally replacing S by the tangent plane — or by a chord of length \ell — creates an error (sagitta) of at most \dfrac{\ell^2 \kappa}{8}. A sphere of radius 1 sampled with mesh size 0.1 is therefore approximated to within \frac{0.01}{8} = 1.25 \times 10^{-3} — the error decreases quadratically with the mesh size: halving the mesh size divides the error by 4.

Proof. A chord of length \ell of a circle of radius r = 1/\kappa is at distance (sagitta) f = r - \sqrt{r^2 - (\ell/2)^2} from the arc. Expansion for small \ell/r: f = \frac{\ell^2}{8r} + O(\ell^4/r^3). ∎

Definition 4.3 — Alpha-shapes: filtering the Delaunay by distance The alpha-complex \mathcal{C}_\alpha of P is the subcomplex of the Delaunay formed by the simplices whose circumcircle (empty portion) has radius \leq \alpha; the alpha-shape is its surface. As \alpha grows from 0 to infinity, the family (\mathcal{C}_\alpha)_\alpha forms a filtration — each complex contained in the next — which gradually grows the details of the cloud: this is the geometric version of a multi-scale analysis (and the key ingredient of persistence, chapter 9).

Definition 4.4 — Marching cubes: polygonizing an implicit field To extract the isosurface f(x) = c of a field sampled on a grid (SDF, density potential, sign field), the marching cubes algorithm (Lorensen & Cline, 1987) walks over the cells: the 8 corners classified “inside/outside” give up to 256 configurations (reduced to 15 by symmetries); the edge/isosurface intersections are linearly interpolated (t = (f_0 - c)/(f_0 - f_1)); the triangles produced stitch a closed surface. It is the natural interface between implicit representations (Def. 1.1) and meshes (Def. 1.2) — and the historical tool of medical imaging.

Example 4.5 — A sphere in marching cubes, numbers SDF f(x) = \\|x\\| - 1 on [-1.2\,;\,1.2]^3, mesh size h: the polygonal surface is at distance \leq h/4 or so from the sphere (linear interpolation of a quadratic field: error O(h^2) on f, divided by the gradient \approx 1). For h = 0.1: 24³ ≈ 13,800 cells, 5,026 cells crossed, ≈ 3,100 triangles, measured error 0.006 (before smoothing); h = 0.05 ⟹ 12,800 triangles, error 0.0015 — a ratio of 4 consistent with Proposition 4.2. The normals obtained from the gradient \nabla f (centered differences) are of “rendering” quality without any curvature computation.

Reconstruction: the global viewpoint The Crust of Amenta–Bern (1999) proves: a sufficiently fine \varepsilon-sample of the surface S + Voronoi/Delaunay = a reconstruction homeomorphic to S (topological guarantee); the Poisson surface reconstruction (Kazhdan et al., 2006) changes paradigm: fit an “inside/outside” indicator function \chi whose gradient matches the oriented normals \nabla\chi \approx \mathbf{n}\,\delta_S — a Poisson problem \Delta \chi = \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{n}\,\delta_S) solved in multi-resolution; robust to holes and non-uniform data. Recent approaches learn SDFs (deep learning on meshes — cf. Multimodal GenAI module, 3D chapter), but the evaluation is always done by marching cubes: the tool has not aged a bit.

Definition 4.6 — Rigid registration: the ICP algorithm Before reconstructing, one often has to align several acquisitions of the same object: this is rigid registration — seeking R \in SO(3) and t applied to the cloud P to fit it onto Q. The ICP algorithm (iterative closest point, Besl–McKay, 1992) alternates:

  1. match: each point of P to its nearest neighbor in Q (kd-tree);
  2. align: the optimal rigid transform in the least-squares sense on these pairs is solved exactly by singular value decomposition of the cross-covariance matrix (the Kabsch–Umeyama solution, implemented by the Computer Vision module);
  3. iterate until the residual converges.

Each step can only decrease the error, so the algorithm converges — but to a local minimum: initialization matters (crude or geodesic transform), and robust variants reject outlier pairs (quantile distance), weight by normals or minimize the point-to-plane error. It is the universal tool of 3D scanning and cloud fusion — rigid only: non-rigid correspondence waits for chapter 8.

5. Smoothing: from uniform to cotangent Laplacian

Definition 5.1 — Combinatorial Laplacian and smoothing scheme On a closed mesh, the uniform (combinatorial) Laplacian of a vertex i with neighborhood \mathcal{N}(i) is \delta_i = \dfrac{1}{\|\mathcal{N}(i)\|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} p_j - p_i, and Laplacian smoothing iterates p_i \leftarrow p_i + \lambda\,\delta_i, \qquad \lambda \in (0, 1]. This is a local projection method; “mean curvature flow” version: p_i \leftarrow p_i + \lambda\,H_i \mathbf{n}_i (chapter 7 for H_i). In matrix form: \mathbf{x} \leftarrow (I - \lambda L)\,\mathbf{x} with L = D^{-1}A - I (normalized) or L = D - A (non-normalized).

Theorem 5.2 — Uniform smoothing shrinks: 1D spectral proof On a ring of n vertices (cycle) with positions x_i, the iteration x_i \leftarrow (1-\lambda)x_i + \lambda \bar{x}_{\mathcal{N}(i)} preserves the mean \bar{x} and makes every non-constant component decrease: x^{(k)} \to \bar{x}\mathbf{1} — the 1D mesh shrinks down to the barycenter. The decay rate of a mode of frequency j is \rho_j = 1 - \lambda\big(1 - \cos\tfrac{2\pi j}{n}\big) \in [0, 1).

Proof. Decompose x on the orthogonal basis of discrete Fourier modes u^{(j)}_i = e^{2i\pi ji/n}. The average of neighbors multiplies u^{(j)} by \frac{1}{2}(e^{2i\pi j/n} + e^{-2i\pi j/n}) = \cos\frac{2\pi j}{n}; hence the iteration multiplies mode j by (1-\lambda) + \lambda\cos\frac{2\pi j}{n}. For j = 0 (constant): factor 1 — conservation of the mean. For j \neq 0: \cos\frac{2\pi j}{n} \< 1 gives \rho_j \< 1 (and \geq -1 for \lambda \leq 1): all non-constant modes decay geometrically, high frequencies fastest. Limit = constant component = barycenter. ∎

Definition 5.3 — Remedies: Taubin λ|μ, cotangents

  • Taubin λ|μ: alternate a smoothing step (\lambda \approx 0.5) and an “unsmoothing” step (\mu \approx -0.53); the frequencies in the pass band (\rho_\mu, \rho_\lambda) are preserved — the mean volume is conserved instead of collapsing;
  • Cotangent (geometric) Laplacian: \delta_i = \frac{1}{2 A_i}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \big(\cot \alpha_{ij} + \cot \beta_{ij}\big)\,(p_j - p_i), where \alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij} are the angles opposite the edge (i,j) and A_i the mixed Voronoi area of the vertex — this is the operator converging to the Laplace–Beltrami operator (theory of hat function forms; cf. Geometric Deep Learning module, operators chapter);
  • Filtering order: smoothing in position regularizes geometry; smoothing the normals then re-projecting (or shrink-and-grow) separates denoising from deformation — the two families coexist in DCCs.

Example 5.4 — Cotangents on an isosceles right triangle Single triangle A = (0,0), B = (1,0), C = (0,1), vertex A. Its two adjacent edges are (A,B) and (A,C): for edge (A,B), the opposite angle is \angle ACB = 90°, of zero cotangent; for edge (A,C), the opposite \angle ABC = 90°, zero cotangent as well. Edge (B,C) is not adjacent to A: the sum of cotangents around A is zero, \delta_A = 0 — consistent, a mesh reduced to a single triangle is “all boundary”. The real implementation test is the planar test: embed a planar mesh in its plane ⟹ all interior \delta_i are zero (discrete equivalent of \Delta f = 0 for harmonic functions). The proof comes from the discrete Green formula: \sum_i A_i\, f_i\, \delta_i = -\sum_{(i,j)} w_{ij}(f_j - f_i)^2 \leq 0, with equality if and only if f is constant on each component.

From smoothing to geometric deep learning The cotangent matrix is the central object of the M2 Geometric Deep Learning module: diffusion of descriptors (HKS), spectral convolutions (eigenfunction bases, chapter 8 below), DiffusionNet. The smoothing of this chapter is its “deterministic” version: same operator, without learned data — and already non-trivial: choice of area/mixed Voronoi, negative weights on obtuse meshes (to be truncated or regularized).

6. Simplification and remeshing

Definition 6.1 — Quadric Error Metrics (QEM) To each vertex v one associates the set \mathcal{P}(v) of the planes of its supported faces; its error quadric is E(v) = \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}(v)} \big(\mathbf{n}_\pi \cdot v - d_\pi\big)^2 = v^\top\!A v + 2\,b^\top v + c, \qquad A = \sum_{\pi} \mathbf{n}_\pi \mathbf{n}_\pi^\top,\ \ b = -\sum_\pi d_\pi \mathbf{n}_\pi,\ \ c = \sum_\pi d_\pi^2. Edge contraction (u, v) \to \bar{v}: choose \bar{v} minimizing E_{u \oplus v} (the sum quadric), then merge. The order of contractions follows increasing cost (priority queue): this is the Garland–Heckbert algorithm (1997), still the standard for game LODs.

Theorem 6.2 — Additivity of quadrics and algebraic optimum (i) E_{u \oplus v}(x) = E_u(x) + E_v(x) for every x — the quadrics add coefficient by coefficient (A, b, c) = (A_u + A_v,\ b_u + b_v,\ c_u + c_v). (ii) If A = A_u + A_v is invertible, the minimal cost of the contraction is attained at \bar{v} = -A^{-1}b and equals c - b^\top A^{-1} b.

Proof. (i) Each quadric is a sum of squares over sets of planes; merging the vertices merges the sets of planes (the incident faces become incident to the merged vertex), and the sum of sums is the sum over the union — hence the term-by-term additivity of the quadratic forms. (ii) E(x) = x^\top A x + 2b^\top x + c, A symmetric positive definite: \nabla E = 2(Ax + b) = 0 gives x^\ast = -A^{-1}b, and E(x^\ast) = c - b^\top A^{-1}b (direct substitution). ∎

Example 6.3 — Two contractions with numbers

  • 1D straight boundary: two vertices of a straight edge carry the planes x = 0 and x = 2 (unit normal (1,0,0), offsets 0 and 2); E(x) = x^2 + (x-2)^2 = 2(x-1)^2 + 2, minimal at \bar{v} = (1,0,0) at cost 2 — the contraction “glues” the two planes halfway;
  • Cube corner: planes x=0, y=0, z=0 (unit normals). A = I_3, b = 0, c = 0 ⟹ \bar{v} = (0,0,0), cost 0: contracting the three faces of a cube corner toward the exact corner costs nothing — this is the strength of QEM: detecting exact algebraic folds.

Definition 6.4 — Isotropic remeshing Transforming a mesh into a quasi-uniform mesh (edge lengths in [l_{\min}, l_{\max}], roughly equilateral triangles) by the loop: (1) split of overly long edges (projected midpoint); (2) collapse of overly short ones; (3) edge flip restoring valence 6 (interior) / 4 (boundary); (4) tangential smoothing (chapter 5, projected onto the tangent plane so as not to shrink the shape). Result: a mesh suited to the simulations of the Computer Animation module and to the convolutions of the Geometric Deep Learning module. Parameterization (chart atlas) is the alternative: cut into topological disks, flatten (Tutte/harmonic, ARAP), remesh in the parameter domain — higher quality, more delicate handling of cuts.

Errors on a budget In production, simplification is both judge and jury: the QEM quadric optimizes an algebraic error (distance to planes), not the Hausdorff distance to the original surface — boundaries and fine details require locks (preserved edges/attributes) and weighted boundary planes; for attributes (color, normals), one stacks quadrics of “planes” in higher dimension. Rule of thumb: a 10:1 reduction ratio is almost invisible on screen on dense meshes — beyond that, steer by curvature fields (chapter 7) to decide where to keep the triangles.

Definition 6.5 — Surface subdivision Subdivision is the reverse path of simplification: start from a coarse control mesh and refine it iteratively by local rules, up to a smooth limit surface. The Loop scheme (1987, triangular) cuts each triangle into four through the edge midpoints: each new edge vertex takes \tfrac38(v_i + v_j) + \tfrac18(v_a + v_b) (endpoints and the two opposite neighbors), each old vertex slides toward (1 - n\beta)\,v + \beta\sum_{\text{neighbors}} v_i with \beta depending on the valence n. In the limit, the surface is a quartic B-spline C^2 everywhere, except at extraordinary vertices (valence \neq 6) where it remains C^1. The Catmull–Clark scheme (1978) is its quadrilateral analogue (bicubic B-spline; extraordinary vertices of valence \neq 4) — the standard of animated film, which the Computer Animation module meets on the rendering side. Theory: these rules extend to surfaces the de Casteljau refinement of curves (def. 1.6); subdivision and simplification thus bracket the level of detail of a single shape, from the control mesh to the rendering mesh.

7. Discrete curvature and Gauss–Bonnet

Definition 7.1 — Curvatures in the continuous setting, to set the targets At a point of a smooth surface, the principal directions have extreme normal curvatures \kappa_1, \kappa_2; Gaussian curvature K = \kappa_1 \kappa_2 (product); mean curvature H = \tfrac{\kappa_1 + \kappa_2}{2} (average); the shape operator satisfies H = \tfrac12 \mathrm{div}\, \mathbf{n} — hence the link with the Laplacian of chapter 5: \Delta x = -2H\mathbf{n}. K is intrinsic (measurable “from within the surface”); H depends on the embedding — this distinction structures all of shape analysis.

Definition 7.2 — Angle deficit At vertex i of a triangulated mesh, the angle deficit is \mathcal{K}_i = 2\pi - \sum_{j}\,\theta_{ij}, the sum of the angles of the faces incident at i. It is the natural discretization of the Gaussian curvature integrated over the neighborhood: positive at tips (cone), zero on developable zones, negative at passes (saddle). The discrete mean curvature is obtained by the discrete Steiner theorem: \mathbf{H}_i = \frac{1}{2 A_i} \sum_j (\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij})(p_j - p_i) — the same cotangent matrix as in chapter 5: geometry and analysis are one.

Theorem 7.3 — Discrete Gauss–Bonnet For a closed mesh of genus g: \sum_{i \in V} \mathcal{K}_i = 2\pi\,\chi = 2\pi\,(2 - 2g), where \chi = V - E + F is the Euler characteristic (chapter 9). The total curvature depends only on the topology — not on the geometry.

Proof. Counting. The double sum of angles runs over all the angles of all the faces: each triangle contributes \pi, so \sum_i \sum_j \theta_{ij} = \pi F and \sum_i \mathcal{K}_i = 2\pi V - \pi F. Each face has 3 edges and each edge carries 2 faces: 3F = 2E, so 2\pi E = 3\pi F, hence -2\pi E + 2\pi F = -3\pi F + 2\pi F = -\pi F \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\pi(V - E + F) = 2\pi V - \pi F = \sum_i \mathcal{K}_i. Verification on the cube (Example 7.4 below): 8 vertices of deficit \tfrac{\pi}{2} give 4\pi = 2\pi \cdot 2, consistent with \chi = V - E + F = 8 - 12 + 6 = 2. ∎

Example 7.4 — Regular tetrahedron, and subdivisions Regular tetrahedron: each vertex carries 3 angles of 60° ⟹ \mathcal{K} = 2\pi - \pi = \pi, total 4\pi = that of the cube — same topological sphere, very different local curvatures (“pointy” vertices of the tetrahedron), same total. Subdividing each face of the cube into 4 triangles (mid-edges): the new edge vertices are flat (zero deficit: 2\pi of angles around them), the 8 corners keep the same deficit \tfrac{\pi}{2} — the sum is unchanged: the theorem is invariant under subdivision, an indispensable condition for “discrete curvature” to converge to the continuous curvature as the mesh is refined.

Estimating in practice Deficits give an integrated curvature; for a pointwise curvature, divide by the mixed Voronoi area A_i: K_i \approx \mathcal{K}_i / A_i, with the same areas as in chapter 5. For noisy meshes, smooth first (chapter 5) — curvature is a second derivative, twice as sensitive to noise as positions. These descriptors (K_i, H_i, principal directions) directly feed: adaptive remeshing (chapter 6), the descriptors of Geometric Deep Learning, and direction fields for quadrangulation.

8. The spectrum of shapes

Definition 8.1 — Laplace–Beltrami and spectrum On a compact boundaryless surface S, the Laplace–Beltrami operator \Delta_S (surface Laplacian) is self-adjoint, negative, with compact resolvent: its spectrum is a sequence 0 = \lambda_0 \< \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \to \infty, with orthonormal eigenfunctions \phi_0, \phi_1, \dots (a basis of L^2(S)). The spectrum \{\lambda_k\} (or its sampling (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N), “ShapeDNA”) is an isometric invariant: two isometric surfaces have the same spectrum. In practice: discretize by the generalized cotangent matrix L = A^{-1}W (chapter 5) and solve the generalized eigenvalue problem W\phi = \lambda A \phi, with A the diagonal matrix of mixed Voronoi areas — the same objects as in chapters 5 and 7.

Example 8.2 — Exact spectra: path graph and cycle For the combinatorial Laplacian of a path with n vertices (chain): \lambda_k = 2 - 2\cos\frac{k\pi}{n+1}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, n, and for the cycle with n vertices (closed ring): \mu_k = 2 - 2\cos\frac{2\pi k}{n}. For n = 10 path: \lambda_1 = 2 - 2\cos(\pi/11) \approx 0.081, \lambda_{10} \approx 3.919; cycle: \mu_1 \approx 0.382, \mu_5 = 4 (alternation ±1, the highest possible frequency). Geometric reading: closing the chain “tightens” the lowest frequency (\lambda_1 multiplied by ~4.7): the smallest eigenvalue measures the connectedness / tightness of the shape — it is what discriminates components and pinch points (the applications of spectral segmentation).

Proposition 8.3 — Heat kernel and spectral signatures The solution of the heat equation \partial_t u = -\Delta_S u, u(0) = f, writes u(t) = e^{-t\Delta_S}f = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t} \langle f, \phi_k\rangle \phi_k and the heat kernel h_t(x, y) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y) is an isometric invariant. Pointwise signatures (HKS: h_t(x, x) for a few t; WKS: oscillation) describe each point by “its” place in the spectrum — the basis of non-rigid shape correspondence, detailed in the M2 Geometric Deep Learning module (signatures chapter). The converse “spectrum ⟹ shape” is false: there exist isospectral non-isometric surfaces (“you cannot hear the shape of a drum”, Gordon–Webb–Wolpert, 1992).

Interpretations and uses The eigenvalue \lambda_k is the square of a spatial frequency: small k = large undulations (silhouette), large k = details. Direct applications: shape compression (projecting onto the \phi_k of small k), correspondence (coordination of eigenbases), retrieval (vector (\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \dots, \sqrt{\lambda_N}) renormalized by scale), segmentation (cutting along the level lines of \phi_2, the “Fiedler function”). Numerical caution: only the first ~\sqrt{N} eigenvalues of a discretization with N vertices are reliable — beyond that, one describes the mesh, not the surface (same phenomenon as numerical dispersion in finite elements).

Definition 8.4 — Shape recognition, non-rigid matching and correspondence Recognition: measuring the similarity of two shapes S, T. The spectral signatures (“ShapeDNA” spectrum of definition 8.1, heat kernel of proposition 8.3) give fast retrieval; the Gromov–Hausdorff distance — comparing the intrinsic geodesic distance matrices — compares without a common embedding. Non-rigid matching: the two shapes are two poses of a single articulated object (a sitting or standing character) — related by a quasi-isometry T that preserves geodesic distances up to a factor \varepsilon; the intrinsic descriptors (HKS, WKS) are then stable and match point to point. Correspondence: the map x \mapsto T(x) itself. The functional maps approach (Ovsjanikov et al., 2012) represents it by the transport operator of functions on the surface, which becomes diagonal in the eigenbases of the two Laplacians: a small k \times k matrix to estimate — constrained by the HKS/WKS pairs and the preservation of signals — before conversion into a point-to-point map. It is the official entry gate of the “non-rigid matching and correspondence” of the syllabus, extended in M2 by Analysis and Deep Learning on Geometric Data.

9. Combinatorial topology: Euler, Betti, persistence

Theorem 9.1 — Euler–Poincaré formula For every connected closed mesh of genus g: \chi = V - E + F = 2 - 2g, and more generally for an arbitrary complex \chi = \sum_{k} (-1)^k c_k (c_k = number of cells of dimension k) = \sum_k (-1)^k b_k (Betti numbers). Proof sketch for a sphere: start from the tetrahedron (\chi = 2, Example 1.4) and note that subdividing a face (adding a vertex on an edge or at the center of a face) does not change \chi (an edge split: +1 vertex, +1 edge; a face split: +1 vertex, +3 edges, +2 faces — \Delta\chi = 0 in both cases); any triangulation of the sphere is obtained from the tetrahedron by these operations. Adding a “handle” subtracts 2 from \chi — hence 2 - 2g. ∎

Definition 9.2 — Homology and Betti numbers On a complex K, the homology groups H_k(K) (with coefficients in \mathbb{Z}_2, to simplify) are the classes of cycles modulo boundaries: H_k = \ker \partial_k / \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{k+1}, with \partial_k \partial_{k+1} = 0 (the boundary of a boundary is zero). The Betti numbers b_k = \dim H_k count the independent “holes”: for a closed surface: b_0 connected components, b_1 = 2g handles, b_2 = 1 if orientable (the volume itself). Examples: sphere (1, 0, 1), torus (1, 2, 1), double torus (1, 4, 1); check: \chi = b_0 - b_1 + b_2 ✓. Homology is computed by Gaussian elimination on the boundary matrices (Smith normal form): purely combinatorial algorithms, polynomial cost.

Definition 9.3 — Persistent homology Let a filtration K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \dots \subseteq K_m (e.g.: alpha-shapes (\mathcal{C}_\alpha)_\alpha of chapter 4, or sublevel sets of a function on the mesh). At each step, cycles are born and die; the persistence of a cycle is \mathrm{pers} = \alpha_{death} - \alpha_{birth}. The diagram/barcode (one bar [\alpha_{birth}, \alpha_{death}] per class) separates structure (long bars) from noise (short bars); the stability theorem guarantees that the barcode changes little when the data change little — this is what makes TDA usable on real data. Standard implementation: column reduction algorithm (boundary matrix of the filtration, Gaussian pivot), in O(m^\omega) worst case, quasi-linear in practice.

Example 9.4 — A noisy ring, read by persistence 1,000 points sampling a ring (disk of radius 2 with a hole of radius 1) + Gaussian noise σ = 0.1. Filtration by alpha-shapes: H_0 bars: 999 short ones (merging clusters, all \alpha \lesssim 0.2) + 1 infinite (the component); H_1 bars: one long bar [0.25\,;\,2.0] — the central hole — plus a few bars of length \< 0.2 (small noise holes plugged early). With σ = 0.4, the central bar survives [0.4\,;\,1.7]: persistence, unlike counting at fixed \alpha, is robust to noise. It is the most widely used topological descriptor for learning (vectorizations of persistence diagrams, persistence kernels — cf. the team’s work, chapter 10).

Topology and learning The invariants of this chapter serve as structural regularizers in learning: persistence loss to reconstruct with the right number of components/holes, genus constraints in mesh generation (the Multimodal GenAI module, 3D chapter, returns to this); the Gauss–Bonnet theorem (chapter 7) is the “curvature” case of the same idea: global quantities that constrain local solutions.

  • Discrete Exterior Calculus (with A. Hirani, M. Leok, J. Marsden, 2005) — arXiv math/0508341: a theory of differential forms on simplicial complexes that preserves the identities of the continuum exactly (Stokes, Hodge): the cotangent Laplacians of chapter 5 and the curvatures of chapter 7 are instances of it;

  • The course Digital Geometry Processing with Discrete Exterior Calculus (K. Crane, F. de Goes, M. Desbrun, P. Schröder, SIGGRAPH Asia 2013) — ACM DL / open DDG notes: the reference for the labs (libigl), from the Laplacian to geometric flows.

Pooran Memari — topology and reconstruction of shapes CNRS senior researcher at LIX (École Polytechnique), P. Memari works at the interface of algorithmic geometry / topology:

  • Geometric Tomography with Topological Guarantees (with O. Amini, J.-D. Boissonnat, Discrete & Computational Geometry, 2013) — arXiv / Springer: reconstructing a 3D solid from 2D cross-sections (serial microscopy); the analysis shows why naive reconstructions fail (two solids of different topologies can have the same cross-sections) and gives guarantees — exactly the “sample, reconstruct, guarantee” program of chapter 4;
  • Her HDR thesis, “Points, Patterns and Shapes: towards accessible geometric modeling” (2024), synthesizes this program: point patterns (sampling, chapter 4), proximity structures (Voronoi/Delaunay, chapters 2–3), accessibility of modeling tools — page: LIX;

Common thread The two research programs meet on one principle: a discretization must preserve the structure — differential identities on one side (Desbrun: exact discrete Stokes), topological invariants on the other (Memari: homeomorphism-type guarantees). This is the ridge line of the whole module: represent faithfully before analyzing, then learn — the gateway to Geometric Deep Learning (M2).

For the sites p_1 = (0,0), p_2 = (4,0), p_3 = (0,3) of Example 2.4: (a) verify that (2, 1.5) is at distance 2.5 from the three sites; (b) draw the three perpendicular bisectors and the unbounded cell of p_2; (c) add p_4 = (4,3) — why are the four points cocircular, and which Delaunay triangulations does one obtain (lift the degeneracy by perturbation)?
  2. Empty circle criterion. In a convex quadrilateral, \angle abc = 70° and \angle adc = 125° (diagonal ac). (a) Decide whether a flip is needed (Lemma 3.2). (b) After the flip, what is at least the smallest of the angles opposite the new diagonal? (c) Prove point (ii) of the proof: b \in \mathrm{disk}(a,c,d) \iff d \in \mathrm{disk}(a,b,c) for a convex quadrilateral.
  3. Guided lift and incircle. (a) Implement the test of Theorem 3.4: for each triangle of Delaunay (scipy), construct the plane \Pi and verify that no lifted point is under \Pi (tolerance 10^{-12}). (b) Compare with the incircle determinant (signs: interior/exterior). (c) Perturb a point toward the circumcircle of a neighboring triangle: observe the flip in the computed triangulation.
  4. Lloyd. (a) Show that Lloyd relaxation decreases the quantization energy \mathcal{E} = \sum_i \int_{\mathcal{V}(p_i)} \\|x - p_i\\|^2\,dx (each iteration is an alternation: optimal cells for the sites, then optimal centroids for the cells). (b) Implement and plot \mathcal{E} (approximated by sampling) as a function of iterations on 100 points of the square.
  5. Guided shrinking. (a) Complete the proof of Theorem 5.2: why does mode j decay exactly like \rho_j^k? What happens for \lambda \> 1? (b) Simulate a ring of radius 1, n = 50, \lambda = 0.5; plot the mean radius — by how much does it shrink in 100 iterations? (c) Apply Taubin \lambda = 0.5, \mu = -0.53 and measure.
  6. QEM. (a) Recover Example 6.3 (cube corner) in detail: write A, b, c, verify A = I.   7. Gauss–Bonnet. (a) Verify Theorem 7.3 on the tetrahedron and the cube (by hand), then on the minimal 3 \times 3 torus (9 vertices, 27 edges, 18 faces — \chi = 0).   8. Spectra. (a) Prove the formula of Example 8.2 for the path (verify that u^{(k)}_i = \sin\frac{k\pi i}{n+1} is an eigenvector).
