0. Recap: the speech signal and its representations

Prerequisites (Signal Processing M1 module) — what is assumed known

  • Sampling (ch. 4): Shannon’s theorem; speech is sampled at 16 kHz (ASR) or 24 kHz (synthesis), useful telephone band 300-3400 Hz, formants up to ~8 kHz;
  • Time-frequency analysis (ch. 6): STFT, Gabor windows, time-frequency trade-off — the spectrogram is the input of almost all the models in this module (cf. Signal Processing module ch. 6);
  • Mel spectrogram and MFCC (ch. 8): 25 ms frames every 10 ms, Hann window, Mel filter bank (perceptual scale linear below 1 kHz then logarithmic: m = 2595\log_{10}(1 + f/700)), 20-80 coefficients, possibly DCT (MFCC);
  • Random signals (ch. 7): power spectral density, periodogram — used by energy-based VADs (ch. 10) and spread-spectrum watermarking (ch. 9);
  • Recurrent networks and transformers (Deep Learning M1): LSTM, attention \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})V — the ASR/TTS encoder is a transformer (ch. 3-4);
  • Diffusion and VAE (Multimodal GenAI M1 ch. 1 and 3): ELBO, DDPM, noise prediction — the generative TTS models (ch. 7) descend directly from them.

Definition 0.1 — ASR and TTS, formalized Recognition (ASR): estimate the transcription y (sequence of tokens — characters, phonemes, BPE) of an acoustic frame x_{1:T} (Mel features, typically T \approx 100 frames for 10 s of speech): model p_\theta(y \mid x_{1:T}). Synthesis (TTS): the inverse, p_\theta(x \mid y) — a one-to-many problem: a text can be said in infinitely many ways (voice, rate, emotion), which will structure all of ch. 7-8 (TTS is a generation problem, ASR a decision problem). The reference ASR error is the WER: \mathrm{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}, substitutions/deletions/insertions obtained by word-level Levenshtein alignment between hypothesis and reference (lab 1).

The module’s line — and why “conversational” The course follows the stack of a voice assistant: understand (ch. 1-5), speak (ch. 6-9), converse (ch. 10-11). The technical thread: two breaks occurred — (i) the abandonment of explicit alignment (HMM → CTC → attention → discrete tokens); (ii) the fusion of the three ASR+LLM+TTS building blocks into a single multimodal model (speech LLMs, ch. 11) that speaks and listens — where full-duplex conversation (ch. 10) becomes computable. Speech regains its status there: the fastest (3-4 words/s produced, 5-8 understood) and most natural interface.

Module status Course proposed in the M2 brochure (period 2): the instructors are not yet confirmed; there is therefore no “research of the instructor” chapter — the content follows the standards of the field and constantly builds on the modules already covered (Signal Processing M1 for the signal, LLMs M2 for the language layer).

1. Classical ASR: the GMM-HMM pipeline

Concrete scene: a business ASR fine-tuned on call-center audio drops as soon as the accent changes. This chapter sets signal representation and decoding objectives.

What breaks Ignoring acoustic domain; evaluating WER alone (no semantics); voice cloning without ethical guardrails / watermarking.

The historical pipeline (1980-2015) survives only as technical memories — but its formalism (emission model, chain of states, Bayesian decoding) reappears everywhere, up to the CTC forward-backward (ch. 2). We cover it in one structured chapter.

Theorem 1.1 — The GMM-HMM acoustic model and decoding One decomposes p(x_{1:T} \mid w) with a hidden Markov chain over phonetic states q_t: each phoneme is a 3-state left-to-right HMM (beginning/middle/end), put in context (triphones); the emission of a state is a Gaussian mixture over the MFCCs (39 dimensions: 13 + first and second derivatives): p(x_t \mid q_t = s) = \sum_{m=1}^{M_s} c_{sm}\, \mathcal N(x_t;\ \mu_{sm}, \Sigma_{sm}), and the retained transcription maximizes Bayesian decoding \hat w = \arg\max_w\ p(x_{1:T} \mid w)\, p_{\mathrm{LM}}(w), where p_{\mathrm{LM}} is an n-gram language model (cf. NLP M1 module ch. 2). The likelihood is computed by the forward recursion \alpha_t(j) = \Big[\sum_i \alpha_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\Big]\, b_j(x_t), (\mathcal O(T N^2), N states) and the best path by Viterbi (same recursion, max instead of the sum). Training: EM (Baum-Welch) — cf. Graph ML M1 module ch. 5 for the EM of latent-variable models.

Proposition 1.2 — Why GMM? And why is it dead? (i) A Gaussian mixture is a universal density approximator (with enough components, any continuous density is approximated): it was the best parameterization before deep learning. (ii) Fatal flaws: MFCCs are not semantic features (the GMM models acoustic blocks, knows nothing about similar phones); the HMM assumes the signal locally stationary per state and conditional independence of the frames; above all, each piece (lexicon, contexts, LM) is optimized separately — impossible to align globally. Neural systems eat raw data: 10,000 labeled hours replace 300 carefully curated ones (ch. 4).

What survives of the pipeline Three direct legacies: (i) the acoustic + LM fusion at decoding (the “shallow fusion” reappears with LLMs to rescore N-bests); (ii) the forward-backward — the central algorithm of CTC in the next chapter; (iii) forced alignment (finding the instants of each word, still used for subtitling and training TTS). Historical reference: Rabiner’s tutorial (Proc. IEEE, 1989) remains a limpid read.

2. CTC: the loss, derived

The central problem of neural ASR: the output (sequence of tokens y) is shorter than the input (T frames) and unaligned — there is no label per frame. The solution of Graves et al. (ICML 2006): marginalize over all possible alignments. We derive everything: this is technical chapter no. 1 of the module.

Definition 2.1 — Alignments and collapse The network emits at each frame a distribution p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{1:T}, t) over the extended alphabet \mathcal V \cup \{\epsilon\} (\epsilon = blank). A path \pi \in (\mathcal V \cup \{\epsilon\})^T is a sequence of T symbols, drawn independently: p(\pi \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(\pi_t \mid x, t). The collapse map \mathcal B first removes the blanks then merges consecutive repetitions: \mathcal B(\texttt{hh ee ll ll o}) = \texttt{helo} (the three consecutive l’s, once the blanks are removed, make only one). Conversely, doubles separated by a blank survive: \mathcal B(\texttt{l}\,\epsilon\,\texttt{l}) = \texttt{ll} — that is the whole art of CTC. The CTC loss marginalizes: p_\theta(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal B^{-1}(y)} p(\pi \mid x), \qquad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{CTC}} = -\log p_\theta(y \mid x). Why the blank is indispensable: without it, a collapsed \texttt{ll} could not be distinguished from \texttt{l} — impossible to write “bonne” with a double n. The blank carries the boundary information.

Example 2.2 — Counting by hand (T = 4, y = “ab”) Extended sequence \ell = (\epsilon, a, \epsilon, b) (length S = 4). The valid paths of length 4 are: aa\epsilon b? No — let us enumerate seriously: \texttt{-a-b}, \texttt{-ab-}, \texttt{a--b}, \texttt{a-b-}, \texttt{aa-b}, \texttt{a-ab}, \texttt{-aab}, \texttt{ab--} (8 paths). Each contributes its product of 4 probabilities: p(y\|x) is the sum of the 8; exercise 3 computes it for numerical values, exercise 4 checks with the implementation. The number of paths grows exponentially (\sim \|\mathcal V\|^T): direct summation is impossible — hence the forward-backward.

Theorem 2.3 — CTC forward-backward (recursions and gradient, proven) Let p_t(k) = p_\theta(k \mid x, t), and \ell the extended sequence. The forward variables \alpha_t(s) = p(\ell_{1:s} \text{ reached at frame } t) follow \alpha_t(s) = \Big[\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s{-}1) + \mathbf 1\{\ell_s \neq \epsilon,\ \ell_s \neq \ell_{s-2}\}\, \alpha_{t-1}(s{-}2)\Big]\cdot p_t(\ell_s), initialized \alpha_1(1) = p_1(\epsilon), \alpha_1(2) = p_1(\ell_2), the rest at 0 (one cannot “jump” ahead). Symmetrically \beta_t(s) (backward, from the end). Then p(y \mid x) = \alpha_T(S) + \alpha_T(S{-}1), \qquad \alpha_t(s)\,\beta_t(s) = p\big(y \mid x,\ \pi_t = \ell_s\big)\cdot p_t(\ell_s), and the gradient of the loss is written in closed form \frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\mathrm{CTC}}}{\partial p_t(k)} = -\,\frac{1}{p(y \mid x)}\ \frac{1}{p_t(k)} \sum_{s\,:\,\ell_s = k} \alpha_t(s)\, \beta_t(s).

Proof. Step 1 (decomposition by the state at t). The sum over paths partitions according to the position s occupied at frame t: each path passing through \ell_s at t factorizes into “prefix reaching \ell_s at t” \times “p_t(\ell_s)” \times “suffix from \ell_s at t{+}1” — hence \alpha_t(s)\beta_t(s) = p(y, \pi_t = \ell_s \mid x) (careful: the definition of \beta includes the p_t factor or not depending on the convention; with the one above, \alpha_t(s) contains p_t(\ell_s) and \beta_t(s) does not). Step 2 (recursion). For the path to be at \ell_s at t, it was at s, s{-}1 or s{-}2 at t{-}1; the jump of two positions is forbidden except if \ell_s is a real letter different from \ell_{s-2} (otherwise two identical letters would be merged by the collapse; and one never jumps over a letter). Step 3 (gradient). p(y\|x) = \sum_{\pi} \prod_{t'} p_{t'}(\pi_{t'}); each term using p_t(k) corresponds to the paths with \pi_t = k \in \ell, whose total sum equals \sum_{s:\ell_s = k}\alpha_t(s)\beta_t(s). Differentiating the log: \partial \mathcal L/\partial p_t(k) = -\partial p(y\|x)/\partial p_t(k) \cdot 1/p(y\|x), and \partial p(y\|x)/\partial p_t(k) = \big[\sum_s \alpha\beta\big] / p_t(k) (each path where \pi_t = k is linear in p_t(k)). Complexity: \mathcal O(T \cdot S) — two tables of T \times (2\|y\|+1), versus the exponential enumeration. ∎

Proposition 2.4 — CTC inference and model assumptions (i) Greedy decoding: \hat y = \mathcal B(\arg\max_k p_t(k)\ \text{for each } t) — fast, suboptimal; beam search: maintain K prefixes with their truncated \alpha scores, merge equal prefixes after collapse, optionally rescored by an LM. (ii) Assumptions of CTC: conditional independence of the frames (no internal language model — hence the external LM) and monotone distribution (the alignment goes left to right): perfect for reading, forbidden for translation (ch. 3 to break free of it).

CTC’s posterity CTC lives everywhere in 2026: forced alignment (lab 1), the pre-training of wav2vec 2 (CTC objective), and — above all — RNN-T (ch. 3) is a CTC extended to real time, with a blank reinterpreted as “move forward in time”. By contrast, Whisper is not CTC (attention encoder-decoder, ch. 4): knowing how to say “this one marginalizes alignments, that one generates them” is a classic oral-exam question.

3. Encoder-decoder: LAS, Conformer, RNN-T

Theorem 3.1 — LAS: Listen, Attend and Spell (Chan et al., ICASSP 2016) Listen: a pyramidal BLSTM (then transformer) encoder compacts the frames x_{1:T} into states h_j. Attend: at each output step i, an additive attention computes e_{ij} = v^\top \tanh\big(W_s s_{i-1} + W_h h_j\big), \qquad \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp e_{ij}}{\sum_{j'} \exp e_{ij'}}, \qquad c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}\, h_j, (location-sensitive attention in practice: the past weights a_{i-1} enter e_{ij} to make it monotone and avoid skipped/repeated words). Spell: an autoregressive decoder emits y_i from (s_{i-1}, y_{i-1}, c_i), trained by teacher forcing and cross-entropy. The loss is no longer a marginalization: the attention learns the alignment — and can reorder it (used for speech translation). Two inherited weaknesses: exposure bias (trained on the reference, tested on its own errors) and the impossibility of strict streaming (all of x is read before the first token).

Proposition 3.2 — Conformer: local convolution + global attention (Gulati et al., Interspeech 2020) The Conformer block chains (“macaron” style) two feed-forward networks with a 1/2 factor around (i) a multi-head attention with relative positional encoding (captures long context — sentences span hundreds of frames) and (ii) a deep convolutional module (pointwise convolution $ $ depthwise with dilation, GLU/SiLU, LayerNorm) that captures local phonetics (formants, transitions). The takeaway intuition: attention alone is bad at the fine local patterns of speech, convolution alone does not see the context — the sandwich of the two dominated industrial ASR 2021-2024 (before being absorbed by the foundation models of ch. 4: wav2vec 2, HuBERT, Whisper encoder).

Theorem 3.3 — RNN-T (Graves, 2012): the CTC that speaks in real time The RNN-transducer combines an encoder f_t (frame \to state, causal — processed on the fly) and a prediction network g_u (tokens already emitted \to state); a joint network produces P(k \mid t, u) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(W\, f_t + W'\, g_u + b\big)_{k}, \qquad k \in \mathcal V \cup \{\epsilon\}, and the likelihood sums over all paths of the (t, u) grid — it is the forward-backward of Theorem 2.3, transposed onto a T \times U trellis where \epsilon = “advance in t without emitting”. Decisive property: the encoder is causal, each f_t is computable as soon as the frame arrives — RNN-T is the queen architecture of streaming ASR (ch. 5), notably on device. Cost: an \mathcal O(TU) lattice at training and the CTC monotonicity assumption (no reordering).

4. Whisper: robustness and multi-task

Theorem 4.1 — Whisper (Radford et al., ICML 2023): the strength of the weak Trained on \approx 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio/text pairs collected from the web (versus a few hundred to thousand carefully curated hours before), Whisper is a simple transformer encoder-decoder (88M-1.5B parameters); the audio is cut into 30 s windows, log-Mel 80 bins. The output is a sequence of special tokens + text: \langle\!\|start\,\|\rangle \to language \to task (transcribe/translate into English) \to timestamps \to text — everything (language identifier, translation decision, time) is learned as language, no dedicated module. Results: unprecedented robustness to noise/reverberation/accents (trained on real, not read data), WER close to humans on clean English; best zero-shot performance on most languages at the time.

Proposition 4.2 — Why it works: the scaling law of dirty data (i) Coverage: robustness is not a module, it is a consequence of diversity — the 680 kh cover real-world mess (background noise, music, accents), whereas LibriSpeech (1000 h of read books) overlearns clean reading; (ii) Filtering beats labeling: the training pipeline filters aggressively (language detection, audio-text matching by an initial ASR, anti-hallucination heuristics) rather than correcting by hand — the moderately clean but massive data wins; (iii) Multi-task as regularization: transcribing AND translating AND timestamping share one encoder — translation forces semantic, not only acoustic, representations. Documented limits: hallucinations on long silences (looped repetitions — the model “invents” text), no native streaming (ch. 5), difficult fine specialization.

Example 4.3 — French in the crosshairs On clean English, a good system reaches human WER (~5%); the same model on spontaneous contemporary French typically does 2 to 3 times worse (accents, speaking rates, code-switching, proper names) — lab 1 measures it. Three French phenomena to know for engineering: liaison (les|ami·z·arbitres: spelling is not phonetics — the ASR must relearn the graphy), enchaînement (final consonant tied to the next vowel: word boundaries blur in the spectrogram) and number verbalization (1996: “mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize” or “dix-neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize”? — normalization ambiguity, both are valid; cf. ch. 8 for the reverse direction in TTS).

5. Streaming: chunking, VAD, latency vs quality

Conversing means answering fast: the ASR must produce words while one speaks. This chapter deals with the “offline \to streaming” transformation, whose cost is measured in WER added per second gained.

Definition 5.1 — Latencies of a streaming ASR The algorithmic latency of a chunked encoder (chunking) of size L frames with a lookahead of R frames equals \lambda = (L + R)\,\Delta, \qquad \Delta = \text{frame step } (10\ \mathrm{ms}), — one cannot predict before having heard the context. The real-time factor (RTF) measures the computational cost: computation time / audio duration (RTF \< 1 required, \<\< 1 on device). The first-word latency adds the VAD (ch. 10) and the energy bar. A conversational voice assistant targets \lambda \leq 300 ms end to end (lab 5) — humans answer in ~200 ms (ch. 10): this is the structuring constraint of the field.

Theorem 5.2 — Three streaming architectures (Moritz et al., ICASSP 2020, for the first)

  1. Block attention: the transformer encoder processes windows of L frames with a complete left context (memory) and a bounded right lookahead R — the attention is masked beyond; the larger R grows, the more the WER drops and the latency rises (curve of ch. 5.3);
  2. Causal RNN-T (Theorem 3.3): pure encoder L = 1, R = 0 — emission from the frame on; the lookahead is reinjected into the prediction network (the right context comes from the tokens already emitted); standard on mobile;
  3. Asynchronous re-scoring: a first streaming pass gives the real-time display, an offline model (Whisper) re-corrects the final transcription with a few hundred ms of delay — the interface shows the text “correcting itself” (the design of 2026 assistants: the truth arrives one second later, reactivity is immediate).

Proposition 5.3 — The latency/quality curve, and how to read it For each point (L, R) (or each model choice): measure WER and first-word latency on a fixed test corpus; plot WER(\lambda). Typical shape: plateau up to \lambda \approx 200 ms (the useful context of a phoneme fits in ~200 ms), then rapid drop (one loses the transitions), then plateau (ultra-context no longer helps). Engineer’s reading: choose the elbow point; scientific reading: the curve is a control curve (fidelity against resource) — exactly the grid of the Control in Generative AI module (M2, ch. 2 and 9), where “diversity” is replaced by “reactivity”.

VAD: the gateway of the stream Voice activity detection decides, window by window (10-30 ms), whether the frame contains speech: energy + periodicity (historical), energy/noise GMM (Signal module ch. 7), lightweight networks (Silero VAD — the lab’s tool). Two errors of opposite cost: the false alarm sends noise to the ASR (Whisper hallucinations on silences!); the miss cuts word beginnings (the initial French “euh”). The VAD also initiates endpointing — deciding that the user has finished speaking (ch. 10): even more sensitive, because it sets the perceived latency of the whole system.

6. Vocoders: from WaveNet to HiFi-GAN

Theorem 6.1 — WaveNet (van den Oord et al., 2016): autoregression over the samples The vocoder models the waveform directly: p(x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{ech}}} p(x_t \mid x_{\<t},\, c), with T_{\mathrm{ech}} = 24,000 samples per second, x_t \mu-law quantized over 256 levels, c the conditioning (Mel, speaker). Architecture: dilated causal convolutions — layer k looks 2^k samples back (dilations 1, 2, 4, …, 512, stacked): the receptive field grows exponentially with depth (\approx 2^L for L layers), where a non-dilated convolution would require 2^L layers. Quality: close to human speech in subjective tests (MOS above 4, versus ~4.5 for the ground truth); fatal flaw: generating one second of speech takes 24,000 sequential steps — orders of magnitude slower than real time, unusable in production.

Proposition 6.2 — HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., NeurIPS 2020): speed through the adversary Complete reversal: the generator is parallel feed-forward (upsampling by transposed convolutions from the 80-bin Mel to the waveform, then multi-receptive-field dilated convolutions for phase coherence); training is adversarial (GAN recap: Multimodal GenAI M1 module ch. 2) with two families of discriminators — multi-period (they rearrange the waveform into matrices of periods 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 to trap periodic artifacts: the “buzz”) and multi-scale — plus an L1 loss on the reconstructed Mel to anchor the content. Result: WaveNet quality, parallel generation — orders of magnitude faster than real time on GPU, the industry’s default vocoder. To remember from the chapter: the pairing of mixing duration (autoregressive over 24 kHz) against parallel architecture + perceptual loss dictates all of modern TTS.

The Mel as a contractual interface The two stages of the TTS (ch. 7) communicate via the Mel spectrogram: 80 bins, one frame roughly every 12 ms (~ 80 Hz), logarithmic scale — the vocoder’s specification sheet. Changing the definition (sampling frequency, hop, n_bins) breaks the model/vocoder compatibility — the first experiment of lab 2. And the Mel is not invertible (phase lost): the whole difficulty of the vocoder is to re-invent a coherent phase — hence the period discriminators.

7. Neural TTS: Tacotron 2, VITS, diffusion and flow

Theorem 7.1 — Tacotron 2 (Shen et al., ICASSP 2018): the two-stage scheme Stage 1 (acrobatic): an attentional encoder-decoder reads the text (graphemes) and writes the Mel frame by frame — the location-sensitive attention learns the text→acoustic alignment there (same mechanism as LAS in ch. 3, applied in reverse); training by teacher forcing on the reference Mels. Stage 2: a WaveNet (ch. 6) turns the Mel into a wave. Historical quality: natural on read sentences. Two chronic diseases: attention failure (skipped or stuttered words on long or out-of-distribution sentences) and abusive determinization — one text, a single voice/rate (the one-to-many of Definition 0.1 denied by training toward the average Mel: smoothed, metallic output).

Proposition 7.2 — VITS (Kim et al., ICML 2021): one-stage, stochastic, end-to-end Three ideas that answer Tacotron’s diseases. (i) VAE: a variational encoder encodes the target speech into z; the decoder (HiFi-GAN) writes the wave from z — the noise of the prior explores the multiplicity of ways of saying; (ii) normalizing flow: a flow (exact change of variables: cf. Graph Generative Models M2 module ch. 4 for the proof) aligns the distribution of the text-conditioned latent with the prior, which makes training end-to-end (exact likelihood + adversary); (iii) implicit monotone alignment (MAS: search for the optimal monotone alignment between text and Mel, refined at each step). Result: a single text→wave network, parallel, stochastic — the first “industrializable” generation of TTS (YourTTS, ch. 9, derives from it).

Theorem 7.3 — Iterative generation arrives: Grad-TTS then Voicebox Grad-TTS (Popov et al., ICML 2021): an encoder predicts, for each phone, the target mean \mu(x) of a Gaussian in Mel space; a diffusion decoder (DDPM — cf. Multimodal GenAI M1 module ch. 3) turns a noise \mathcal N(0, I) into a Mel around \mu, guided by the noise prediction \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \mu); a duration predictor handles the number of frames per phone. Quality grows with the number of steps… like latency: the step/quality curve is the trade-off curve of the Control module (M2) ch. 9, in TTS version. Voicebox (Le et al., 2023): conditional flow matching in infilling mode — one masks a region of the Mel and regenerates only it, the context remaining fixed: the same machinery as DiGress’s inpainting (Graph Generative Models M2 module ch. 5) transported into acoustics. Immediate applications: editing a word in a sentence while keeping the voice, de-noising, voice translation… and zero-shot cloning from a few seconds of reference. Non-autoregressive and parallel: the dominant family of research TTS 2024-2026.

Example 7.4 — Latency budget of a conversational TTS Goal: < 300 ms between the end of the LLM’s decision and the first sound. An AR Tacotron-like: the first sentence must be synthesized before the first sound (if one wants the intonation) — 1-2 s of lead time required: prohibitive. VITS: the first chunk of Mel (a few frames) suffices — ~100 ms; Voicebox: one must bound the number of sampling steps: 4-10 steps (instead of 50-100) with distillation, otherwise quality drops (the curve of thm. 7.3). Conclusion: the full-duplex of ch. 10 was only possible after the non-autoregressive revolution — architecture and conversation are coupled constraints.

8. Expressivity: prosody, style tokens, SSML

Definition 8.1 — Prosody, three continuous sliders The manner of saying decomposes into F_0 (melody, in Hz — ~100-200 Hz for men, 160-300 for women, final fall of declarative utterances), duration (contrastive lengthening: a stretched “énorme”), energy (accents, stressed words). These sliders are continuous and intertwined: the TTS must predict them all, and text alone is ambiguous (one neutral sentence, ten valid readings) — it is the one-to-many of ch. 7, aesthetic version. French adds its signature: final group accent (not lexical as in English), interrogative rise without inversion, sustained rate that makes segmentation critical.

Theorem 8.2 — Global Style Tokens (Wang et al., Interspeech 2018): style as a controllable latent A reference encoder reads a reference audio (a few seconds) and produces an embedding; an attention reads this embedding over a bank of style tokens (small learned dictionary of “manners of saying”); the weighted vector conditions the Tacotron (rendezvous of ch. 7). Three uses: transfer (give the audio of an enthusiastic speaker, generate the target text with their prosody), control (move the embedding in token space — attenuate “aggressive”), exploration (the tokens spontaneously organize into interpretable axes: rate, energy, emotion). It is the steering vector of the Control module (M2) ch. 4, applied to TTS: a latent direction read from contrasts, added instead of learned — same virtues (soft, composable control), same limits (no guarantee).

Definition 8.3 — SSML: the hard control of production The Speech Synthesis Markup Language (W3C) is the control language of industrial TTS: pauses (<break time="500ms"/>), rate/pitch (<prosody rate="+10%" pitch="+2st">), emphasis (<emphasis>), pronunciation (<phoneme alphabet="ipa" ph="ʁə.ʒan">), dates and numbers. It is a hard constraint in the sense of the Control module ch. 2: the engine is obliged to obey (exact pause), as opposed to the soft conditioning of style tokens. Text normalization (front-end): “1 234 567 €” \to “un million deux cent trente-quatre mille cinq cent soixante-sept euros” is the hidden half of TTS — neural systems integrate the front-end (letter tokenizer) at the price of amusing errors (acronyms, “SARS-CoV-2”: to be tested in lab 2).

Controlling what, and how: the complete grid Chapter recap in the taxonomy of the Control module (M2):

9. Voice conversion and cloning: ethics and watermarking

Definition 9.1 — Conversion, adaptation, zero-shot cloning Voice conversion: re-emit what a source voice says with the timbre of a target voice (content preserved, speaker changed). Zero-shot cloning: synthesize any text in a given voice from an audio prompt of a few seconds (VALL-E, Voicebox: conditioning by reference, ch. 7). Technically: a speaker embedding (learned by speaker verification — the GE2E loss of Wan et al., 2018, aligns the embeddings of the same voice); YourTTS (ICML 2022) adds it to VITS (the TTS of ch. 7 becomes multi-speaker in one training); VALL-E (Microsoft, 2023) treats cloning as language: AR over the EnCodec acoustic tokens (ch. 11), the 3 s prompt serving as prefix — TTS is absorbed by the LLM paradigm.

Theorem 9.2 — Ethics: the law follows (slowly), the engineer decides (fast) Cloning from 3 s of reference makes possible: voice fraud (calls to the “boss”), audio disinformation, impersonation. Frameworks: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act, art. 50) imposes transparency — systems generating or manipulating image/voice must mark their outputs machine-readably; in France, the voice is protected as an attribute of personality (civil case law on imitation). For the engineer, the operational answer is technical: consent (voice datasets under a clear license — lab 2 uses only authorized voices), marking (watermarking, below), detection (provenance: cf. Explainability module ch. 9 for text, the logic is identical). The exam question: “why is TTS watermarking easier than text watermarking?” — because the output space is continuous and the channel capacity is immense (ch. 6): one can hide bits without perceptible loss… but the channel degrades under compressions.

Theorem 9.3 — Audio watermarking: from spread spectrum to networks Classical: spread-spectrum tattooing — add a low-energy pseudo-random noise (key k), masked by the psychoacoustic threshold effect, in a time-frequency domain (STFT — Signal Processing M1 modules ch. 6-7: time-frequency analysis and random signals are proven there); detection by correlation with the key — the exact structure of the textual green/red watermark (ch. 6 of the Control module): a pseudo-random key, a hypothesis test, a strength/quality trade-off. Neural: WavMark (2023) hides ~32 bits per second of speech with a learned encoder/detector, robust to re-encodings; AudioSeal (Meta, ICASSP 2024) adds localization: a sample-level detector indicates which segments are synthetic (adversarial generator/detector, perceptual loss); marking during generation (in-generation) applies the watermark in the TTS itself — inseparable from the output. Evaluation protocol: ROC curves detection rate / false positives, under attack (mp3, re-voicing, reverberation) — the robustness budget of the Control module ch. 10, word for word.

Example 9.4 — Voice fraud in 3 steps (and its 3 countermeasures) (1) 3 s of public voice \to zero-shot cloning; (2) urgency script generated by an LLM (referral to LLMs M2); (3) vishing call. Countermeasures: (i) channel: automatic call-back on a known number (the most robust control is organizational); (ii) challenge posed by the interlocutor (off-script question — conversational TTS answer these better and better, cf. ch. 10); (iii) provenance: watermark verified at reception if both phones support it. Moral of the module: no single layer suffices — defense in depth.

10. The conversational loop: VAD, turn-taking, full-duplex

Theorem 10.1 — The human speaking turn, in numbers Human conversation alternates turns with a median delay on the order of 200 ms — measured across a dozen languages (Stivers et al., PNAS 2009); now pronouncing the first word of a turn takes ~600 ms: humans anticipate the end of the other’s turn (prosodic, syntactic, pragmatic cues) to prepare their own. An assistant that listens to everything, waits for silence, transcribes, thinks, synthesizes, shows ~1.5-3 s of latency — five to ten times too slow: turn-taking is the real conversational bottleneck, not intelligence. Reference framework: the survey by Skantze (Computer Speech & Language, 2021): transition-relevance points (TRP), end-of-turn prediction (end-of-turn: classifier on a sliding window — VAD + prosody + syntax), backchannels (“hmm”, audible nods: listening without taking the turn).

Proposition 10.2 — Pipeline: endpointing as control Deciding “the user is done” is an error/latency trade-off: cutting too early = interruptions (the assistant speaks over — the most badly perceived error), cutting too late = dead silences. Parameters: silence threshold \tau_s (typically 300-700 ms), depth of context (rising prosody? incomplete syntax?), and policy (letting long hesitations speak). Formalization: a sequential test — detect the “end of turn” regime change as early as possible under a false-positive constraint; the same fidelity/cost curve as streaming ASR (ch. 5), this time at the dialogue level. Modern implementation: the end-of-turn predictor becomes a small multi-modal model (audio + partial text) that replaces hand-tuned thresholds.

Theorem 10.3 — Full-duplex: dGSLM then Moshi (the 2023-2024 leap) dGSLM (Nguyen et al., TACL 2023): the first entirely vocal dialogue model — two streams of HuBERT tokens (discretized speech, ch. 11) run in parallel; the model learns by itself alternation and overlaps (simultaneous laughs): proof of concept that turn-taking is learnable without text. Moshi (Défossez et al., Kyutai, 2024): the first real-time full-duplex voice assistant. Three pieces: (i) the neural codec Mimi (descendant of EnCodec, ch. 11) tokenizes audio at low rate (~12.5 frames/s) into two streams: semantic and acoustic; (ii) the multi-stream architecture: the tokens of Moshi (the model’s voice) and of the user are predicted in parallel, not in alternation — the model can cut itself off or fall silent during an interruption; (iii) the inner monologue: each audio token of the model is aligned with its corresponding text token — the text guides, the audio realizes; theoretical latency ~200 ms (the human target of thm. 10.1), dialogue memory via the transformer context. The module’s loop is closed: VAD, endpointing, ASR-LLM-TTS pipeline disappear into a single generative model — the next topic.

What full-duplex changes in practice (i) Interruptions: handled natively (the user stream continues to be predicted while Moshi speaks — the model “hears itself” speak); (ii) Backchannels: emergent, unscripted; (iii) The voice of machines: unique and marked vocal identity (transparency policy — AI Act ch. 9); (iv) the risk: human latency makes the machine socially indistinguishable — watermarking (ch. 9) becomes the only frontier. To be debated in session: should a 200 ms assistant announce itself?

11. Speech LLMs and multilingualism

Definition 11.1 — Tokenizing audio: neural codecs and RVQ A neural codec (EnCodec: Défossez et al., 2022) compresses speech with a convolutional auto-encoder constrained by a residual vector quantization (RVQ): the continuous latent is approximated by a sum of vectors from successive codebooks — each frame (at 24 kHz: one frame \approx 320 samples, ~75 Hz) becomes a stack of ~2-32 discrete indices. Result: speech becomes a text of tokens — consumable by a transformer. The hierarchy of AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2023) orders these tokens: first semantic (discretized units of a self-supervised model — wav2vec 2/HuBERT — that capture the content), then coarse then fine acoustic (timbre, environment): generating in this order guarantees the content before the texture. It is the architecture of dGSLM and Moshi (ch. 10).

Theorem 11.2 — Speech LLMs: three audio grafts onto an LLM

  1. Audio-in: the speech encoder (Whisper, HuBERT) is projected into the LLM’s space by an adapter (SALMONN, SpeechGPT, and all multimodal assistants 2025+) — the model listens (implicit transcription, paralinguistics: emotion, speaker) and answers in text; it is the “audio-in” family of ch. 8 of the LLMs module (M2), same machinery as vision;
  2. Audio-out: the LLM generates acoustic tokens (VALL-E: AR over EnCodec with a voice prompt; AudioLM units at the output); the cloning of ch. 9 becomes a skill of the language model;
  3. Audio-in-out (speech-to-speech): Moshi (ch. 10): the text is the inner monologue guiding the audio tokens in both directions — the LLM is the pipeline. SpeechT5 (2022) had laid down the first unified text/speech architecture (T5 encoder-decoder + shared codebook); 2024-2026 generalize: one model, all the module’s tasks (ASR, TTS, speech translation, editing, voice to voice).

Proposition 11.3 — Multilingualism: 7,000 languages, 100 covered The bottleneck of low-resource languages: labeling is expensive, writing is sometimes lacking. MMS (Meta, 2023 — “Scaling Speech Technology to 1000+ Languages”): a single wav2vec 2 model covers 1,107 languages in ASR and TTS (4,017 in language identification), learned on public religious readings (the paradox: the bible is the most translated corpus in the world) — a jump by a factor of 10-40 in coverage. Common Voice (Mozilla): open crowd-sourcing (including French — regional accents: lab 1 draws from it). The specificities of French return (ch. 4, ex. 4.3): number normalization (the date sub-test: “en 1996”), acronyms, loanwords. For the engineer: zero-shot (Whisper/MMS) first, light adaptation next (LoRA on the encoder — LLMs module M2 ch. 6), targeted data last; and measure per language — a multilingual “average” WER masks local collapses.

Conclusion of the module — the fusion of the three worlds Signal (M1), language (LLMs M2), conversation (this module): speech is no longer an interface in front of language, it becomes one of its dialects — discrete tokens like any others, generatable, guidable, controllable. The control of the Control in Generative AI module (M2, ch. 2: the taxonomy) now applies word for word: hard constraints = SSML and prosody grammars; guidance = style tokens and cloning by prompt; watermarking = voice provenance (ch. 9); evaluation = WER/MOS/latency and trade-off curves. Wherever you set foot in 2026 — research or product — the final question is the same: what must be controlled, and at what price?

  5. Latency budget of a voice assistant.
