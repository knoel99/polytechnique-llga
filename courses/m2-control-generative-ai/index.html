1. Why control: safety, fidelity, intellectual property

Consider an agent that “follows” its prompt in every respect except the output grammar that the business application expects. This chapter explains why such failures are unavoidable without dedicated mechanisms, and motivates each family of control by the failure it addresses.

What breaks Three naive approaches fail in practice: a prompt alone imposes no constraint on the output, rejecting bad outputs and resampling is too costly when good outputs are rare, and a watermark added without care does not survive changes in sampling or editing.

A generator is a law over an immense space. Asking it politely (a prompt) only constrains this law statistically: no theorem guarantees the exact format, the safety of the content or the traceability of the origin.

Definition 1.1 — Controlling a generator Let p_\theta(\cdot \mid x) be a conditional law over outputs y \in \mathcal{Y}^* (token sequences, images, graphs). Controlling means replacing p_\theta with a law \tilde p that

  • (i) lives on a specified subset S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}^* (support constraint: a language, a JSON schema, a chemical valence); or
  • (ii) maximizes in expectation a property c (fidelity constraint: following the instruction, the style, the target); or
  • (iii) carries a verifiable mark of origin (provenance constraint: watermarking).

Chapters 3 to 8 realize (i)-(ii)-(iii) through different mechanisms; ch. 2 will organize them.

Example 1.2 — Three real failures, three needs

  1. Format safety: one asks the LLM for a JSON of the form {"user": ...}; it replies with a trailing comma, a typographic apostrophe, or worse a preamble “Here is the requested JSON”. Any downstream parser breaks. The need: S = the strict JSON language, a 100% guarantee — that is ch. 3.
  2. Fidelity: one asks for a neutral summary in formal French; the model slips into casual style one time out of ten. The prompt guarantees nothing; the need is statistical — soft steering of the law: ch. 4 and 5.
  3. Provenance: a text circulates: human or machine? The classifier is unreliable; the need is cryptographic: a statistical mark planted at generation, testable — ch. 6.

Theorem 1.3 — Intellectual property and law as drivers of control Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (“AI Act”, art. 50) imposes provenance transparency: providers of generative systems must mark their outputs in a machine-readable way and make them detectable as artificially generated.

Concretely: watermarking (ch. 6) is no longer an academic exercise but a compliance requirement; likewise for audio marking (cf. Signal Processing module) and images. This legal framework motivates the control/robustness articulation (ch. 10): an attackable mark is not a proof.

Control cuts across modalities The module’s guiding thread: the same four mechanisms (condition, guide, constrain the support, filter a posteriori) apply to text, images, and graphs. The tools differ (automata for text; ControlNet and CFG for images; valence masks for graphs), the theory does not. The table in ch. 2 sets the grid; each chapter fills it in for one modality.

2. Taxonomy: four families of control

Many techniques claim to “control” a generator; this chapter sorts them into four families according to where they intervene and what they guarantee, so that every later chapter can be placed on the same grid.

Definition 2.1 — The four families Let c be the control signal (instruction, target property, admissible language).

  1. Conditioning: c enters the model (prompt, prefix, fused embedding); the law becomes p_\theta(\cdot \mid x, c). Zero inference cost, no guarantee — soft control;
  2. Guidance: one biases the sampling toward c without relearning p_\theta — guided score \hat\epsilon_u + w(\hat\epsilon_c - \hat\epsilon_u) in diffusion (ch. 7), activation steering for LLMs (ch. 4), gradient guidance of flows (ch. 8); the strength w tunes fidelity at the price of diversity;
  3. Hard constraints: one restricts the support — automaton-constrained decoding (ch. 3), valence masks (ch. 8), inpainting. A 100% guarantee; risk: a locally renormalized law, distorted with respect to p_\theta(\cdot \mid S) (theorem 2.2) and possibly impoverished;
  4. Post-processing / filtering: generate, check c, reject (best-of-n, resampling, correction); exact but costly when c is rare.

Theorem 2.2 — The mask guarantees the support, not the conditional law Let S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}^* be a set of admissible outputs and p_\theta an autoregressive law. At step t, the mask keeps the set A_t(y_{<t}) of the tokens after which the prefix can still be completed into an element of S.

Write Z_t(y_{<t}) = \sum_{v \in A_t(y_{<t})} p_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) for the mass that survives the mask at step t. Masked decoding only produces elements of S, and it samples them from the locally renormalized law

Key result — masking renormalizes step by step \tilde p_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t} \frac{p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{Z_t(y_{<t})} = \frac{p_\theta(y \mid x)}{\prod_t Z_t(y_{<t})}, \qquad y \in S .

Exact conditioning divides by one constant instead: p_\theta(y \mid x,\ y \in S) = \frac{p_\theta(y \mid x)}{p_\theta(S \mid x)} . The two laws coincide if and only if the product of the normalizers \prod_t Z_t(y_{<t}) takes the same value for every y \in S. In general it does not, and the mask is not an exact conditioning.

Proof.

  1. Support. Every prefix of an element of S survives the mask, every surviving prefix can still be completed in S, and EOS is allowed only on an element of S: the masked law lives on S.
  2. Formula. At step t the decoder draws y_t with probability p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) / Z_t(y_{<t}); by the chain rule, the product of the numerators is p_\theta(y \mid x).
  3. Comparison. If the product of the normalizers equals a constant c on S, summing the masked law over S gives 1 = p_\theta(S \mid x) / c , so the constant is p_\theta(S \mid x) and the two laws coincide. Conversely, if the laws coincide, the product equals p_\theta(S \mid x) for every y \in S (the softmax gives every output a positive probability).

The exact sampler would weight each token by its chance of still ending in S, p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}, S) = p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\, \frac{p_\theta(S \mid x, y_{\leq t})}{p_\theta(S \mid x, y_{<t})} ; the mask only knows whether this chance is positive. ∎

Counterexample — two steps suffice Vocabulary \{a, b\}, outputs of two tokens, and the model p_\theta(a) = p_\theta(b) = \tfrac12, \qquad p_\theta(a \mid a) = 0.1, \quad p_\theta(b \mid a) = 0.9, \qquad p_\theta(a \mid b) = p_\theta(b \mid b) = \tfrac12 . The constraint forbids ab: S = \{aa,\ ba,\ bb\}, \qquad p_\theta(S) = 0.05 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.55 .

  • Masked decoding. At step 1 both letters can still reach S, so nothing is masked. After a only a survives (Z_2 = 0.1), and it is renormalized to probability 1. Hence \tilde p(aa) = 0.5 and \tilde p(ba) = \tilde p(bb) = 0.25.
  • Exact conditioning. p(aa \mid S) = 0.05 / 0.55 \approx 0.091 and p(ba \mid S) = p(bb \mid S) \approx 0.455.

The mask makes aa 5.5 times too likely, the ratio p_\theta(S) / Z_2 = 0.55 / 0.1: at step 1 it ignored that a leads to a near dead end.

When the mask is nearly exact, and how to be exact Suppose that at every step the mask removes a mass of at most \varepsilon, so that Z_t \geq 1 - \varepsilon, on outputs of at most T tokens. Then p_\theta(S \mid x) \geq (1-\varepsilon)^T and, for every y \in S, (1-\varepsilon)^{T} \;\leq\; \frac{\tilde p_\theta(y \mid x)}{p_\theta(y \mid x,\ y \in S)} \;\leq\; (1-\varepsilon)^{-T} . The mask is nearly free in bias when the model rarely tries to leave S (\varepsilon T \ll 1, training data already in S), and strongly biased when it often walks towards dead ends, as in the counterexample.

Two exact alternatives exist. Rejection sampling (proposition 2.3) draws exactly from p_\theta(\cdot \mid S), at the expected price of 1/p_\theta(S \mid x) passes. Importance sampling keeps the masked proposal and corrects it with the weight \prod_t Z_t(y_{<t}), which the decoder computes for free.

Sequential Monte Carlo applies this correction during decoding, resampling a population of partial outputs according to their weights (Lew et al., 2023; Loula et al., ICLR 2025); grammar-aligned decoding estimates the missing look-ahead from earlier samples (Park et al., NeurIPS 2024). Graph Generative Models (period 2) meets the same distortion with valence masks (its ch. 3).

Proposition 2.3 — Rejection costs 1/p_\star(S) Filtering by rejection (drawing iid until landing in S) realizes p_\theta(\cdot \mid S) exactly, but the number of draws follows a geometric law of expectation 1/p_\theta(S \mid x).

Proof. Each draw is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability q = p_\theta(S \mid x); the waiting time for the first success is geometric, \mathbb{E}[N] = \sum_{k \geq 1} k(1-q)^{k-1}q = 1/q . For complex unconstrained JSON, q can be 10^{-2} (100 draws on average); for a valid molecule without constraint, GraphAF measures 68% (ch. 8); for a sentence satisfying both ten rare constraints, q collapses exponentially — hence the practical advantage of the mask, which pays only a single pass — at the price of the distortion of theorem 2.2. ∎

Hard or soft: how to choose? A favorite exam question. If failure is catastrophic (the parser breaks, the molecule is invalid): hard constraint. If failure is gradual (tone, style, relevance): guidance, with the strength tuned by the fidelity/diversity curve of ch. 9. In practice one stacks: format constraint (hard) + alignment (RLHF) + watermark (provenance) — the three layers of 2026 production systems, which ch. 10 shows fall… together if one is not careful.

3. Constrained decoding: grammars, automata, masks

The technical heart of the module for text: guaranteeing that an output authorized token by token belongs to a formal language — regular expression, context-free grammar (JSON, SQL), or logical constraint. The founding idea (PICARD, then Outlines and XGrammar): decoding is a walk in an automaton, and the LLM is only allowed to follow valid transitions.

3.1. From regex to automaton

Definition 3.1 — DFA and regular language A deterministic finite automaton is a quintuple A = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F): states Q, alphabet \Sigma, transition \delta : Q \times \Sigma \to Q, initial state q_0, accepting states F.

One extends \delta to words by \delta^*(q, uv) = \delta^*(\delta^*(q, u), v). The recognized language is L(A) = \{w \in \Sigma^* : \delta^*(q_0, w) \in F\}.

Construction: regex \to \varepsilon-NFA (Thompson, linear in the pattern size), then determinization by subset construction (2^{|Q|} states at worst, rare in practice).

Course example, the ISO date: \d{4}-(0[1-9]|1[0-2])-(0[1-9]|[12]\d|3[01]) — a nontrivial F: “2026-02-31” must be syntactically accepted (calendar semantics is not a simple regular language: we return to this).

Proposition 3.2 — Prune before masking: co-accessibility For masking, one replaces A with its co-accessible sub-automaton: one removes the states q from which no accepting state is reachable (computation: reverse traversal of the transitions from F, \mathcal{O}(|Q| + |\delta|)). It is this sub-automaton that guarantees that a prefix always remains extensible into a complete word: without pruning, the decoder could lock itself in a dead end (no admissible token, including EOS) and generate indefinitely.

3.2. The masking algorithm, in detail

Theorem 3.3 — Constrained decoding by state→tokens index (Willard & Louf, 2023) Let V be the tokenizer vocabulary (multi-character tokens!). One precomputes, for each state q of the pruned DFA, the set of admissible tokens \mathrm{Adm}(q) = \big\{ v \in V \ : \ \delta^*(q, \mathrm{str}(v)) \ \text{is defined and co-accessible} \big\}, with the table \mathrm{next}(q, v) = \delta^*(q, \mathrm{str}(v)).

Constrained decoding replaces each distribution with p^{\mathrm{mask}}_\theta(v \mid y_{<t}) \propto p_\theta(v \mid y_{<t})\, \mathbf{1}\big[v \in \mathrm{Adm}(q_t)\big], \qquad q_{t+1} = \mathrm{next}(q_t, v_t), and allows EOS only if q_t \in F.

Then every output is in L(A): the guarantee is structural, by induction over the steps (theorem 2.2).

Proof.

  • Complexity. Precomputation: |Q| \times |V| token reads, each \mathcal{O}(|v|) transitions — once and for all (and shareable between requests of the same grammar: XGrammar further accelerates by separating context-independent tokens, pre-checked globally, from dependent ones, the only ones resimulated).
  • Online: one lookup per step, \mathcal{O}(1) — the overhead over the LLM pass (\mathcal{O}(n^2)) is negligible.
  • BPE subtlety: a token is not a character; "12" and "12/" are two distinct tokens; the definition of \mathrm{Adm} copes with this because \delta^* reads the string of the token, character by character — the DFA does not even know it is being run over BPEs.

Instructive counterexample: a token can cross the boundary of the language ("}," is valid at the end of JSON, "},x" is not); this is precisely why one must index over tokens and not over characters. ∎

# pedagogical skeleton: automaton-based constrained decoding (cf. lab 1)
import torch

class DfaMask:
    """dfa: transitions[q] = {character: q2}, accepting = {q...} (already pruned)."""

    def __init__(self, dfa, tokenizer):
        self.index = {}                        # q -> {token_id: q2}
        for q in dfa.states:                   # precomputation O(|Q| x |V| x |v|)
            self.index[q] = {}
            for tid, s in tokenizer.vocab.items():
                q2 = dfa.walk(q, s)            # read character by character
                if q2 is not None:             # None = dead prefix
                    self.index[q][tid] = q2

    def mask(self, logits, q):
        mask = torch.full_like(logits, float("-inf"))
        for tid in self.index[q]:
            mask[tid] = 0.0
        return logits + mask                   # -inf = forbidden

Example 3.4 — A date by hand Constraint with a month check, \d{2}/(0[1-9]|1[0-2])/\d{4}. After "26/0", the state encodes “second digit of the month in progress, 0 already seen”; the token "0" is masked (only 1-9 complete a month 01-09), and so is "/". Conversely, if the first digit of the month were 1, the second would be restricted to 0-2.

Writing the table \mathrm{Adm}(q) for these states is exercise 1; the classic pitfall: allowing the token "13" as a single BPE block when the state accepts only "1" followed by "0-2" — "13" must be masked as a whole.

Beyond regular languages: grammars and JSON Schema Regular languages cannot express nesting ({"a": {"b": ...}} of arbitrary depth). One needs context-free grammars: PICARD (Scholak et al., EMNLP 2021) constrains a T5 on SQL by incremental parsing (forbidden logits are set to -\infty as soon as the token makes the derivation dead).

XGrammar compiles EBNF/JSON Schema into nearly free online masks (2-10× faster than the previous state of the art). In 2026 industrial practice: every LLM tool call (function calling) goes through a grammar mask — the output is valid JSON by construction. Cf. LLMs module (M2) ch. 8 for the agent layer.

4. Steering: controlling in activation space

Constrained decoding acts on the outputs. Steering acts inside the network: if concepts (“positivity”, “gender”, “refusal”) are encoded by directions of the activation space, then moving them should move the behavior. This is the most fascinating and least guaranteed control of the module.

Definition 4.1 — Steering vector (ActAdd, Turner et al. 2023) Let h^{(l)}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d be the residual activation of layer l after reading the prefix x. For two contrasted prompts x^+ (“Love”) and x^- (“Hate”), one defines v = h^{(l)}(x^+) - h^{(l)}(x^-) \in \mathbb{R}^d, and, during generation, one replaces h^{(l)}(x_t) \leftarrow h^{(l)}(x_t) + \alpha\, v at every step (and often on the first token only); \alpha \in [-4, 4] is the guidance strength.

The model, the weights, the prompt: nothing changes — only a vector is added.

Example 4.2 — The founding experiment, described step by step (to replay in lab 3) Model: GPT-2 (124M-1.5B in the paper; the lab uses the small one).

  1. Compute v = h^{(6)}(\texttt{Love}) - h^{(6)}(\texttt{Hate}).

Proposition 4.3 — Why a simple addition shifts the output (first-order analysis) Let W_U denote the unembedding matrix (output layer). The logit of token w equals \ell_w = \langle W_U^{(w)}, h^{(L)} \rangle (final layer, ignoring the softmax).

If the steered residual h^{(L)} + \alpha v^{(L)} stays within the network’s linearity domain, then \Delta \ell_w \;=\; \alpha\, \langle W_U^{(w)}, v^{(L)} \rangle\ : all tokens aligned with the direction v are favored proportionally to \alpha — a global, monotone reweighting, even before any nonlinear effect.

Proof. Differentiability: to first order, h^{(L)} \mapsto \ell_w is affine; \Delta \ell_w = \nabla_h \ell_w^\top (\alpha v) = \alpha \langle W_U^{(w)}, v\rangle. This is exactly the structure of guidance (ch. 2, family 2): each token’s logit is shifted by a “compatibility with the direction” score.

The difference from the mask: here no logit is sent to -\infty — the law keeps full support, the control is soft; and the linear approximation becomes false as \alpha grows, hence the observed degeneration. ∎

The steering family in 2026: reading, writing, weights

  • Read: probing and concept directions (cf. Explainability module ch. 1);
  • write: ActAdd (difference of two activations), contrastive activation addition (differences averaged over pairs of behaviors), inference-time intervention (moving activations toward the truthfulness direction), representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023: extracting via self-interpretation directions of honesty, refusal, bias and subtracting/adding them);
  • on weights: task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., ICLR 2023): adding to the weights the difference between a fine-tuned model and the base model — the same “add a direction” gesture moved from one space to another, composable (adding two tasks, canceling one by subtraction).

Common limits: empirical choice of layer and \alpha, non-monotone effects, fragile out-of-distribution generalization — and no support guarantee: to truly forbid a format, only ch. 3 applies.

5. RLHF: control in behavior space

RLHF was presented as alignment (cf. LLMs M2 module ch. 5 — we do not redo its practice); here we reread it as a control mechanism, and we prove that it is — mathematically — the twin of classifier-free guidance in ch. 7. This double reading is the module’s synthesis point.

Theorem 5.1 — RLHF = exponential guidance of the output law (closed-form solution proved) The alignment program with KL penalty reads \pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big). Its unique solution is

Key result — closed form of the KL-regularized policy \pi^*(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\big(r(x, y)/\beta\big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y'} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y' \mid x) e^{r(x,y')/\beta}.

Proof. One maximizes over the law \pi (an infinite-dimensional variable). Set J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[r] - \beta\,\mathbb{E}_\pi[\log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}].

First order: for every perturbation \pi + \varepsilon(\nu - \pi) (\nu any law), \frac{d}{d\varepsilon} J\big|_{\varepsilon=0} = \mathbb{E}_{\nu}[r - \beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \beta - c] = 0 for all \nu, where c is the multiplier of the constraint \sum_y \pi(y) = 1.

This forces r(x,y) - \beta \log\frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} to be constant in y: \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} = r/\beta + \mathrm{const}, giving the result (the constant is fixed by normalization; the strict concavity of the KL gives uniqueness).

Control reading: the aligned policy is the reference law reweighted by an exponential factor in the reward, exactly as CFG reweights p(x_t) by p(c \mid x_t)^w (ch. 7).

\beta (the inverse of the reward temperature) plays the role of the guidance strength w, and the KL is the budget of allowed deviation — high fidelity to r, low diversity if \beta is small. ∎

Proposition 5.2 — DPO: the same solution, without a reward model By rewriting r = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} + \beta\log Z, the preference between y_w \succ y_l is expressed directly in policies: the DPO loss \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\Big) is the likelihood of the preferences under the closed form of theorem 5.1: DPO learns the same exponential guidance by direct regression on pairs.

GRPO (group-relative reinforcement) is its extension via within-group comparisons — developed in detail in ch. 5 of the LLMs module (M2), which should be consulted for the actual training.

Reward hacking: when control misses its target The reward is a spec; the model optimizes the spec, not the intention. The mechanism (well documented): the closed-form solution 5.1 concentrates mass on the peaks of r — including artifactual peaks (long answers, sycophancy, formats favored by the reward model).

The KL brakes (budget!), without forbidding: with KL budget zero, \pi^* = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}; as \beta \to 0, collapse onto the argmax of r. Same structure as the CFG fidelity/diversity trade-off — ch. 9 for the measurement, ch. 10 for the attack (exploiting the rewarder’s artifacts is a control hijack).

6. Watermarking: proving the origin of a text

Provenance constraint (def. 1.1 iii): modify the generation so that outputs carry a secret statistical mark, verifiable without wiggle room. The green/red scheme of Kirchenbauer et al. (ICML 2023) is the most taught because its test can be proved at the M2 level — it is also the bridge with the audio watermarking of the Signal Processing module and ch. 9 of the Explainability module.

The law of the test under the null hypothesis is proved in Large Language Models ch. 9; this chapter recalls it in one step and adds what that course leaves out: the power of the test and the text length it requires.

Definition 6.1 — Green/red watermark (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023) Key: secret k.

At every step: one hashes the previous token with k (PRF h_k), the result serves as a pseudo-random seed to partition the vocabulary: a green list G_k(y_{t-1}) of fraction \gamma (typically \gamma = 0.5), the rest in the red list.

Generation: before the softmax, one adds \delta to the green logits (typ. \delta = 2): \ell^{(t)}_v \leftarrow \ell^{(t)}_v + \delta \cdot \mathbf{1}\big[v \in G_k(y_{t-1})\big].

Detection: knowing k, one recounts the greens.

Theorem 6.2 — The z-test and its power (H0: unmarked text) Let a suspect text of T tokens, s = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{1}[y_t \in G_k(y_{t-1})] its number of greens, and the statistic

Key result — watermark z-score z \;=\; \frac{s - \gamma T}{\sqrt{T\, \gamma (1 - \gamma)}}.

Under the null hypothesis “text written without knowledge of k” (hence human, or an unmarked model), z converges in law to \mathcal{N}(0, 1) as T \to \infty, and one rejects H_0 at level \alpha if z > z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha).

If a marked text draws a green token with probability p_\delta > \gamma at each step, the power of this test and the length needed for a power 1 - \beta are approximately \mathbb{P}(z > z_{1-\alpha} \mid H_1) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{(p_\delta - \gamma)\sqrt{T} - z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)}}{\sqrt{p_\delta(1-p_\delta)}}\right), \qquad T \approx \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_\delta(1-p_\delta)}}{p_\delta - \gamma}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

  1. Law under H0 (proved in LLMs Theorem 9.3, recalled here). The pseudo-random seed h_k(y_{t-1}) makes each token of an unmarked text green with probability \gamma, so s \sim \mathrm{Bin}(T, \gamma) (neglecting the weak sequential dependence). The De Moivre-Laplace theorem gives z \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0,1).
  2. Threshold. P(false positive) = \alpha: with z_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 4, \alpha \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-5} — a human text is accused once in ~30,000.
  3. Law under H1. For a marked text, each step draws a green with probability p_\delta > \gamma (the bump \delta favors greens). With the same heuristic s \sim \mathrm{Bin}(T, p_\delta), hence \mathbb{E}[z \mid H_1] = \frac{(p_\delta - \gamma)\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)}}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(z \mid H_1) = \frac{p_\delta(1-p_\delta)}{\gamma(1-\gamma)} .
  4. Power and length. Approximating z under H1 by a Gaussian with these two moments, the power is the probability that this Gaussian exceeds z_{1-\alpha}: this is the first formula. Setting the argument of \Phi equal to z_{1-\beta} and solving for \sqrt{T} gives the second.

With \gamma = 0.5, \delta = 2, p_\delta \approx 0.7-0.8 in practice, and T = 200, \mathbb{E}[z] \approx 5.7-8.5 — detection is nearly certain. ∎

Example 6.3 — The test in numbers (exercise 2 in full) T = 200, \gamma = 0.5. A marked text observes s = 150 (75% greens): z = (150 - 100)/\sqrt{200 \times 0.25} = 50/\sqrt{50} \approx 7.07 — massive rejection of H0.

A typical human text would have s \in [87, 113] at 95% (z \in [-1.96, 1.96]).

Bonus question: how many tokens are needed to distinguish \gamma = 0.5 from p_\delta = 0.6 at level 5% (two-sided, as above) with power 0.9? The length formula of theorem 6.2, with the two-sided quantile \Phi^{-1}(0.975) in place of z_{1-\alpha}, gives T \approx \big(\Phi^{-1}(0.975)\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5} + \Phi^{-1}(0.9)\sqrt{0.6 \times 0.4}\big)^2 / (0.6 - 0.5)^2 = (0.98 + 0.63)^2 / 0.01 \approx 260.

The required length scales as (p_\delta - \gamma)^{-2}: halving the bias toward green tokens multiplies by four the length of text needed to detect it — this is the scaling law of watermarking.

Proposition 6.4 — The family: distortion, robustness, undetectability Four descendants structure the field.

  1. Unigram (Zhao et al., 2023): the green list is fixed (context-independent) — robust to paraphrase (tokens survive reordering), at the price of lower detectability at given T.
  2. Gumbel/Aaronson: the token draw follows an inverse rank sampled by PRF — distortion-free (the per-step marginal law is exactly the model’s); the score becomes a sum of logs.
  3. Kuditipudi et al. (2023): marking by a re-parameterizable key sequence, zero distortion and robust to edits;
  4. SynthID-Text (Dathathri et al., Nature 2024): deployed in production at Gemini’s scale — a tournament of keys per step, qualitatively preserved, corpus-aggregated test.

In contrast: the negative result of Sadasivan et al. (2023) — against a paraphraser good enough, every detector (watermark included) tends toward blindness.

Christ, Gunn & Zamir (2024) show the other extreme: a cryptographic watermark can be made undetectable without the key (marked outputs are computationally indistinguishable from the model’s), but such constructions are fragile to edits. Ch. 10 returns to this in attack mode.

Tuning: \gamma, \delta, and the triple trade-off Increasing \delta → easy detection (p_\delta \to 1) but degraded text (the choice is biased toward greens even when a red was the “right” word); decreasing \gamma → rare green lists, a sharper signal but a stiffer constraint on quality.

In production one targets \gamma = 0.25-0.5, \delta = 1-2: imperceptible quality loss (evaluated by perplexity and human tests), z > 4 reached from T \approx 50-100. The same bipolar tuning signal quality / law fidelity structures audio watermarking (WavMark, AudioSeal — cf. Speech Technology module ch. 9): two modalities, one theory.

7. Controlling diffusions: CFG, ControlNet, inversion

Control of diffusion models was introduced in M1 (Multimodal GenAI module ch. 4); here we give the short proof of classifier-free guidance, then the two industrial standards of spatial control (ControlNet) and editing (inversion, which no M1 module covers). The box below recalls the DDPM notation for students who met diffusion in a Deep Learning course rather than in Multimodal GenAI.

Recap — DDPM in one page Forward process. A fixed chain adds Gaussian noise in T steps, with a variance schedule \beta_t and \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \leq t}(1 - \beta_s). Its marginal is explicit, so a noisy sample is drawn in one shot: x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). With T \approx 1000 steps and \bar\alpha_T \approx 0, the last state is almost pure Gaussian noise.

Training. A network \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) predicts the noise that was added, for a step t drawn uniformly; the simplified variational bound of Ho et al. is a regression \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, t, \epsilon}\, \big\| \epsilon - \hat\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon,\ t\big) \big\|^2 . The optimal predictor is the posterior mean of the noise, which is proportional to the score, \hat\epsilon(x_t, t) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t), the parametrization used in theorem 7.1.

Sampling. Start from x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) and denoise step by step, with \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t and z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I): x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big) + \sigma_t z , with, for instance, \sigma_t^2 = \beta_t; each of the T steps costs one network evaluation.

DDIM reuses the same trained network with a deterministic step: it predicts the clean image, then re-noises it to the level t-1, \hat x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \qquad x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \hat x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t). It runs on a subsequence of 20 to 50 steps, and the same initial noise always gives the same image: inversion (proposition 7.3) exploits exactly this determinism.

Conditioning. The network also takes a condition c, replaced by a null token during part of the training: one network then gives both \hat\epsilon_c and \hat\epsilon_u, the two ingredients of classifier-free guidance. Full treatment: Multimodal GenAI ch. 3–4, Deep Learning P2 Definition 7.10, Deep Learning ch. 9.

7.1. CFG: the short proof

Theorem 7.1 — Classifier guidance and CFG are the same exponential reweighting Let \hat\epsilon_c, \hat\epsilon_u denote the noises predicted with/without conditioning, and \hat\epsilon_w = \hat\epsilon_u + w\,(\hat\epsilon_c - \hat\epsilon_u) the CFG prediction. Since \hat\epsilon(x_t, t) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t) (score parametrization), the CFG combination realizes \nabla_{x_t} \log p_w(x_t) \;=\; \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t) + w\, \nabla_{x_t} \log p(c \mid x_t) \;=\; \nabla_{x_t} \log \big[p(x_t \mid c)^w \, p(x_t)^{1-w}\big], that is, sampling from the reweighted law

Key result — CFG samples a reweighted law p_w \propto p(x_t \mid c)^w p(x_t)^{1-w}

— exactly the family of theorem 5.1 (RLHF) with w \leftrightarrow 1/\beta.

Proof. Linearity of the score: \hat\epsilon_w = (1 - w)\hat\epsilon_u + w \hat\epsilon_c (rewrite the combination); each term is proportional to the gradient of the corresponding log-density, so the sum is the gradient of the sum of the logs.

For the conditional term, Bayes’ rule p(x_t \mid c) \propto p(c \mid x_t)\, p(x_t) gives \nabla \log p(x_t \mid c)^w p(x_t)^{1-w} = w\nabla\log p(c \mid x_t) + \nabla \log p(x_t) — which is precisely the formula of classifier guidance with weight w: CFG obtains it without a classifier, by training the same network with conditioning dropout.

  • At w = 1: pure conditional model;
  • w > 1: over-guidance (fidelity \uparrow, diversity \downarrow, color saturation — the classic visual symptom);
  • w < 1: interpolation toward the unconditional. ∎

7.2. ControlNet: spatial control, without breaking the pre-training

Theorem 7.2 — ControlNet architecture (Zhang, Rao & Agrawala, ICCV 2023) Let F(x ; \Theta) be a frozen UNet block (operation on the activation x of layer i). ControlNet branches off a trainable copy initialized at \Theta and locks the output with two null convolutions (zero-convolutions): y \;=\; F(x;\Theta) \;+\; Z_2\Big(F_c\big(Z_1(c;\Theta_{z_1});\ \Theta_c\big);\ \Theta_{z_2}\Big), where c is the control map (Canny edges, depth, skeletal pose…); Z_i is a 1\times1 convolution with zero weights at initialization.

Proof.

  • Identity property at the start. At t = 0: Z_2(\cdot) = W_2 \, h = 0 since W_2 = 0 — the branch outputs exactly zero, and the composed model is identically the pre-trained model: fine-tuning starts from the initial quality, without “catastrophic shift”.

  • The gradient does not vanish. The derivative of the output with respect to W_2 equals \partial y / \partial W_2 = h_c \neq 0 (the copy F_c, initialized at \Theta, produces nonzero activations).

    The first update pulls W_2 away from zero, which then unlocks the gradients toward F_c and Z_1 (which were zero as long as W_2 = 0, by the chain rule).

Compare the naive alternative (branching F_c directly): at step 0, uncontrolled random noise is added to the activations of the frozen UNet and destroys the coherence of the output for hundreds of steps. ∎

Why a “copy” and not a “new network” The copy inherits the pre-trained features: control learns the correspondence control map → existing rich features, not vision from scratch — hence the data efficiency (a few thousand pairs suffice for a new type of control). This is a general pattern of the module: condition around a frozen model and protect the interface with an identity initialization (the mask of ch. 3 is the exact analogue for discrete slots: allow only what preserves the invariant).

7.3. Editing a real image: inversion and null-text

Proposition 7.3 — DDIM inversion and its failure under CFG DDIM defines a deterministic step linking x_t and x_{t-1} via the prediction \hat x_0(x_t, c) (recap box above; Multimodal GenAI Construction 4.2). Inversion, new in this module, traverses the relation backwards to recover the noise that generates a given real image, approximating the noise prediction at x_{t+1} by the one at x_t: x_{t+1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t+1}}\, \hat x_0(x_t, c) + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t+1}}\; \hat\epsilon_c(x_t, c).

With CFG, the applied “noise” depends on a w-combination of the conditional/unconditional predictions; inversion becomes an implicit equation (the step depends on x_t through two networks): error accumulates, the reconstruction drifts.

Null-text inversion (Mokady et al., CVPR 2023): keep the inversion trajectory as an anchor and optimize, step by step, the null condition embedding c_\varnothing^{(t)} to minimize \|\hat x_0\big(x_t; c, c_\varnothing^{(t)}\big) - x_0\|^2 — a few dozen gradient steps per image.

Editing (Prompt-to-Prompt: substituting the cross-attention maps; InstructPix2Pix: learning editing on synthetic data) then operates along this faithful trajectory.

9. Evaluating control: fidelity versus diversity

Controlling without measuring is believing. This chapter sets the protocol: two axes (fidelity, diversity), one curve (the strength sweep), one decomposition (precision/recall), and a bound showing that the trade-off is structural, not an engineering defect.

Definition 9.1 — Fidelity and diversity Control fidelity: \mathrm{Fid}(S) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[y_i \in S] (pass rate; or a continuous metric — property score, similarity to the prompt).

Diversity: mean intra-batch distance \mathrm{Div} = \frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j} d(y_i, y_j) (self-BLEU for text, LPIPS distance for images, Tanimoto distance between fingerprints for molecules — Graph Generative Models ch. 6, period 2); or entropy of the effective law.

Quality in the data-fidelity sense: FID (recall of the Multimodal GenAI module ch. 7), perplexity under a reference model.

Theorem 9.2 — The trade-off is structural (entropy bound) Let S \subset \mathcal{Y}^* be the admissible set (|S| = m finite; for sequences, consider outputs of length \leq L over a vocabulary of size k: m \leq k^L, the whole space).

Every law \tilde p supported on S satisfies

Key result — entropy ceiling of a constrained law H(\tilde p) \;\leq\; \log m \;=\; H_{\max}(\mathcal{Y}^{\leq L}) \;-\; \log\frac{k^L}{m}.

Proof. Entropy is maximal for the uniform law (Gibbs’ inequality: for every p on S, \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, \mathrm{Unif}_S) = \log m - H(p) \geq 0, with equality iff uniform).

Support constraint → entropy ceiling lowered by \log(k^L/m): each division of the number of admissible outputs by e costs 1 nat of maximal diversity.

Guidance acts likewise but smoothly: p_w \propto p^{1-w}\mathrm{score}(c)^w concentrates mass on the modes compatible with c — the effective entropy decreases monotonically in the usual cases (theorems 5.1 and 7.1: the KL from the start is exactly the price paid); hence the curve. ∎

Proposition 9.3 — The control curve (protocol) For each strength w \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\} (or the watermark’s \delta, steering’s \alpha, RLHF’s \beta^{-1}): generate a fixed batch (same seeds), measure (\mathrm{Fid}(w), \mathrm{Div}(w)), plot the trajectory.

Reading:

  1. a method dominates if its curve is above/to the right of another’s everywhere;
  2. the “elbow” (fidelity stops growing, diversity collapses) is the recommended tuning point;
  3. complete with the precision/recall decomposition (Kynkäänniemi et al., NeurIPS 2019): precision measures the quality of the occupied modes, recall the coverage of the data’s modes — over-guidance kills recall (abandoned modes) before precision.

Classic anti-protocol: plot only fidelity (“100% valid JSON!”) and keep silent that the model now emits only three templates — mode collapse, control version.

Example 9.4 — Toy curve, by hand Law over 4 outputs with probabilities (0.4,\ 0.3,\ 0.2,\ 0.1); exponential control p_w \propto p\, e^{w c} with score c = (3,\ 3,\ 0,\ 0), so that the admissible set is S = \{1, 2\} (m = 2).

  • At w=0: entropy 1.28 nats, fidelity \mathrm{Fid} = p(S) = 0.70.
  • At w=1: weights \propto (8.03,\ 6.03,\ 0.2,\ 0.1) \to probabilities (0.56,\ 0.42,\ 0.014,\ 0.007), entropy 0.78 nats, fidelity 0.979.
  • At w=2: weights (161.4,\ 121.0,\ 0.2,\ 0.1) \to (0.571,\ 0.428,\ 0.0007,\ 0.0004), entropy 0.69 nats, fidelity 0.999.

As w \to \infty the law tends to (4/7,\ 3/7,\ 0,\ 0): the score does not separate the two admissible outputs, so their ratio 4:3 is preserved and the entropy tends to 0.683 nats, just below the ceiling \log m = \log 2 \approx 0.693 of theorem 9.2.

Fidelity is bought with entropy, and the price is paid almost entirely between w = 0 and w = 1. Generalize in exercise 4.

10. Attacks on control and robustness

Control is a promise; this chapter tests the promise. Four surfaces, in the module’s order: the mask, steering/RLHF, the watermark, spatial control.

Theorem 10.1 — Attacking constrained decoding (hostile content inside the format) The mask guarantees y \in S — syntax, never semantics. An injected prompt can fill a perfectly valid JSON with {"instruction": "ignore the previous rules"}; a constrained tool call can be diverted into hostile parameters (prompt injection in agents: cf. Explainability module ch. 5).

Second: the complexity of the grammar is a surface — an ambiguous grammar explodes the number of states (subset construction 2^{|Q|}) and allows a denial of service of the control itself; finally the tokenizer interaction: changing the tokenizer invalidates the state→tokens index (recompilation).

Defenses: downstream semantic validation (schema + bounds: \delta-constraints beyond the regular), state-size limits, server-side pre-compiled grammars (defense-in-depth pattern of the Explainability module ch. 6).

Proposition 10.2 — Attacks on the watermark (and impossibility results)

  1. Edits: paraphrase, synonym substitution, emoji/homoglyph insertion, LLM rewriting — every exchange of a green token for a red one costs \approx 1/\sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)} in z; to bring \mathbb{E}[z] = 6 below 2 one must rewrite a substantial fraction of the tokens, at the price of quality;
  2. Spoofing: can an attacker without the key mark an innocent text to accuse a provider? Public-key schemes and the reliability analysis (Kirchenbauer et al., ICLR 2024 — “On the Reliability”; already referenced in the LLMs module) quantify these false accusations: key management becomes the real problem (revocation, multi-keys, aggregated tests);
  3. Undetectability: Sadasivan et al. (2023) show that a good enough paraphraser drives every detector toward chance; Christ, Gunn & Zamir (2024) show that a watermark can be made undetectable without the key, but their construction does not survive substantial edits: invisibility and robustness pull in opposite directions.

Operational conclusion: a watermark is a provenance marker, not a cryptographic proof of origin; stacking it (text + audio + image, multi-key styles) is the realistic strategy.

Attacks on guidance and steering, in brief Steering (ch. 4) has no support guarantee: a behavior removed on a test prompt reappears out of distribution (jailbreaks by rephrasing, other languages, encodings); RLHF is vulnerable to reward hacking (ch. 5) and preference attacks; CFG/ControlNet: adversarial prompts (forbidden contents rephrased), image injections (diverted inpainting, editing of persons — deepfakes), hence mandatory marking (theorem 1.3) and downstream moderation. A vast program crossing the Explainability (LLM security) and Responsible AI modules.

Definition 10.3 — Robustness budget (the quantity to remember) For a fidelity control c and a family of attacks \mathcal A, one defines the degradation

Key result — robustness budget \Delta(w) = \mathrm{Fid}(w) - \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mathcal A}[\mathrm{Fid}(w, a)].

A robust control has flat \Delta in w; the evaluation of ch. 9 must therefore be repeated under attack — the complete protocol fits in one sentence: plot the fidelity/diversity curve, with and without an adversary, at fixed quality budget.

    Then: (c) plot the power of the test as a function of T \in [20, 500]; (d) derive the minimal T for power 0.9 (compare with example 6.3); (e) repeat with \gamma = 0.25 and conclude on the tuning.

    Proof. Guidance:

    • (a) the distribution of z sticks to \mathcal N(0,1) from T \geq 50 (De Moivre-Laplace);
    • (b) p_\delta depends on the standard deviation of the logits; with logits \mathcal N(0,1), p_\delta \approx 0.75;
    • (c) sigmoidal power in \sqrt{T};

  4. ★★ Theoretical fidelity/diversity frontier. Prove the entropy bound 9.2 (Gibbs), then generalize example 9.4: arbitrary initial law, score c, plot (\mathrm{Fid}(w), H(w)) for w \in [0, 10] and verify that the curve converges to \big(\max c,\ H(p \mid \arg\max c)\big) — the corner (\max c, 0) when the maximizer of c is unique (in example 9.4 it is not, hence the limit 0.683 nats). What happens to the curve if c is noisy?

    Proof. Guidance: normalize p_w \propto p\, e^{w c} (exponential family) and write A(w) = \log \sum_y p(y) e^{w c(y)}, so that A'(w) = \mathbb{E}_w[c] and \frac{d}{dw}\mathbb{E}_w[f] = \mathrm{Cov}_w(f, c).

    Differentiating H(w) = -\mathbb{E}_w[\log p] - w\,\mathbb{E}_w[c] + A(w) gives dH/dw = -\mathrm{Cov}_w(\log p, c) - w\,\mathrm{Var}_w(c); for a uniform initial law the first term vanishes and H decreases for w > 0, otherwise it can rise briefly when c favors outputs that p makes rare.

    With noise, a plateau appears before the collapse: the analyst chooses the elbow. ∎

  5. ★★ Zero-convolution: the gradient under the magnifier.     Proof. Guidance:

    • (a) Z_2 = W_2 h = 0;
    • (b) \partial y/\partial W_2 = h_c \neq 0 (activations of the copy initialized at \Theta);
    • (c) chain rule through W_2 = 0, unlocked as soon as W_2’s first update happens;
    • (d) the variant without zero-conv starts from a high loss and destroys the frozen model’s output — the experiment reproduces the motivation figure of the ControlNet paper. ∎

Q1 (4 pts) — Masked decoding is not conditioning. Vocabulary \{a, b\}, outputs of two tokens, constraint S = \{aa, ba, bb\}, and the model p(a) = p(b) = \tfrac12, \qquad p(a \mid a) = 0.2, \quad p(b \mid a) = 0.8, \qquad p(a \mid b) = p(b \mid b) = \tfrac12 . (a) Give the law of masked decoding on S. (b) Give the exact conditional law. (c) How many draws does rejection sampling need on average, and which weight turns masked samples into exact ones?

Proof. Marking points

    1. Step 1 masks nothing, since both letters can still reach S. After a only a survives (Z_2 = 0.2) and is renormalized to 1. Masked law: aa 0.5, ba 0.25, bb 0.25 (1.5 pts).
    1. The constraint has probability 0.1 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.6, so the exact law is aa 1/6 ≈ 0.167, ba and bb 5/12 ≈ 0.417 each (1 pt).
    1. The number of draws is geometric with success probability 0.6, hence 1/0.6 ≈ 1.67 draws on average (0.5 pt). The weight is the product of the normalizers: 0.2 for aa, 1 for ba and bb. Self-normalized, 0.5 × 0.2 : 0.25 : 0.25 gives back 1/6, 5/12, 5/12 (1 pt). Common error: calling the mask exact because every output lies in S (theorem 2.2).

Q2 (4 pts) — The closed form of RLHF. (a) Show that the maximizer of the first objective below is the law on the right: \max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big), \qquad \pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta}}{Z(x)} . (b) Three answers have reference probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and rewards 0, 1, 2; with \beta = 1, compute \pi^*. (c) Describe the limits \beta \to \infty and \beta \to 0, and name the quantity of classifier-free guidance that plays the role of 1/\beta.

Proof. Marking points

    1. Rewrite the objective as -\beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*) + \beta \log Z(x) ; the KL is nonnegative and vanishes only at \pi = \pi^*, which gives the maximizer and its uniqueness (2 pts). The first-order derivation of theorem 5.1 is accepted.
    1. Weights (0.5,\ 0.3e,\ 0.2e^2) = (0.5,\ 0.815,\ 1.478), normalizer 2.793, so \pi^* \approx (0.179,\ 0.292,\ 0.529) (1 pt).
    1. A large \beta gives back the reference law; a small \beta puts all the mass on the answer of largest reward (the third). The guidance strength w of theorem 7.1 plays the role of 1/\beta (1 pt). Common error: forgetting the normalizer Z(x), or multiplying the reward by \beta.

Q3 (4 pts) — Watermark test and its power. The green fraction is \gamma = 0.25, and a suspect text has 100 tokens, 40 of them green.

  1. Compute z and decide at level 10^{-3}, whose quantile is 3.09. (b) A marked text draws a green token with probability p_\delta = 0.4: compute the expected z for 100 tokens, then the length needed for a power of 0.9 at the threshold z = 4 (the 0.9 quantile is 1.28). (c) What happens to this length if p_\delta - \gamma is halved?

Proof. Marking points

    1. The score is above the threshold, so H0 is rejected and the text is flagged, with a p-value of about 2.7 \times 10^{-4} (1 pt): z = \frac{40 - 25}{\sqrt{100 \times 0.25 \times 0.75}} = \frac{15}{4.33} \approx 3.46 > 3.09 .
    1. Expected score 0.15 \times 10 / 0.433 \approx 3.46 (0.5 pt). With the length formula of theorem 6.2 (1.5 pts), T \approx \left(\frac{4 \times 0.433 + 1.28 \times 0.490}{0.15}\right)^2 \approx 15.7^2 \approx 247 \text{ tokens}.
    1. The length scales as (p_\delta - \gamma)^{-2}: it is multiplied by 4 (1 pt). Common errors: the H1 standard deviation used under H0, or a two-sided quantile for this one-sided test.

Q4 (4 pts) — Hard constraint or guidance. A law p = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) on three outputs has the admissible set S = \{2, 3\}.

  1. Give the law renormalized on S and its entropy, and compare it with the ceiling of theorem 9.2. (b) For the exponential guidance p_w \propto p\, e^{w c} with score c = (0, 1, 1) and w = 1, compute the law, the fidelity p_w(S) and the entropy. (c) Is the entropy monotone in w? Justify.

Proof. Marking points

    1. The law (0, 0.6, 0.4) has entropy 0.673 nats, below the ceiling \log 2 \approx 0.693 (1 pt).
    1. Weights (0.5, 0.815, 0.544) with sum 1.859, law (0.269, 0.439, 0.292), fidelity 0.731 (against 0.5 without guidance), entropy 1.074 nats (1.5 pts).
    1. No: the entropy of p is 1.030 nats, so guidance first raises it. With the derivative of Ex. 4, \frac{dH}{dw} = -\mathrm{Cov}_w(\log p, c) - w\, \mathrm{Var}_w(c), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}_p(\log p, c) \approx -0.168 , the slope at w = 0 is positive: mass moves from the most likely output to rarer admissible ones. As w \to \infty, the law tends to that of (a) (1.5 pts). Common error: “guidance always lowers diversity”.

Q5 (4 pts) — Diagnosis. A team plugs a JSON-schema mask into its agent and reports: “100 % valid tool calls, so control is solved, and it is free.” (a) Give two measurements that test “free”. (b) Give one failure that the mask cannot prevent, and the defense of ch. 10.

Proof. Marking points

    1. Any two of (1 pt each): the fidelity/diversity curve with and without the mask, since reporting fidelity alone is the anti-protocol of proposition 9.3; the distortion of theorem 2.2, by comparing masked outputs with rejection sampling on a sample of prompts, or by the spread of the weights \prod_t Z_t; the time overhead per token and the one-off cost of compiling the index.
    1. The mask guarantees syntax, never semantics: a valid call can carry an injected instruction or a wrong value (a date 31/02), and an ambiguous grammar can blow up the automaton (1 pt). Defense: downstream semantic validation with bounds, limits on the number of states, server-side precompiled grammars (1 pt).
