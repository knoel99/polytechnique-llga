1. Tokenization

Before a model sees any text, a tokenizer cuts it into units from a fixed vocabulary; this chapter explains how that vocabulary is built and why the choice weighs on cost and quality.

A concrete scene: send the same prompt in French and in English to a Llama-style tokenizer. The French version typically costs 15–25% more tokens, so your context budget shrinks, and a rare word splits into six subwords — the model “understands” less and bills more.

This is why BPE, WordPiece and Unigram come before the architecture.

What breaks Mixing train/inference tokenizers; ignoring multilingual cost (latency + $); treating UNK as a detail. All three sabotage a prod RAG as much as a nanoGPT lab.

Definition 1.1 — Byte-level BPE: the exact construction BPE (proved step by step in NLP ch. 1) starts here from the alphabet of 256 bytes: any Unicode string can be represented without an “unknown” symbol. Learning loop: \mathrm{merge}^{(k)} = \arg\max_{(a,b)}\; f(a,b),\qquad f(a,b)=\sum_{w}\mathrm{freq}(w)\,n_{(a,b)}(w), where n_{(a,b)}(w) counts the occurrences of the adjacent pair (a,b) in word w (counting weighted by word frequency). The merge is applied everywhere and one starts again until the vocabulary \mathcal{V} reaches its target size |\mathcal{V}| (32k-128k).

Encoding a new text replays the merges in the learned order (GPT-2/4, Llama). WordPiece (BERT) maximizes \frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)f(y)}; Unigram (T5) learns a generative model by EM then segments with Viterbi (NLP ch. 1.2).

Vocabulary, compression, multilingual costs The compression rate (characters/token) depends on the vocabulary and the language: vocabularies trained on English compress English well (~4 characters/token) but degrade other languages.

Orders of magnitude measured in the lab: with GPT/Llama-type tokenizers, the same French text costs typically 15 to 25% more tokens than in English; for poorly represented non-Latin scripts, the factor can reach 3× or more (each rarely seen character decomposes into bytes).

Direct consequences: API cost (billed per token), context length consumed, and inequality of quality.

Llama 3 raised the vocabulary to 128k tokens — a large part of the gain serves non-English languages; GPT-4o (o200k) claims up to ~1.4× to ~4× fewer tokens depending on the language compared to the previous generation. Exercise 1 measures all this on your own texts.

Special tokens and security Special tokens (<|endoftext|>, <|im_start|>, template roles) delimit turns and documents. If the decoder accepts them in user input, an attacker can inject a fake dialogue turn or prematurely terminate a document (boundary confusion).

Defense: systematically re-encode input and neutralize special tokens (encode_ordinary, the allowed_special flag of tiktoken), never raw string concatenation around the prompt. This class of attacks returns in ch. 9 (prompt injections).

import tiktoken
enc = tiktoken.encoding_for_model("gpt-4o")
okapi = tiktoken.encoding_for_model("gpt-3.5-turbo")   # cl100k vocabulary

fr = "Les graphes pour les LLMs : une révolution pour la recherche d'information."
de = "Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaftskapitän"
for s in (fr, de, "1234 + 5678 = ?"):
    print(len(okapi.encode(s)), "vs", len(enc.encode(s)), "->", s[:40])

# "safe" tokenization: special tokens are NOT decoded by default
b = enc.encode("end <|endoftext|> next", allowed_special=set())
print("ids :", b, "| decode round-trip ok :", enc.decode(b))

Why it matters (M1 recall) Tokenization governs cost (length in tokens), biases (fuzzy arithmetic because “123 + 456” tokenizes poorly), multilinguality (the extra cost measured above) and real vulnerabilities (special tokens). Systematic diagnosis in the lab with tiktoken / tokenizers. For adapting an LLM to a low-resource language — vocabulary, template, data — ch. 10 presents Shang’s work (Atlas-Chat, Nile-Chat).

2. The architecture in depth

A language model must turn a sequence of token embeddings into a distribution over the next token, at a cost that stays manageable for long sequences. This chapter dissects the decoder-only transformer that does this in every modern LLM, and the design choices (normalization, positions, attention heads, experts) that make it trainable and cheap to run.

Definition 2.1 — The decoder block An LLM stacks n_{\text{layers}} blocks: x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{LN}(x)), \qquad x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(x)), with causal attention (triangular mask: token i sees only j \leq i), grouped heads (grouped-query attention for inference), a gated MLP (SwiGLU: three matrices with a hidden size of about 8d/3, so that the MLP keeps about 8d^2 parameters, as a plain 4× MLP would), RoPE positional embeddings (rotations; relative generalization to long contexts), and a final LN.

Proposition 2.2 — Pre-norm vs post-norm: the gradient flow Post-LN (original transformer): x_{l+1}=\mathrm{LN}(x_l+F(x_l)) — each block renormalizes the residual itself. The gradient of x_L to x_0 crosses L LN Jacobians whose product decays/explodes exponentially with L: warmup mandatory, fragile depth.

Pre-LN (GPT-2+, all modern LLMs): x_{l+1}=x_l+F(\mathrm{LN}(x_l)) — the residual path is the identity: \frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_l} = I + \sum_{l'\,>\,l}\frac{\partial F_{l'}}{\partial x_l} → a gradient that reaches every layer without multiplicative attenuation: stable training without fine warmup.

Price: the norm \|x_l\| grows with depth (additive contributions), hence the final LN. (Xiong et al., ICML 2020.)

Theorem 2.3 — RoPE derived (Su et al., 2021) Position is injected by rotation: the dimension-d vector is viewed as d/2 2D pairs; pair i of the token at position m undergoes R(m\theta_i) with \theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}: R(\varphi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}, \qquad q^{(i)}_m = R(m\theta_i)\,q^{(i)},\quad k^{(i)}_n = R(n\theta_i)\,k^{(i)}.

Proof. Relative property: the dot product of a pair depends only on the difference of positions: \langle q^{(i)}_m,k^{(i)}_n\rangle = q^{(i)\top} R(m\theta_i)^\top R(n\theta_i)\, k^{(i)} = q^{(i)\top} R\big((n-m)\theta_i\big)\,k^{(i)}, since 2D rotations commute and R(a)^\top R(b)=R(b-a).

Summing over pairs = an attention score depending on (n-m) and the contents — a “soft” generalization of relative embeddings.

Practical bonus: to extend the context, one interpolates the angles (m\to m/L) instead of relearning. ∎

Proposition 2.4 — MQA/GQA: the memory arithmetic of the KV-cache The cache stores the keys and values of all past tokens. With n_{\text{layers}} layers, n_{\text{kv}} key/value heads of dimension d_{\text{head}}, b bytes per element and T cached tokens: M_{\text{KV}} = 2\ (\text{K and V}) \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot b \cdot T \ \text{bytes}.

Multi-Query Attention (Shazeer, 2019): a single shared KV head. Grouped-Query (Ainslie et al., 2023): n_{\text{kv}} < n_{\text{heads}}.

Example — Llama-3-8B: 32 heads, 8 KV heads, 32 layers, d_{\text{head}}=128, fp16: 2\times32\times8\times128\times2 = 128\,\text{KiB/token}. A 100k-token context ≈ 12.2 GiB (13.1 GB) of cache, versus about 49 GiB with full multi-head attention — a 4× reduction for nearly identical quality.

This is THE lever of long context (ch. 6).

Key result — KV-cache memory M_{\text{KV}} = 2 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot b \cdot T \ \text{bytes}

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): routing and balancing Each MLP block is replaced by n_E experts \mathrm{FFN}_1,\dots,\mathrm{FFN}_{n_E}; a router g(x)=\mathrm{softmax}(W_g x) activates only the top-k: y = \sum_{i\in \mathrm{top}\text{-}k} g_i(x)\,\mathrm{FFN}_i(x), \qquad \#\text{active params} \ll \#\text{total params}. Capacity decorrelated from inference cost (Switch Transformer: Fedus et al. 2021; DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, ~37B active).

Trap: routing collapse — the winning experts attract everything. Auxiliary balancing loss (Switch): \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha_{\text{aux}}\, n_E \sum_{i=1}^{n_E} f_i\,P_i, f_i = fraction of the k\cdot T routing slots of a batch of T tokens sent to expert i, P_i=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t g_i(x_t) = mean router probability.

Since \sum_i f_i=\sum_i P_i=1, balanced routing (f_i=P_i=1/n_E) gives \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}=\alpha_{\text{aux}}, the minimum over routings with f_i=P_i (Ex. 9, by Cauchy–Schwarz). The loss never reaches zero. On these routings the router’s confidence plays no role: only the balance is rewarded. Without f_i=P_i, balance is not the minimum in general (Ex. 9).

Modern variants: fine experts + shared experts, balancing without an auxiliary loss (dynamically adjusted bias, DeepSeek-V3).

Efficient attention: FlashAttention and IO complexity Exact attention costs \mathcal{O}(n^2) compute but is dominated in practice by input/output: the naive implementation writes the n\times n matrix to HBM (large, slow memory).

FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022): exact tiled computation that fits in SRAM, “online” softmax with an accumulator (running max m and sum \ell updated per tile): m_{\text{new}}=\max(m,x), \qquad \ell_{\text{new}}=e^{m-m_{\text{new}}}\ell+e^{x-m_{\text{new}}}. The n^2 matrix is never materialized; HBM reads are reduced from \Theta(n^2) to \Theta(n^2d^2/M) (M = SRAM size).

The general lesson: optimize memory access, not just FLOPs (the GPU memory hierarchy, HBM versus SRAM, is in Deep Learning ch. 6.3).

Beyond: SSM/Mamba (state equation sketch) A state-space model discretizes a linear system x'(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t),\ y=Cx: x_k = \bar A x_{k-1} + \bar B u_k,\qquad y_k = C x_k,\qquad \bar A = e^{\Delta A},\ \bar B \approx \Delta B, computable in \mathcal{O}(n) by a scan (no n^2 matrix).

The selectivity of Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023): \bar B, C, \Delta depend on the input → content-dependent (selective forgetting), which yields attention quality at linear cost.

SSD duality (Mamba-2, 2024): the same computation rewrites as y_k=\sum_{s\le k} C_k^\top\big(\prod_{s<k'}\bar A\big)\bar B_s\,u_s — an “attention” with a causal kernel; hence the attention+SSM hybrid architectures. Continued in ch. 8.

  • Parameters: where are they? embedding (|\mathcal{V}| \times d), attention (4d^2/block), MLP (8d^2/block: two matrices with a 4× expansion, or three SwiGLU matrices with hidden size \approx 8d/3; Llama 3 widens it to 3.5d, about 10.5d^2) — the rule \#\text{params} \approx 12\, n_{\text{layers}}\, d^2 and the training memory cost (parameters + Adam + activations + gradients: ~20 bytes/parameter in mixed precision; cf. ch. 3 for actual compute);
  • LLGA bridge: attention is message passing on a token graph (Graph ML module ch. 5, Advanced GNN); graph transformers inherit from it.

3. Pre-training

Pre-training gives the model its knowledge of language by training it on trillions of tokens of raw text. This chapter answers three questions: which objective to optimize, which data to feed, and how to split a fixed compute budget between model size and data size.

Pre-training objectives

  • Causal modeling (CLM) — GPT: \mathcal{L} = -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) — predict the next token; generative by construction;
  • Masked modeling / denoising (MLM) — BERT (NLP ch. 7): mask ~15% and predict (bidirectional); excellent for encoding (classification, retrieval), does not generate;
  • Span corruption / text-to-text — T5: mask spans and regenerate them; everything is formulated as sequence→sequence.

3.1. The data pipeline: quality, filtering, deduplication

  • Curation: web → rule-based filtering (length, code/punctuation ratio, languages), then model-based filtering (quality classifiers trained on reference pages — e.g. distilled judges); controlled mixture (web, code, books, articles, mathematics, synthetic data generated then filtered);
  • MinHash-LSH deduplication (proved in NLP ch. 8.3): k-gram shingles → MinHash signatures (a min-hash collision has probability equal to the Jaccard similarity) → LSH bands/ranks with \mathbb{P}_{\text{detect}}(J)=1-(1-J^r)^b → remove near-duplicates down to a threshold. At scale: tens of TB of text, approximately linear clustering; measured effect: less memorization, better benchmark (deduplicating is regularizing — LLMs learn duplicates “twice”);
  • Mixtures and curriculum: per-domain proportions (annealing: raise quality/lower web at the end of training); easy→hard ordering (by perplexity) improves compute efficiency — demonstrated at scale by Shang (ch. 10, “Beyond Random Sampling”);
  • Multilingual: balancing per language (otherwise the tokenizer and the model drift toward English — cf. ch. 1 and 10).

3.2. Scaling laws: decomposition and the Chinchilla optimum

Combined law (Hoffmann et al. 2022, “Chinchilla”) The loss of a model with N parameters trained on D tokens follows, over decades of scale: L(N,D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, E = irreducible entropy of natural text; the other two terms = under-capacity and exhausted data (fitted on ~400 training runs; Kaplan et al. 2020 for the L(N)-only version). Reading: one must grow N and D together.

Theorem 3.1 — The compute optimum, derived At fixed budget C, with \mathrm{FLOPs}(C) \approx 6ND (2 per parameter × forward + backward, × D tokens): (N^\star, D^\star) = \arg\min_{N,D}\; L(N,D)\quad \text{s.t.}\quad 6ND = C.

Proof. Lagrangian: \mathcal{J}=E+AN^{-\alpha}+BD^{-\beta}-\lambda(6ND-C). Stationarity: \frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial N}= -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1}-6\lambda D=0, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial D}= -\beta B D^{-\beta-1}-6\lambda N=0. Dividing side by side, \lambda cancels: \frac{\alpha A\,N^{-\alpha-1}}{\beta B\,D^{-\beta-1}} = \frac{D}{N} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\,D^{\beta} = N^{\alpha} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{N^{\alpha}}{D^{\beta}} = \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}.

The condition together with 6ND=C gives N^\star\propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}, \qquad D^\star\propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}, that is N^\star\propto C^{0.46} and D^\star\propto C^{0.54} with the fitted exponents (\alpha\approx0.34, \beta\approx0.28): both grow roughly like \sqrt{C}.

The ratio D^\star/N^\star grows like C^{(\alpha-\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)}\approx C^{0.1}, so it is not a universal constant, and its value is very sensitive to the fitted constants.

Plugged into this formula, the published parametric fit of Hoffmann et al. (the exponents above, A\approx406, B\approx411) gives about 50 tokens per parameter at 10^{21} FLOPs and about 90 at Chinchilla’s budget.

The rule D^\star\approx 20\,N^\star comes from their two other estimation methods (fixed model sizes and IsoFLOP profiles), and a refit of the parametric law on their data (Besiroglu et al., 2024) recovers about 20 over the budgets of interest.

Either way, GPT-3 (300B tokens for 175B params, ratio 1.7) was “undertrained” at its budget. ∎

Key result — the Chinchilla optimum 6ND = C, \qquad D^\star \approx 20\,N^\star

Compute and real costs (Llama 3, 2024) Orders of magnitude from the Llama 3 technical report (H100 training): 8B / 15T tokens ≈ 1.5M GPU-hours; 70B ≈ 7M; 405B / 15.6T tokens ≈ 30.8M GPU-hours (≈ 3,500 GPU-years, about 4 months on 10⁴ H100s).

At ~2-3 $/GPU-hour, the 405B costs ~60-90M$ of compute. This is why one “continues” from checkpoints rather than retraining (ch. 4.3: LoRA, distillation).

It is also why the post-Chinchilla ratio went up: since inference costs daily, one deliberately overtrains small models (Llama-3-8B: 15T tokens ≈ 1900 tokens per parameter, ~100× Chinchilla) — the optimum depends on what is being optimized: the training cost (Chinchilla) or the total cost including inference.

Lab: training a mini-GPT On a Shakespeare-type corpus (~1 MB), train a ~10M-parameter transformer (nanoGPT): 30 minutes of GPU suffice to generate credible text. Observe: the loss curve, the effect of context, the sampling temperature (ch. 6). Extend: do exercise 8 (check the law L(N,D) by regression on the published points).

4. Post-training: prompting, SFT and PEFT

A pre-trained model only continues text; it does not follow instructions or hold a conversation. This chapter goes from the cheapest way to adapt it to the most invasive one.

It starts with prompting, which changes no weight (4.0), then supervised fine-tuning (4.1) and the reason a preference stage is still needed (4.2, continued in chapter 5). It ends with LoRA, QLoRA and distillation, which make fine-tuning affordable (4.3).

4.0. Prompting and in-context learning

Before changing any weight, one can change the input. A well-prompted model is the zero-cost baseline that every fine-tuning must beat.

Definition 4.1 — Zero-shot, few-shot, in-context learning A zero-shot prompt states the task and the query x. A k-shot prompt prepends k demonstrations: \text{prompt}_k = (x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k, x), \qquad \hat y = \arg\max_y\, p_\theta(y \mid \text{prompt}_k). The weights \theta do not change: the model picks up the task from its context alone. This is in-context learning (ICL), observed at scale with GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020).

  • Order and format sensitivity: with the same demonstrations, some orders give near state-of-the-art accuracy and others near chance (Lu et al., 2022). Models favour the most frequent and the last label of the prompt; contextual calibration rescales by the probabilities given to a content-free input such as “N/A” (Zhao et al., 2021).
  • What the demonstrations teach: the format, the label space and the input distribution matter more than whether the demonstration labels are correct (Min et al., 2022).
  • Chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022): demonstrations that show intermediate steps, or simply “Let’s think step by step” (Kojima et al., 2022), raise accuracy on multi-step problems for large models. Sampling several chains and voting is self-consistency (RLVR box of ch. 5); training the model to produce good chains with verifiable rewards is GRPO (ch. 5).
  • Practice: a system prompt for role and constraints, delimiters around untrusted content (9.4), an explicit output format, 3–8 diverse demonstrations, and the protocol of 9.6 to choose between prompts (Ex. 4).

Proposition 4.2 — ICL as implicit Bayesian inference (Xie et al., 2022) Suppose each pre-training document is generated by first drawing a latent concept \vartheta (topic, format, task), then text from p(\cdot\mid\vartheta). The ideal next-token predictor marginalizes over concepts: p(y \mid \text{prompt}) = \int p(y \mid x, \vartheta)\; p(\vartheta \mid \text{prompt})\; d\vartheta . Each demonstration multiplies the posterior by its likelihood p(x_j, y_j\mid\vartheta). When the demonstrations share one concept \vartheta^\star, the posterior concentrates on it as k grows, and the prediction tends to p(y\mid x,\vartheta^\star).

Reading: ICL locates a task already seen in pre-training rather than learning a new one — hence the weak effect of wrong labels, and the failures on tasks absent from pre-training.

4.1. Supervised fine-tuning

Step 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (instruction tuning) Fine-tune the base model on quality (instruction, response) pairs — a few tens/hundreds of thousands of examples suffice to turn an autocomplete into an assistant. Engineering details: loss mask (the loss is computed only on the response), chat templates, data “distilled” from a large model.

Templates and loss mask — the detail that matters The template serializes the turns: <|system|>…<|user|>…<|assistant|>. The loss masks the prompt tokens (system/user roles) and keeps only the completion — otherwise the model learns to imitate the user and prompt logging becomes noise: \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\sum_{t\,\in\,\text{completion}} \log p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t}, x).

4.2. Why alignment beyond SFT

Step 2 — Why alignment beyond SFT? SFT imitates demonstrations; it does not directly optimize “the best response” and does not learn to avoid harmful responses. The central idea of InstructGPT/ChatGPT: learn a human reward, then optimize the policy against that reward (RL) — this is where the M1 RL module becomes the M2’s central tool.

Rejection sampling (best-of-n) An intermediate and indestructible step: generate n responses, keep those that pass a filter (reward model, tests, heuristics), re-fine-tune on them — the Llama 2 recipe between RLHF rounds (Touvron et al., 2023). This is exactly “exploit the best of n”, without policy gradients; the link with bandits (RL module ch. 4) is direct: each draw informs the policy for the next round.

4.3. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning and distillation

Full fine-tuning of an 8B model with Adam needs about 16–20 bytes per parameter (ch. 2), well over 100 GB. Two families avoid this cost: train a few new parameters on top of a frozen model (LoRA, QLoRA), or train a smaller model to imitate a large one (distillation).

Definition 4.3 — LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) Freeze a pre-trained weight W_0 of shape d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}} and learn a low-rank update of rank r \ll \min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}): h = W_0x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx, \qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r},\quad A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}.

  • Initialization: A random (Gaussian), B=0, so \Delta W = BA = 0 at step 0: training starts exactly from the pre-trained model.
  • Scale \alpha/r (lora_alpha in the PEFT library): changing r barely changes the size of the update, so the learning rate need not be retuned for each rank.
  • Parameters: r(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}) per adapted matrix instead of d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}.
  • Merging: after training, W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA costs nothing extra at inference; or keep the adapters separate and serve many of them on one base model (MLOps ch. 7).

Example — Llama-3-8B with r=16 on W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O of its 32 layers (W_K and W_V map 4096 → 1024 dimensions under GQA): 32 \times 16 \times \big(2\cdot 8192 + 2\cdot 5120\big) \approx 13.6\text{M parameters} \approx 0.17\,\%\ \text{of the model}.

  • QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023): store the frozen base in 4 bits (NF4, a data type matched to normally distributed weights), quantize the quantization constants as well, and page the optimizer states to CPU memory on spikes. Gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit weights into bf16 adapters. An 8B base then takes about 5 GB, and a 65B model can be fine-tuned on one 48 GB GPU.
  • Limits: tasks far from pre-training (a new language, long continued pre-training) need a higher rank or full fine-tuning. LoRA learns less than full fine-tuning, but it also forgets less of what the base model knew (Biderman et al., 2024).

Definition 4.4 — Logit distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) A teacher with logits z_t trains a student with logits z_s at temperature T: \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}(y, z_s) + \lambda\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z_t/T)\,\big\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(z_s/T)\big). A large T exposes which wrong tokens the teacher finds almost right. The gradient of the KL term with respect to z_s shrinks like 1/T^2, hence the factor T^2, which keeps the two terms balanced when T changes.

  • Token-level distillation applies \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} at every position of the sequence; teacher and student must share the tokenizer.
  • Sequence-level distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016) fine-tunes the student on texts generated by the teacher: this is the “data distilled from a large model” of 4.1, and the recipe of the small DeepSeek-R1 distilled models. It works across tokenizers.

5. RLHF, DPO, GRPO

This chapter continues chapter 4: once the model imitates good answers, we want it to prefer better answers to worse ones. It derives the three methods that do this — RLHF, which learns a reward and optimizes it with PPO; DPO, which skips the reward model; and GRPO, which uses verifiable rewards and no critic.

Notation: \pi_\theta is the policy being trained, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} the frozen reference (the SFT model), y_w \succ y_l a preferred and a dispreferred response to the same prompt x, and \beta the KL coefficient.

RLHF — the classic pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022)

  1. Reward model: train r_\phi(x, y) on human preferences y_w \succ y_l (response pairs); Bradley-Terry loss: \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big).
  2. Policy optimization with PPO (RL module, ch. 5): \max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}\big[r_\phi(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big) — the KL term prevents reward hacking and out-of-distribution drift.

Proposition 5.1 — Bradley-Terry, derived If humans compare two responses y_1, y_2 with a probability of preferring y_1 increasing in the “strength” difference, the axiomatics (transitive binary choice, invariance) imposes a logistic model on the difference of latent scores: \mathbb{P}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\big). The log-likelihood of the observed preferences y_w \succ y_l gives the loss above. Only the difference of rewards is identifiable: adding any function f(x) to r changes no preference probability.

This offset is harmless. In the KL-regularized objective it multiplies e^{r/\beta} by a constant that the normalizer Z(x) absorbs, so the optimal policy does not change (step 1 of Theorem 5.2). The KL penalty is there for another reason: to limit the drift away from \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} and the over-optimization of an imperfect reward.

Proof. Derivative in r_w = r(x,y_w): -\big(1-\sigma(r_w-r_l)\big) — the penalty is maximal when the pair is misclassified, zero when the margin is amply positive; it is a logistic regression on reward differences. ∎

PPO for LLMs — a recall of the RL module The PPO-clip objective and the advantage estimates are those of RL ch. 5, the prerequisite reading. For an LLM, the state is the prompt plus the tokens so far, an action is one token, the reward r_\phi(x,y) arrives at the end of the sequence, and a per-token KL penalty toward \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} is added along the way.

Four models in memory: policy \pi_\theta, reference \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} (frozen), reward r_\phi (frozen), critic (value head). Typical settings: \beta\in[0.01,\,0.2], a learning rate about 10× below SFT, clip \epsilon\approx0.2.

Classic symptoms: KL explodes (β too small); degenerate responses (hacked reward); drifting length (verbosity bias of the reward model).

Theorem 5.2 — DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023): complete derivation The loss \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big) optimizes the policy directly on preferences — without a reward model or rollouts.

Proof. Step 1 (closed form of the KL-penalized problem: the Gibbs variational principle). Fix x and let \pi^\star = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\,e^{r/\beta}/Z, with Z=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta}. For any policy \pi, \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^\star) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \frac{r}{\beta} + \log Z\Big] = \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) - \frac{1}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_\pi[r] + \log Z, so \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \beta\log Z - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^\star), which is maximal exactly at \pi=\pi^\star: \pi^\star(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}. This is the KL-regularised policy-improvement lemma of RL ch. 6, behind soft RL and SAC.

Step 2 (implicit reward). Inverting: r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x).

Step 3 (injection into Bradley-Terry). In \sigma(r_w-r_l), the term \beta\log Z(x) — the same for y_w and y_l — cancels: only the difference of log-ratios matters.

Maximizing the likelihood of the preferences gives \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} above (with \pi_\theta in place of \pi^\star).

Gradient: -\beta\,\sigma(-\hat r)\,\nabla_\theta\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)\big] with implicit reward margin \hat r = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w)} -\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l)}: the log-ratio margin of the preferred responses is increased, all the more strongly as the pair is misclassified. ∎

Key result — the DPO loss \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)

The loss fits in a few lines of PyTorch. The only subtle part is the sequence log-probability: sum the token log-probabilities of the response only, with the prompt masked exactly as in SFT (4.1).

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def sequence_logprob(model, input_ids, attention_mask, labels):
    """log pi(y|x): sum of token log-probs over the response (labels = -100 on the prompt)."""
    logits = model(input_ids=input_ids, attention_mask=attention_mask).logits[:, :-1]
    labels = labels[:, 1:]                                # token t is predicted at t-1
    mask = labels != -100
    logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)
    tok = torch.gather(logp, 2, labels.clamp(min=0).unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
    return (tok * mask).sum(-1)                           # shape (batch,)

def dpo_loss(policy, ref, batch, beta=0.1):
    """batch["chosen"] / batch["rejected"]: (input_ids, attention_mask, labels) for y_w / y_l."""
    lp_w = sequence_logprob(policy, *batch["chosen"])
    lp_l = sequence_logprob(policy, *batch["rejected"])
    with torch.no_grad():                                 # pi_ref is frozen
        ref_w = sequence_logprob(ref, *batch["chosen"])
        ref_l = sequence_logprob(ref, *batch["rejected"])
    margin = beta * ((lp_w - ref_w) - (lp_l - ref_l))     # implicit reward margin r_hat
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean()
    accuracy = (margin > 0).float().mean()                # share of pairs ranked correctly
    return loss, accuracy.detach(), margin.detach()

# sanity check: at step 0, policy == ref, so margin = 0 and loss = log 2 ≈ 0.693

Theorem 5.3 — GRPO (Shao et al., DeepSeekMath 2024): derivation For each prompt x, sample a group of G responses y_1,\dots,y_G from the current policy \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} and score them. Every token of y_i gets the same group-normalized advantage: \hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_{1..G})}{\mathrm{std}(r_{1..G})}. With the per-token ratio \rho_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}, GRPO maximizes the PPO-clip objective with a KL penalty toward \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}: \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\Big(\min\big(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_i\big) - \beta\,\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{i,t}\Big)\Big]. The per-token KL estimator, evaluated at the sampled token, \widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - \log\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\pi_\theta} - 1 \;\ge 0, is unbiased for \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) under sampling from \pi_\theta, and never negative.

At the first update (\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}, so every \rho_{i,t}=1), the gradient of the clipped term is the policy gradient \frac{1}{G}\sum_i \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})\,\hat A_i .

Proof. Variance. REINFORCE, \nabla\mathbb{E}[r]=\mathbb{E}[r\,\nabla\log\pi], stays unbiased with any baseline b that does not depend on the sampled response: \mathbb{E}[b\,\nabla\log\pi]=b\,\nabla\!\sum_y\pi(y)=0 . The leave-one-out mean b_i=\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j\neq i}r_j is such a baseline (RLOO, Ahmadian et al., 2024), and the group mean is a rescaling of it: r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_{1..G}) = \frac{G-1}{G}\,(r_i - b_i). So the centred advantage is unbiased up to the factor 1-1/G. It removes the variance due to the prompt’s difficulty without training any network.

The std normalization is a design choice, not a theorem. Dividing by \mathrm{std}(r_{1..G}) gives every prompt the same weight, so an almost-solved prompt with a small spread is amplified: the gradient is biased toward such prompts. The 1/|y_i| factor also penalizes long wrong answers less per token. Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) removes both; Ex. 11 measures the effect.

No more critic. Unlike PPO, the baseline comes from the group itself — the value network (and its memory and training) disappears. It is a Monte-Carlo estimate of the advantage A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s) obtained by sampling several responses. ∎

RLVR and reasoning (2024-2026) When quality is verifiable (a passing unit test, an exact mathematical answer), the reward no longer needs to be learned: RL with Verifiable Rewards.

GRPO is the engine of DeepSeek-R1: by optimizing verifiable correctness, long chains of thought emerge (self-verification, backtracking, “aha” moments) — the “o1” style.

Self-consistency at inference (Wang et al., 2023): sample n reasonings and answer by majority vote. If the samples were independent and the correct answer had probability p>1/2 in a binary choice, the error of the vote would decay exponentially (Hoeffding): \mathbb{P}(\text{majority wrong}) \le e^{-2n(p-1/2)^2}. In practice the samples are correlated (same model, same prompt): the first few samples bring most of the gain, then accuracy saturates.

Costs: 10× more tokens; recent works regularize length (ch. 10: “Shorter but not Worse”). Exploration (bandits, RL module ch. 4) becomes central: the GRPO group is structured exploration.

Overview SFT (imitate) → RLHF (optimize preferences) → DPO (preferences without RL) → RLVR/GRPO (optimize verifiable truth). A “modern” LLM chains: base → SFT → rejection sampling → DPO/GRPO.

6. Running an LLM: inference

A trained model still has to produce text, one token at a time, under memory and latency constraints. This chapter explains how tokens are chosen (decoding), where the memory goes (the KV-cache), and the three main ways to make serving faster or cheaper without changing the output much: speculative decoding, quantization and smarter batching.

6.1. Decoding: formulas and effects

Definition 6.1 — From greedy to nucleus At each step, logits z over the |\mathcal{V}| tokens of the vocabulary;

  • Temperature: p_i = \dfrac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)} — T\to0: argmax (greedy); large T: uniformization;
  • Top-k: truncate to the k best logits then renormalize;
  • Top-p (nucleus): smallest set of tokens with cumulative probability \ge p (adaptive size: wide on an uncertain step, narrow on a deterministic step);
  • Repetition penalty: divide the logit of already emitted tokens (factor 1.1-1.3); min-p: threshold relative to the best token.

T = 0 (greedy) deterministic, possible repetitions none extraction, code, factual RAG

6.2. KV-cache: the memory arithmetic

Proposition 6.2 — Cache memory = \mathcal{O}(n\cdot d), quantified In autoregression, each new token must attend to the keys/values of the whole past — they are cached, and their size M_{\text{KV}} is given by Proposition 2.4, the formula to reuse.

Llama-3-8B (32 layers, 8 KV heads, 128 dim, fp16) → 128 KiB/token: 8k context ≈ 1 GiB, 100k ≈ 12.2 GiB (13.1 GB), 1M ≈ 122 GiB (weights excluded!).

Three ways out: GQA (÷4 here), int8 cache quantization (÷2), and eviction/recomputation (sliding window).

Engineering rule: context length is first a memory bandwidth problem, not a FLOPs problem — hence the techniques of the next sections.

6.3. Speculative decoding: the correction proved

Theorem 6.3 — Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) A small model q proposes \gamma tokens; the large model p computes the \gamma distributions in a single forward.

Each proposal y is accepted with probability \min(1, p(y)/q(y)); at the first rejection, one resamples from the normalized residual \mathrm{norm}\big(\max(0,\,p-q)\big).

Then the marginal distribution of each produced token is exactly p — quality unchanged, latency divided by ~2-3 when q\approx p.

Proof. For one token:

  1. acceptance contribution: q(y)\min(1,p(y)/q(y)) = \min(q(y),p(y));
  2. rejection contribution: the total rejected mass equals \sum_x q(x)\big(1-\min(1,p(x)/q(x))\big) = \sum_x \max(0,q(x)-p(x)) = R, and the resampled residual adds R\cdot\frac{\max(0,p(y)-q(y))}{R} = \max(0,p(y)-q(y)).

Total: \min(q,p)+\max(0,p-q)=p. ∎

6.4. Quantization: principles and errors

  • Post-training int8: nearly lossless (batch calibration, per-channel scales); int4: the standard compromise;
  • GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022): layer by layer, minimize \|Wx-\hat W x\|_2^2 on a calibration set — column-by-column quantization with error compensation on the remaining columns (via the inverse of the Hessian \mathbf{H}=2\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top);
  • AWQ (Lin et al., 2023): protect the “salient” channels (high activations) with per-channel scaling before quantization — the error is shifted toward weakly active channels;
  • Typical errors: int8 ≈ +0.0-0.1 PPL; well-calibrated int4 ≈ +0.1-0.5 PPL (often imperceptible); below 4 bits, degradation accelerates (2-3-bit quantization = research); in production, always re-validate on your benchmarks, not PPL alone.

6.5. Serving: continuous batching, PagedAttention, costs

  • Continuous batching (vLLM, Orca): schedule at the decoding step — finished sequences leave, new ones enter the same batch; throughput multiplied by ~10-20× vs static batching;

  • PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023): the KV-cache split into pages (like virtual memory) — no more fragmentation; sharing of common prefixes (system prompt); ~2-4× memory gain → larger batches;

  • Costs: two regimes — API (on the order of $/M tokens, steadily decreasing) vs on-prem (amortized GPU-hour: an H100 at ~3$/h serving ~2-5k tokens/s on an int4-quantized 8B → a few $/M tokens under heavy load).

    The real cost of an agent = repeated input tokens × history length (ch. 8); the “ML Ops” angle is deepened in the dedicated M2 module.

7. RAG in depth

A model only knows what was in its training data, up to its cutoff date, and cannot cite sources. Retrieval-augmented generation fixes this by fetching relevant documents at query time and placing them in the context; this chapter goes through each stage of such a pipeline and how to evaluate it.

It assumes the minimal pipeline of NLP ch. 7.4 — chunk, embed, retrieve, prompt — and goes deeper at each stage.

Definition 7.1 — RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) p(y \mid q) \approx p_\theta\big(y \mid q, \mathrm{top}_k\, \mathrm{sim}(e_q, e_d)\big) Index documents by embeddings, retrieve those closest to the prompt, inject them into the context.

Reduces hallucinations, updates knowledge, handles proprietary data without retraining.

7.1. Chunking

  • Fixed with overlap: 256-1024 tokens, stride 10-20%; structural splitting (sections, paragraphs) > blind splitting (cut sentences destroy retrieval);
  • Semantic chunking: cut where the embedding similarity between successive windows drops (topic change);
  • Multi-granularity: index by passages, but return the parent section to the LLM (small-to-big); for structured documents, keep titles/metadata in the chunk (pre-retrieval filters).

7.2. Retrieval: BM25, dense, hybrid — with numbers

Retriever Principle BEIR (NDCG@10, ~18 tasks) Strengths / weaknesses
BM25 probabilistic weighting (NLP ch. 2) ≈ 0.44 on average zero training, exact (lexical matches); misses reformulations
Dense (E5/BGE, …) SBERT embeddings + ANN (FAISS/HNSW) ≈ 0.50-0.52 for the best captures paraphrase; errors on jargon/exact refs, must be indexed
Hybrid + reranker fusion (RRF) then cross-encoder ≈ 0.53-0.55 practical state of the art; reranking cost O(top-100)

ANN indexes: IVF, HNSW, PQ Exact search costs \mathcal{O}(nd) per query, and a million 768-dimensional float32 embeddings take 3 GB. Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for orders of magnitude in speed and memory (FAISS: Johnson et al., 2019).

  • IVF (inverted file): k-means splits the vectors into n_{\text{list}} cells; a query visits only its n_{\text{probe}} nearest cells, about a fraction n_{\text{probe}}/n_{\text{list}} of the data.
  • HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2018): a layered proximity graph; a query descends greedily from a sparse top layer to the dense bottom one in about \log n hops. Fast and accurate, but memory-hungry: the links add roughly 100–300 bytes per vector, and the vectors stay uncompressed.
  • PQ (product quantization, Jégou et al., 2011): cut each vector into m sub-vectors and replace each one by the index of one of 256 centroids, so a vector costs m bytes — 96 MB instead of 3 GB above with m=96. Distances come from small lookup tables; IVF-PQ combines both ideas.

The figure of merit is recall@k — the share of the exact top-k that the index returns — plotted against queries per second, and tuned by n_{\text{probe}} (IVF) or the search width (HNSW). The table’s NDCG figures assume a recall@k close to 1: measure it before blaming the embedding model.

Cross-encoder rerankers The bi-encoder scores s(q,d)=\cos(e_q,e_d) separately (indexable); the cross-encoder scores s(q,d)=f_\theta([q\,;d]) jointly — it sees the exact term interaction (mini-attention) but must run on every candidate.

Hence the cascade architecture: dense/BM25 (top-100) → cross-encoder (top-5). Typical gain: +3 to +5 points of NDCG@10.

The “lost in the middle” effect (Liu et al., 2023): even with the right passages, the LLM exploits better those at the beginning/end of the context → reorder the best ones at the extremes.

7.3. RAG evaluation

RAGAS-type metrics (Es et al., 2023)

  • Faithfulness: decompose the answer into atomic claims (by an LLM judge); faithfulness = fraction supported by the retrieved context;
  • Answer relevance: the LLM generates candidate questions from the answer — average similarity with the true question;
  • Context precision / recall: are the useful chunks there and well ranked? / does the corpus contain what was missing?

Lab protocol: QA set with reference context (golden context), compare faithfulness with/without reranking, measure the cost (tokens, latency) of each stage.

7.4. GraphRAG and variants

  • HyDE (Gao et al., 2022): generate a hypothetical answer to the question and search with its embedding — the imaginary answer is closer to the documents than the question;
  • Structured queries: text → graph query (graph linearization, see the publications in ch. 10) or SQL (text-to-SQL): the LLM writes the query, the database answers — maximal auditability.

Retrieval over a knowledge graph. Vector RAG retrieves passages that look like the question; it fails when the answer needs a chain of relations, as in “drugs that target a gene associated with disease X”. GraphRAG retrieves a subgraph of a knowledge graph of (head, relation, tail) triples, built as in NLP ch. 6.4 and Graph ML ch. 10.1.

  1. Seed nodes: link the question’s entities to graph nodes, by string matching or dense retrieval over node descriptions.
  2. Subgraph:
    • k-hop: every node within k hops of the seeds — about \bar d^{\,k} nodes per seed for an average degree \bar d, often too many beyond k=2.
    • PCST (G-Retriever, He et al., 2024): nodes and edges get a prize that grows with their similarity to the question, edges a cost c; keep the connected subgraph S that maximizes \sum_{v\in S}\mathrm{prize}(v)+\sum_{e\in S}\mathrm{prize}(e)-c\,|E(S)|, a prize-collecting Steiner tree with fast approximate solvers. The result is small, connected and relevant.
  3. Linearization: one triple per line, such as “(aspirin, treats, headache)”, plus short node descriptions, with the instruction to cite node ids.

Token cost. Vector RAG sends about 5 × 300 = 1,500 tokens. At about 10 tokens per triple, the 2-hop neighbourhood of an entity of degree 30 (about 900 edges) costs about 9,000 tokens, and a PCST subgraph of 30 edges about 300: pruning keeps GraphRAG affordable.

Global questions. Microsoft’s GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) also splits the graph into communities (Leiden algorithm) and has an LLM summarize each one, to answer questions about a whole corpus. The cost moves to indexing: one LLM call per chunk and per community.

Benchmark. STaRK (Wu et al., 2024) mixes textual and relational queries. Its STaRK-Prime split runs over the biomedical graph PrimeKG, with one text document per node, scored by Hit@1, Hit@5, Recall@20 and MRR; it is the benchmark of the final lab (ch. 8). The worked version, with a named corpus and baselines, is Graph ML ch. 10.4.

Planning and multi-agents

  • Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023): search a tree of reasoning steps, evaluate the nodes with the LLM, backtrack — beyond the linear chain (CoT);

  • Multi-agents: orchestrate a DAG of specialized agents (planner → retriever/coder → verifier → synthesis), LangGraph-type frameworks; each agent = an LLM with its own prompt/tools/memory; patterns: supervisor, debate, generator-critic.

    RL link ch. 7: policy decomposition; Graph ML link: orchestration is an executed graph;

  • Memory: scratchpad (the current context), vector memory (RAG over past exchanges), episodic memory (structured session summaries), external working memory (files) — it is the ch. 7 RAG reused on oneself.

Evaluating (and billing) an agent Benchmarks: GAIA (generalist assistant), SWE-bench (real GitHub tickets), WebArena and τ-bench (tools and dialogues).

  • Task success: the share of tasks whose final state passes an automatic check (the tests pass, the database holds the right record) — not a judge’s opinion of the transcript.
  • Reliability, pass^k (Yao et al., 2024, τ-bench): the probability that k independent runs of the same task all succeed. Run each task n times, count c successes, and average \binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k} over the tasks.
  • Cost per task: tokens (often 10⁵-10⁶ for a multi-turn agent), tool calls and wall-clock time, divided by the success rate to get the cost per solved task — hence ch. 6: a small model for routine steps, a large one for decisions.
  • Safety: the rate of injections suffered (ch. 9.4).

With a per-task success probability p, pass^k equals p^k, whereas the code-generation metric pass@k (at least one success in k tries) equals 1-(1-p)^k. An agent with p=0.8 has pass@8 ≈ 1 but \text{pass}^8 = 0.8^8 \approx 0.17: a user who runs it every day has only a 21 % chance (0.8⁷) of a failure-free week (Ex. 14).

Agent evaluation is an open research topic — and a major career path of the master.

8.2. Multimodality: how an image enters a context

  • Vision: a ViT encoder cuts the image into 16×16 patches → one token per patch (Deep Learning ch. 6.6 for ViT); a projector (MLP or Q-Former) aligns these tokens onto the LLM’s embedding space → the image becomes “text” inside the context (LLaVA scheme).

    The encoder is often pre-trained contrastively image-text — the common latent space of CLIP, worked in 8.2b (and in depth in the Multimodal GenAI module ch. 5);

  • Audio: mel spectrograms → encoder (Whisper-style) → tokens; the “transcription + style” output feeds the LLM (voice assistant = encoder → LLM → synthesis pipeline);

  • Video: sampled images (1-4/s) + temporal pooling — very high token cost: the sampling step choice is a compression choice;

  • Documents: pages = images + interleaved OCR text (the number 1 enterprise use case — contracts, reports).

8.2b. Vision-language models, worked (self-study)

This self-study section (about 2 h, with Ex. 6) turns the vision bullet of 8.2 into a worked recipe: how an image becomes tokens, how CLIP aligns images and text, and how LLaVA plugs a vision encoder into an LLM. It is the vision-language recap that the Geometric Data module (ch. 8) points to.

  • ViT recap (Deep Learning ch. 6.6): an H\times W image is cut into P\times P patches, each flattened and linearly projected, plus a position embedding; a transformer encoder processes the HW/P^2 patch tokens. ViT-L/14 at 336 px gives (336/14)^2 = 576 tokens.

Definition 8.2 — CLIP’s symmetric contrastive loss (Radford et al., 2021) Take a batch of B image–caption pairs (x_i, t_i), an image encoder f and a text encoder g, with normalized embeddings u_i = \frac{f(x_i)}{\|f(x_i)\|}, \qquad v_j = \frac{g(t_j)}{\|g(t_j)\|}. With the similarities s_{ij} = u_i^\top v_j and a learned temperature \tau: \mathcal{L}_{\text{CLIP}} = -\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\Big[\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{s_{ij}/\tau}} + \log\frac{e^{s_{ii}/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{s_{ji}/\tau}}\Big]. Each row (one image against all captions) and each column (one caption against all images) is a B-way classification whose correct class is the diagonal: InfoNCE in both directions. CLIP was trained this way on 400M web pairs.

Zero-shot classification follows directly: embed one prompt per class, “a photo of a {class}”, and predict the class whose text embedding is closest to the image embedding. No label of the target dataset is used.

  • LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023): a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encodes the image; a projector W (linear in LLaVA, a two-layer MLP in LLaVA-1.5) maps its patch features to the LLM’s embedding space; the resulting visual tokens are placed in the prompt before the text tokens.
    • Stage 1, feature alignment: freeze the vision encoder and the LLM and train only W on about 600k image–caption pairs (filtered CC3M), the instruction being “describe the image”.
    • Stage 2, visual instruction tuning: keep the encoder frozen and train W and the LLM on about 158k multimodal conversations, with the SFT loss mask of 4.1: the loss covers the assistant’s answer only, never the image tokens or the user’s question.
  • Token budget: 576 visual tokens for one 336-px image in LLaVA-1.5, as many as a page of text. High-resolution variants tile the image and multiply this count, which is why video (8.2) keeps few frames.
# zero-shot CIFAR-10 with open_clip (Ex. 6) — pip install open_clip_torch torchvision
import torch, open_clip
from torchvision.datasets import CIFAR10

model, _, preprocess = open_clip.create_model_and_transforms(
    "ViT-B-32", pretrained="laion2b_s34b_b79k")
tokenizer = open_clip.get_tokenizer("ViT-B-32")
model.eval()

data = CIFAR10(root="data", train=False, download=True, transform=preprocess)
loader = torch.utils.data.DataLoader(data, batch_size=256)
prompts = [f"a photo of a {c}" for c in data.classes]

with torch.no_grad():
    v = model.encode_text(tokenizer(prompts))
    v = v / v.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)              # one text embedding per class
    correct = 0
    for x, y in loader:
        u = model.encode_image(x)
        u = u / u.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)          # image embeddings
        pred = (u @ v.T).argmax(dim=-1)               # nearest class prompt (cosine)
        correct += (pred == y).sum().item()
print(f"zero-shot accuracy: {correct / len(data):.3f}")

9. Evaluation and safety of LLMs

A score on a benchmark is only useful if it measures what we think it measures, and a deployed model must resist misuse. This chapter covers how benchmarks are built and skewed, the biases of LLM judges, hallucinations, attacks on LLM systems, a statistical test to detect machine-generated text, and how to compare two models honestly; a self-study section on privacy and bias closes it.

9.1. Benchmarks: construction and contamination

Definition 9.1 — MMLU-type benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2020) Multiple-choice questions (57 subjects for MMLU; variants: MMLU-Pro, GPQA, HELM for multi-faceted evaluation). Two scoring modes: log-likelihood of the options (deterministic, but biased by answer length/frequency) vs generation then extraction (realistic, but prompt- and parsing-dependent).

Rule: always specify the protocol — rankings change with the mode.

Contamination: the measurement that skews everything If the benchmark is in the pre-training data (the web!), the score measures memorization, not competence.

Detection: n-gram overlap (8-13 grams) between questions/training data, canary strings (hidden markers), cloze recall tests.

Remedies: private/rotating evaluations, dynamic question generation, temporal re-splits (post-cutoff questions).

Recent work on this: memorization in fine-tuning and multilingual hallucinations — ch. 9 of the M1 NLP module (Buscaldi’s work: MUCH, memorization prediction).

9.2. LLM-as-judge: convenient, but biased

  • Positional bias: the judge prefers the first (or second) answer depending on order — mitigation: evaluate in both orders and average;
  • Verbosity / style bias: long, well-formatted answers are overrated — mitigation: rubrics (explicit criteria), constrained format;
  • Self-preference: a judge favors answers from its own model family — mitigation: judge ensembles, judges trained for calibration (references: MT-Bench, G-Eval);
  • Calibration: a judge score is not a probability — verify on a set where ground truth exists (fraction of judgments matching humans); cheap distilled metrics (FrugalScore, ch. 10) were born exactly from this need.

9.3. Hallucinations

Taxonomy 9.2 Intrinsic: the answer contradicts the source/context (RAG: wrong citation); extrinsic: unverifiable (invented) claims; prompt fidelity: silent disobedience to instructions.

Causes: sampling (temperature), knowledge cutoff, the “always answer” training bias (no learned abstention), sycophancy (telling the user yes).

Mitigations: RAG + verifiable citations (ch. 7), self-consistency (ch. 5), low temperature for factual tasks, abstention training, post-hoc verification by decomposition into claims (the heart of RAGAS “faithfulness”).

9.4. Attacks: jailbreaks and prompt injections

This section is the guaranteed source on jailbreaks and prompt injections for the modules that use them later — Control of Generative AI, Security and Robustness, MLOps and Advanced Topics in LLMs; the Explainability, Security and Privacy module (ch. 5–6) goes deeper.

  • Direct jailbreaks: role-play (“DAN”), hypothetical framing, encoding (base64, unicode), multi-turn crescendo — circumventing safety instructions;

  • Indirect injections: the retrieved content (web page, PDF, tool result) contains malicious instructions — the number 1 attack on RAG/agent systems (ch. 7-8);

  • Defense in depth: input/output classifiers (Llama Guard type), spotlighting (delimiting and marking untrusted content), least privilege on tools (the retriever does not write to the database!), prompt/data separation (ch. 1 — special tokens: the same hygiene), human in the loop for irreversible actions.

    No defense is complete — one reduces the attack surface.

9.5. Watermarking

Theorem 9.3 — Statistical watermark (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023) Before each token, hash the prefix → a pseudo-random seed that partitions the vocabulary into “green”/“red” (fraction \gamma, e.g. 1/2); generation penalizes reds. On a text of T tokens containing s_G green ones: z = \frac{s_G - \gamma T}{\sqrt{T\,\gamma(1-\gamma)}} \;\xrightarrow{T\ \text{large}}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{under H}_0 . Reject “human text” if z exceeds the threshold (p-value) — under H1 (marked), z grows like \sqrt T times the introduced bias.

Proof. Under H₀ (unmarked text), each token is green with probability \gamma independently, so s_G\sim\mathrm{Bin}(T,\gamma); the standardized variable tends to the normal (CLT), hence the z-score. The partition depends on the prefix: impossible to remove without the seed.

Limitations (follow-up paper, Kirchenbauer et al. 2023): paraphrasing, truncation, and false positives on short texts — the test is statistical, not deterministic. ∎

9.6. Comparing two models honestly

A two-point gain on a 1,000-question benchmark may be noise. Before claiming that model B beats model A, measure the uncertainty due to the items, the prompt and the seed; this is the LLM form of Machine Learning ch. 4.9.

  • Pair the items. Run A and B on the same n items and record per-item outcomes a_i, b_i (correct or not, or a score). Compute the standard error on the paired differences b_i-a_i: it is much smaller than the unpaired one when both models fail on the same hard items.
  • Bootstrap over items. Resample the n items with replacement 10^4 times, recompute the difference each time, and report the 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles as a 95 % confidence interval. This works for any metric: F1, judge win rate, RAGAS faithfulness.
  • Paired test. For right/wrong outcomes, McNemar’s test uses only the discordant items: n_{10} (A right, B wrong) and n_{01} (A wrong, B right). Under H₀ (equal accuracy), each discordant item favours B with probability 1/2, and \chi^2 = \frac{(n_{01}-n_{10})^2}{n_{01}+n_{10}} \;\approx\; \chi^2_1 . For other metrics, a paired permutation test randomly swaps a_i and b_i within each pair.
  • Worked example. On 1,000 items, A scores 70.0 % and B 72.0 %, with n_{01}=60 and n_{10}=40: \chi^2 = \frac{(60-40)^2}{100} = 4.0, \qquad p\approx0.046 (0.057 with the exact binomial version). An unpaired two-proportion test on the same accuracies gives z\approx0.99, p\approx0.32: ignoring the pairing hides a real, if small, difference.
  • Prompts and seeds. Scores move by several points with the prompt template and the sampling seed (4.0). Report the mean and the standard deviation over at least 3 templates and 3 seeds next to the item interval, fix the protocol (scoring mode, 9.1) before looking at the results, and correct for multiple comparisons (Holm) when many benchmarks are compared.

9.7. Privacy and bias of LLMs (self-study)

An LLM can leak pieces of its training data and treat groups of people unequally. This self-study section (about 1.5 h) gives the core measurements; the Explainability, Security and Privacy module (ch. 7–8) goes deeper.

Definition 9.4 — Canary exposure (Carlini et al., 2019) Insert into the training data a canary: a template with a secret r drawn uniformly from a space \mathcal{R}, such as “my PIN is r” with |\mathcal{R}|=10^9. After training, rank all candidate secrets by the model’s perplexity on the template: \mathrm{exposure}(r) = \log_2 |\mathcal{R}| - \log_2 \mathrm{rank}(r). Near \log_2|\mathcal{R}| (about 30 bits here), the secret can be extracted by sorting candidates; near 0, it is not memorized.

  • Extraction recipe (Carlini et al., 2021): generate many samples from short prefixes; rank them by a memorization score, such as the ratio of the log-perplexity to the zlib entropy (compressed length in bits); check the top candidates against the training data. On GPT-2 this exposed hundreds of verbatim training sequences, including names, phone numbers and URLs.
  • Membership inference as a test. Decide H₀: x was not in the training set, against H₁: it was. By the Neyman–Pearson lemma the best test thresholds a likelihood ratio; in practice the target’s log-likelihood is calibrated by a reference model that did not see x: \Lambda(x) = \log p_\theta(x) - \log p_{\text{ref}}(x) \;>\; t . Report the true-positive rate at a low false-positive rate (0.1–1 %), not the AUC (Carlini et al., 2022). On large models pre-trained for about one epoch such attacks are close to chance (Duan et al., 2024); on small fine-tuning sets seen several times, they are not.
  • Mitigations. Deduplicate (3.1): a sequence present 10 times in the training set is generated about 1,000 times more often than one present once (Kandpal et al., 2022). Fine-tune with DP-SGD (clipping C, noise multiplier \sigma; ESP ch. 8), which suits LoRA because few parameters are trained (Yu et al., 2022). Remove personal data before training; never put secrets in a system prompt.
  • Bias evaluation, BBQ-style (Parrish et al., 2022): questions about two people from different social groups, in an ambiguous context (the correct answer is “unknown”) and a disambiguated one. Among the answers other than “unknown”, n_{\text{biased}} follow the stereotype; the bias score s = 2\,\frac{n_{\text{biased}}}{n_{\text{non-unknown}}} - 1 \in [-1, 1] is multiplied by (1-\text{accuracy}) in ambiguous contexts. Report it per category (age, gender, nationality…) with a bootstrap interval (9.6).

  5. ★ Watermark. With \gamma=0.5 and T=400 tokens, compute z for a text carrying 230 green tokens; threshold at 5% (one-sided); how many tokens are needed to detect a +5% green bias?

    Proof. Guidance: z=(230-200)/10 = 3 → one-sided p ≈ 0.13 %: rejected (marked).

    For a green fraction \gamma+\varepsilon, the expectation of z is \varepsilon\sqrt{T/\gamma(1-\gamma)}. It reaches the one-sided 5 % threshold 1.645 when T = \Big(\frac{1.645}{\varepsilon}\Big)^2\gamma(1-\gamma) \approx 271 \text{ tokens for } \varepsilon=0.05, which detects the bias only half the time. For 80 % power, replace 1.645 by 1.645+0.84: T\approx 620 tokens — hence the difficulty on short texts. ∎

  6. ★ CLIP zero-shot and projector size (self-study, 8.2b). (a) Count the parameters of LLaVA-1.5’s projector, a two-layer MLP from the 1024-dimensional ViT-L/14 features to the 4096-dimensional embeddings of a 7B LLM (biases included), and compare with the LLM. (b) Run the open_clip code of 8.2b on CIFAR-10, then replace the single prompt by the average embedding of 5 templates (“a photo of a {}”, “a blurry photo of a {}”, …).

    Proof. Guidance: (a) the projector has 1024\cdot4096 + 4096 + 4096\cdot4096 + 4096 = 20{,}979{,}712 \approx 21\text{M} parameters, about 0.3 % of the 7B LLM — stage 1 of LLaVA trains only these.

    (b) Expect roughly 90 % or more for ViT-B-32; the prompt ensemble typically adds about a point. Inspect the confusion matrix (cat/dog, automobile/truck). ∎

  7. ★★ RoPE. Prove the relative property of thm 2.3 (R(a)^\top R(b) = R(b-a)), then check numerically that the attention score is invariant under a common translation of positions; implement context interpolation m\to m/L and measure perplexity beyond the training length.

    Proof. Guidance: 2D rotations = complex numbers — commutativity and e^{-ia}e^{ib}=e^{i(b-a)}; translation invariance follows from the theorem; in practice naive extrapolation degrades PPL, interpolation largely preserves it (this is the basis of extended contexts 32k→128k without full retraining). ∎

  8. ★★ Scaling. From published data (LLaMA 1/2/3, Chinchilla), verify the law \mathcal{L} \approx A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta} + E by regression; situate each model relative to the compute optimum (thm 3.1).

  9. ★★ MoE. Assume f_i=P_i for every expert (the router’s mean probabilities match the actual loads). Derive the minimum of the auxiliary loss \alpha_{\text{aux}} n_E\sum_i f_iP_i under \sum_i P_i=1 and show that it corresponds to uniform routing. Then show that without the assumption f_i=P_i the minimum is not uniform in general (compare Advanced Deep Learning, exercise 7 (b)); simulate the collapse (biased initialization of the router) without then with the loss.

    Proof. Guidance: with f_i=P_i the loss is \alpha_{\text{aux}} n_E\sum_i P_i^2. Cauchy–Schwarz gives 1=\Big(\sum_i P_i\Big)^2\le n_E\sum_i P_i^2, with equality iff P_i=1/n_E: uniform routing is the minimum, where the loss equals \alpha_{\text{aux}}, not 0.

    Without the assumption, take n_E=2, top-1 routing and four tokens: three with gate (\tfrac12+\varepsilon,\tfrac12-\varepsilon), sent to expert 1, and one with gate close to (0,1), sent to expert 2. Then f=(\tfrac34,\tfrac14) and P\approx(\tfrac38,\tfrac58), so \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}\approx 2\,\alpha_{\text{aux}}\Big(\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac38+\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac58\Big)=\tfrac78\,\alpha_{\text{aux}}<\alpha_{\text{aux}}. Tokens sent to expert 1 by a narrow margin keep P_1 low while f_1 is high, which lowers the loss below its value at balance.

    In the simulation, without the loss the winning expert attracts tokens (reinforced feedback) and specialization degenerates. ∎

  10. ★★ DPO on paper. Derive the closed form \pi^\star of the KL-penalized problem (Gibbs variational principle), then the DPO loss by re-injecting into Bradley-Terry; check the gradient: it increases the log-ratio margin of the preferred responses y_w over the dispreferred ones y_l. Then check numerically that the PyTorch loss of ch. 5 equals \log 2 when \pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}.

    Proof. Guidance: follow the proof of thm 5.2 step by step — the key argument is that \mathcal{J}=-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z)+\beta\log Z; then Z(x) disappears in the difference r_w-r_l.

    The gradient pushes \log\pi(y_w)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w) up and the y_l term down, modulated by \sigma(-\hat r) (hard pairs first).

    At \pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \hat r=0 and the loss is -\log\sigma(0)=\log 2. ∎

  11. ★★ Toy GRPO.   12. ★★ Speculative, on paper. Write out completely the proof of thm 6.3 in the case \gamma=2, then estimate the expected latency gain if q=p on 80% of the mass but diverges on the rest.

    Proof. Guidance: accept token 1 with probability \alpha=\sum_y\min(q,p)\approx0.8, then the second conditionally. With \gamma draft tokens and i.i.d. acceptances, the expected number of tokens produced per target forward is (Leviathan et al., 2023) \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha} = \frac{1-0.8^3}{0.2} \approx 2.44 \quad (\gamma=2), at most \gamma+1=3; the limit 1/(1-\alpha)=5 needs \gamma\to\infty. With a draft costing a fraction c of a target forward, the speedup is 2.44/(1+\gamma c), about 2.2× for c=0.05. ∎

  14. ★★ Agent reliability. An agent is run n=10 times on each task of a benchmark.

    • (a) Show that \binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k} is an unbiased estimator of p^k for a task with success probability p and c successes.
    • (b) For a task with c=8, compute the estimates of pass^1, pass^2, pass^3 and compare with the plug-in \hat p^k.
    • (c) One run costs 60k input and 4k output tokens at 3 $ and 15 $ per million: what is the cost per solved task?

    Proof. Guidance: (a) \binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k} is the average, over all k-subsets of the n runs, of the product of their success indicators; each product has expectation p^k by independence.

    (b) 0.8, 28/45\approx0.62, 56/120\approx0.47, against the plug-in 0.8, 0.64, 0.51, which is biased upward.

    (c) 0.18+0.06=0.24 $ per run, so 0.24/0.8=0.30 $ per solved task. ∎

  15. ★★ Comparing two models. On the 1,319 test problems of GSM8K, model A scores 61.0 % and model B 63.5 %, with n_{01}=95 problems solved by B only and n_{10}=62 by A only. (a) Run McNemar’s test. (b) Compute a paired bootstrap 95 % interval for the difference from per-item outcomes you simulate with these counts. (c) Explain why an unpaired test is the wrong tool. (d) Say what you would add before publishing the comparison.

    Proof. Guidance: (a) \chi^2=(95-62)^2/157\approx6.9, p\approx0.008 (exact binomial: 0.0104): B is better at the 5 % level; at the 1 % level only the χ² approximation says so, and the exact test falls just short. (b) The difference is 2.5 points and the interval runs from about 0.7 to 4.3 points, excluding 0. (c) The unpaired test ignores that both models share the hard problems, which inflates the standard error. (d) Repeat over 3 prompt templates and 3 seeds, and fix the answer-extraction rule in advance (9.1, 9.6). ∎

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours; one handwritten A4 sheet allowed; no electronic device; 4 questions — a course question, a computation, a short proof and a statistics question.

  1. Question (5 points). Compare RLHF (with PPO), DPO and GRPO: for each, say what is learned, what is sampled during training, which models sit in memory, and one typical failure.

    Expected:

    • RLHF learns a reward model, then optimizes the policy online with PPO; four models (policy, frozen reference, reward model, critic); failures: reward hacking, length drift, KL explosion.
    • DPO learns the policy directly from offline pairs, with no sampling; two models (policy, reference); failures: overfitting the preference set, likelihoods of both responses falling, no exploration.
    • GRPO samples G responses per prompt and uses a group baseline, with no critic; policy and reference plus a reward function; failures: prompt weighting biased by the std normalization, long wrong answers, the cost of G samples.

  2. Question (6 points). A model has 40 layers and 8 KV heads of dimension 128, with a bf16 cache; its weights take 16 GiB of an 80 GiB GPU. (a) KV-cache bytes per token and per 32,768-token sequence (2 pts). (b) The largest number of such sequences served at once (1 pt). (c) With speculative decoding, \gamma=4 and acceptance rate \alpha=0.7, the expected number of tokens per target forward (1.5 pts). (d) LoRA with r=8 on W_Q and W_V of a 32-layer model with d=4096 and full multi-head attention: the trainable parameters (1.5 pts).

    Expected: (a) 2 · 40 · 8 · 128 · 2 = 163,840 bytes = 160 KiB per token, so 5 GiB per sequence. (b) (80 − 16)/5 = 12.8, so 12 sequences. (c) (1-0.7^5)/0.3\approx2.77 tokens, out of at most 5. (d) 32 × 2 × 8 × (4096 + 4096) = 4,194,304 ≈ 4.2M.

  3. Question (5 points). Let \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} be a reference policy, r a reward and \beta>0.

    • (a) Show that the objective \mathbb{E}_\pi[r]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) is maximized by \pi^\star\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\,e^{r/\beta} (3 pts).
    • (b) Deduce that the Bradley–Terry likelihood of a preference y_w\succ y_l does not involve the normalizer Z(x), and state the DPO loss (2 pts).

    Expected: (a) expand \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^\star) to get the identity of step 1 of Theorem 5.2: \mathbb{E}_\pi[r]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \beta\log Z-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^\star); the KL is non-negative and zero only at \pi=\pi^\star.

    (b) Invert: r=\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}+\beta\log Z(x); the term \beta\log Z(x) cancels in \sigma(r_w-r_l), and replacing \pi^\star by \pi_\theta gives the DPO loss (key result of ch. 5).

  4. Question (4 points). On 500 items, model A scores 80.0 % and model B 84.0 %; 15 items are solved by A only and 35 by B only. (a) Is B significantly better at the 1 % level (2 pts)? (b) A colleague runs an unpaired two-proportion test and finds p\approx0.10. Who is right, and why (2 pts)?

    Expected: (a) McNemar: \chi^2=(35-15)^2/50=8.0, p\approx0.005<0.01: yes. (b) The paired test: both models answer the same items, so the variance of the difference must be computed on paired outcomes. The unpaired test ignores that the models share their hard items, overestimates the standard error and loses power.
