1. Evaluation: contamination, judge bias

Every later session asks whether a paper’s numbers can be trusted, so the seminar starts with the tools to answer that question: how test data leaks into training, how biased LLM judges are, and how to report a score honestly. A concrete scene: a state-of-the-art benchmark score that turns out to be inflated because the test set was in the training data.

What breaks Three classic reading mistakes: reading the abstract without the protocol; confusing the advertised context length with what the model really uses; and taking a contaminated score for a capability.

Today’s readings C. Xu, S. Guan, D. Greene, M.-T. Kechadi, Benchmark Data Contamination of Large Language Models: A Survey, 2024 (arXiv:2406.04244); L. Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2306.05685); P. Wang et al., Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators, ACL 2024 (arXiv:2305.17926); P. Liang et al., Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM), TMLR 2023 (arXiv:2211.09110).

1.1. Contamination: when the test leaks into training

Definition 1.1 — Benchmark data contamination (BDC) The pollution of a benchmark when its data (questions and/or answers) have been seen during pre-training — accidental (the web contains the solutions), or even deliberate (training on the test’s variant). Signals: n-gram overlaps with the corpus, embedding similarity, abnormally low test perplexity, the ability to complete the items without their instruction, “random” performance on renamed variants (changing the figures of a math problem).

Responses: canaries (“do not train on this string”), dynamic benchmarks refreshed (e.g. LiveBench), private sets, kept-current evaluations (challenges with a cutoff date).

Proposition 1.2 — What a contaminated score estimates (and what it does not) A benchmark measures performance on a task distribution P_{\text{test}}. If the model has seen n examples of P_{\text{test}} during training, the score observes a mixture \rho \cdot \text{memory} + (1-\rho) \cdot \text{generalization} : the estimand has shifted — the score overestimates and distorts comparisons (the models trained more recently, on larger corpora, are more contaminated: the bias advances with time).

The methodological remedy: report jointly (i) the raw score, (ii) the score on re-generated variants, (iii) an overlap measure — and treat the (i)-(ii) gap as an interval, not as noise.

1.2. LLM judges: useful, biased, calibratable

MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) validates the LLM judge: agreement with human preferences is high (GPT-4 as judge ≈ 80%+ of inter-human agreement level), which legitimizes its industrial use (ML Ops module ch. 5). But the biases are systematic:

  • position (favoring the first answer — corrected by alternating order),
  • verbosity (long answers win),
  • self-preference (the judge favors the answers of its own model family).

Wang et al. (ACL 2024) show that a simple order swap flips a substantial share of verdicts — “not fair evaluators”.

HELM (Liang et al.) answers with the holistic: same models, dozens of scenarios, multiple metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, bias, toxicity…), published protocol — evaluation as a slow science versus the leaderboard as a fast sport.

Critical analysis — the “methods” session

  1. Goodhart: every public benchmark becomes a training objective — the value of a score decays with its popularity;
  2. Variance: a leaderboard at ±0.5 points with 500 questions ranks nothing (cf. ML Ops module ch. 2, Hoeffding);
  3. Judge the judge: calibrate each judge on a human golden set before using it (measured biases, alternating orders, rubrics);
  4. Saturation: when everyone exceeds 90% accuracy, the benchmark measures noise — move to the dynamic/adversarial evaluations (red teaming in Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 10, agents of ch. 9).

LLGA rule: no evaluation number without its protocol — neither in the papers you read, nor in your own.

2. Reasoning: chain-of-thought, RLVR

Can a language model be made to reason better, either by letting it write intermediate steps or by training it on problems whose answers can be checked? This session reads the papers that introduced chain-of-thought and self-consistency, compared process and outcome rewards, and showed with DeepSeek-R1 that reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards makes long reasoning emerge.

Today’s readings J. Wei et al., Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2201.11903); X. Wang et al., Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models, ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2203.11171); H. Lightman et al., Let’s Verify Step by Step, ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2305.20050); DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning, 2025 (arXiv:2501.12948); N. Lambert et al., Tulu 3, 2024 (arXiv:2411.15124) — which names RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards).

2.1. Chain-of-thought and self-consistency

The prompt “let’s think step by step” triggers the generation of an intermediate reasoning chain z before the answer y — and the gains emerge with scale (clear from ~1011 parameters in the original study): the model allocates compute tokens to the problem. The formal reading:

Proposition 2.1 — Self-consistency = marginalization (Wang et al., 2023) Generating a chain z then an answer y realizes a joint model p(y, z \mid x) = p(z\mid x)\,p(y \mid x, z). The probability of the correct answer under the full model is the margin p(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{z} p(y, z \mid x).

Self-consistency approximates \arg\max_y p(y\mid x) by majority vote over k sampled chains z_1, \dots, z_k \sim p(\cdot \mid x): a Monte Carlo of “latent reasoning”. Typical gains: +10 to +20 points on GSM8K compared to greedy decoding — for an inference cost of ×k.

Proof. Sampling z_i \sim p(z\mid x) then y_i \sim p(y \mid x, z_i) produces pairs (y_i, z_i) distributed according to the joint; the count \frac1k \sum_i \mathbf{1}[y_i = y] is an unbiased estimator of \sum_z p(z \mid x)\,p(y \mid x, z) = p(y\mid x) (the two successive samplings compose exactly the joint).

The empirical argmax converges to the true argmax as k \to \infty (concentration of frequencies) — it is Monte Carlo estimation by the law of large numbers (Refresher Statistics ch. 4), with the particularity that the latent space is text. ∎

2.2. Process vs outcome rewards

Training a reward model on the final answer (ORM, outcome) is noisy: an answer that is right by chance receives the same reward as a clean reasoning. Lightman et al. build PRM800K (800,000 step-by-step labels given by humans) and train a PRM (process reward model): at equal labeling budget, the PRM selects better solutions on MATH than the ORM — supervision of the process is more informative than that of the outcome.

Immediate limit: step-by-step labeling is expensive and subjective; hence the idea of automating verification…

2.3. RLVR: verifiable rewards and the emergence of reasoning

Definition 2.2 — RLVR Instead of a learned reward model (subject to reward hacking), the reward is a verifiable rule: does the mathematical answer match the ground truth? does the code pass the tests? is the format correct? Training (GRPO — cf. LLMs module ch. 5; PPO — cf. RL module ch. 5) maximizes \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim \pi_\theta} \bigl[\, r_{\text{verif}}(x, y) \,\bigr] over domains where truth is decidable. Tulu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024) makes it the third stage of open post-training (SFT → DPO → RLVR).

Pivot result — DeepSeek-R1 (2025) R1-Zero: purely RL (no initial SFT) with rule-based rewards on math/code/format; the model develops by itself reasoning behaviors — verification, backtracking, reflection (“aha moment”), lengthening chains — measurable by the growing generation length over the course of training.

R1: adding cold-start data + multiple stages (SFT → RL → re-SFT) stabilizes and aligns, up to rivaling the best closed reasoning models on math/code benchmarks. Associated publication in Nature (2025) — read both: the technical account and the scientific letter do not tell exactly the same story.

Critical analysis (a whole session)

  1. Generalization: RLVR only improves domains close to pre-training + verifiable; transfer to “general” reasoning is debated.
  2. Benchmarks: MATH/AIME are saturated and contaminable (ch. 1); are the advances measured on the scale of their own pollution?
  3. Residual reward hacking: even “hard” rules are bypassable (misleading format, weak tests);
  4. Costs: chains of tens of thousands of tokens — reasoning is a product that is compute-bound at inference (cf. ML Ops module ch. 4, green ops ch. 10);
  5. Alternative reading: does R1-Zero select capabilities already present (pre-training) rather than create them? The distillation experiments (small models trained on R1’s traces outperforming their direct RL cousins) lean toward the first reading.

Exercise: defend both theses with the figures of the paper.

3. Long context: RoPE, ring attention, RAG or LC?

A model trained on short sequences must be made to read much longer ones, and one must decide whether to give it everything or only retrieved excerpts. This session reads how rotary positions are extended, how exact attention is distributed over GPUs, and what the comparisons between long context and RAG really show.

Today’s readings J. Su et al., RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), 2021 (arXiv:2104.09864); S. Chen et al., Extending Context Window via Positional Interpolation, 2023 (arXiv:2306.15595); B. Peng et al., YaRN, 2023 (arXiv:2309.00071); H. Liu et al., Ring Attention with Blockwise Transformers for Near-Infinite Context, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2310.01889); N. Liu et al., Lost in the Middle, 2023 (arXiv:2307.03172); Z. Li et al., Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?, EMNLP 2024 industry (arXiv:2407.16833); P. Xu et al., Retrieval meets Long Context LLMs, ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2310.03025).

3.1. RoPE: position by rotation

Definition 3.1 — RoPE (Su et al., 2021) Each coordinate pair (2i, 2i{+}1) of a query or key at position m is rotated by the angle m\theta_i, with \theta_i = b^{-2i/d} (base b, typically 10,000): q_m = R(m\Theta)\, q, \qquad k_n = R(n\Theta)\, k, \qquad \Theta = \mathrm{diag}(\theta_0, \dots). High frequencies \theta_i resolve local order, low frequencies the global one.

Proposition 3.2 — RoPE encodes only relative position (recap) For every pair of coordinates,

Key result — RoPE is relative \langle R(m\theta)q,\, R(n\theta)k\rangle = \langle R((m-n)\theta)\, q,\; k \rangle .

The one-line proof (plane rotations are orthogonal and commute) is LLMs Thm 2.3. What this seminar uses: extending the context amounts to re-parameterizing the angles, not to relearning positional embeddings.

3.2. Extending the window: interpolation and YaRN

Positional interpolation (Chen et al., 2023): trained with angles up to L, one serves L' > L by linearly compressing the positions m \mapsto m \cdot L/L' — the model sees angles already covered; a few hundred fine-tuning steps suffice (Llama-2 4k → 32k).

YaRN (Peng et al., 2023) refines: interpolate selectively by frequency (the low — global — frequencies are interpolated; the high — local — extrapolated; “NTK-aware”) and rescale the attention temperature (\times constant depending on the extension factor) to compensate for the dilution of the scores; result: an extension that reduces data and fine-tuning steps by roughly 10× compared to PI.

Spectral viewpoint (cf. Signal Processing module): handling a larger window = handling a wider frequency spectrum without aliasing.

3.3. Ring attention: cutting the memory cost in a ring

Proposition 3.3 — Ring Attention (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2023) Cut the sequence of length N into D blocks distributed over D GPUs; at each step, each GPU computes the attention of its query block against a key/value block received from a neighbor, then passes this block to the next (ring topology) — overlapping communication and computation.

After D steps, each query has seen all the blocks: the attention is exact (not an approximation), and the memory per GPU drops from \mathcal{O}(N) to \mathcal{O}(N/D). Demonstration at scale: contexts on the order of 32 million tokens on 32 A100 GPUs — ~27× the memory state of the art of the time.

3.4. RAG or long context? Lost in the middle and the hybrid router

“Lost in the Middle” (N. Liu et al., 2023): when the information is placed in the middle of a long context, performance collapses in a U shape (beginning and end favored) — a 128k window does not “read” its 128k uniformly.

The RAG vs LC dossier: Li et al. (2024) compare systematically on long-context tasks: with sufficient resources, long context wins on average (retrieval can miss the needle).

But RAG remains much more economical — and their Self-Route (the model estimates whether the query is answerable with the retrieved excerpts; if not, the whole document is sent) achieves LC quality for ~39% less cost.

Xu et al. (ICLR 2024) complete the picture: retrieval improves models whatever the window (4k/16k/32k), and a 4k + retrieval approaches a 16k without retrieval — RAG is not dead, it is complementary.

Critical analysis

  1. “Needle in a haystack” (finding a sentence planted in a long text) is a retrieval test, not a long-context reasoning test — multi-document aggregation benchmarks break the scoreboard;
  2. the KV cache grows linearly with N (LLMs Prop. 6.2; serving in ML Ops ch. 3): “free” LC at the API is an economic choice of the provider, not a physical property;
  3. RoPE extensions are measured in perplexity and in downstream tasks — YaRN shows they can diverge;
  4. Self-Route relies on the model’s self-assessment (meta-cognition): how good is it on adversarial queries?

To be related to ch. 4 (memory) and the ML Ops module ch. 6 (index cost vs context cost).

4. Memory: episodic, vector stores, GraphRAG

What a model sees is limited by its context window, and what it learned is frozen in its weights. This session compares the ways of giving an LLM a memory beyond both: raw key-value memories, episodic memory streams, memory paged by the model itself, and graph-structured memory.

Today’s readings Y. Wu et al., Memorizing Transformers, ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2203.08913); J. Park et al., Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior, UIST 2023 (arXiv:2304.03442); C. Packer et al., MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems, 2023 (arXiv:2310.08560); D. Edge et al., From Local to Global: A Graph RAG Approach, 2024 (arXiv:2404.16130).

4.1. A taxonomy of memories

Type Cognitive analogue Implementation Example (paper)
Context working memory KV window — (cf. ch. 3 here, LLMs ch. 2)
Episodic event memories journal + retrieval (recency/importance/relevance) Generative Agents (2023)
External semantic knowledge vector store / index (RAG) LLMs ch. 7, ML Ops ch. 6

Paged — context + external storage managed by the model MemGPT (2023)

4.2. Three papers, three ideas of memory

  • Memorizing Transformers (Wu et al., 2022): an attention layer, non-differentiable, looks up its keys/values in an external memory of (k, v) pairs by nearest neighbors (kNN) over past tokens — including other documents. The model learns to use a raw memory; effective for perplexity, but the memory is frozen (no update during generation).
  • Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023): 25 simulated agents in a virtual small town; each agent owns a timestamped memory stream; retrieval weights recency × importance × relevance; a reflection process periodically synthesizes the memories into higher-level beliefs (“I should organize a party”), re-injected as memories. The conceptual architecture of every memory-equipped conversational agent.
  • MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023): the model becomes its own operating system: main context (RAM) vs external storage (disk), paging driven by function calls the LLM triggers itself (core_memory_append, archival_memory_insert…). Memory management becomes a learned/instructed behavior — the ancestor of the “agent + notepad” loops.
  • GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024): structured memory — an entity graph extracted by an LLM, communities detected (Leiden), hierarchical summaries; global questions (“which themes?”) become a map-reduce over the community summaries, where vector RAG falls apart (cf. Graph ML module ch. 10 for the graph foundations; ML Ops module ch. 6 for the indexing cost).

Proposition 4.1 — The retrieval triptych of generative agents The retrieval score of a memory s at time t for the query q reads \mathrm{score}(s; q, t) \;=\; w_r\, e^{-\Delta t/\tau} \;+\; w_i\, \mathrm{imp}(s) \;+\; w_p\, \cos\bigl(e(s), e(q)\bigr), exponential recency + importance (predicted by the LLM at writing time) + semantic relevance. This is exactly the signal fusion of a search engine reranker (cf. NLP ch. 2): “agent memory” reinvents learning to rank — the originality lies in the reflection loop that creates new synthetic memories.

Critical analysis

  1. Evaluation: each of these papers evaluates memory differently (perplexity, simulated interviews, multi-session dialogues, global questions) — no consensus benchmark; wariness toward small-sample gains.
  2. Writing: the hard question is what one stores (everything? a summary? a graph?) and when one forgets; MemGPT delegates to the model, GraphRAG to a pipeline — two philosophies (agency vs infrastructure).
  3. The return of context: with windows of a million tokens (ch. 3), “keep everything in the context” becomes credible again — until the next “lost in the middle”.
  4. LLGA position: graph-structured memory is the exact place where the master crosses its two legs (LLMs + graphs); ch. 8 comes back to it.

5. Mixture-of-Experts

A larger model is usually better but costs more per token. Mixture-of-experts layers break this link by activating only a few sub-networks per token; this session reads how the routing works, how the load is balanced, and what the experts actually learn.

Today’s readings N. Shazeer et al., Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer, ICLR 2017 (arXiv:1701.06538); W. Fedus, B. Zoph, N. Shazeer, Switch Transformers, JMLR 2022 (arXiv:2101.03961); A. Jiang et al., Mixtral of Experts, 2024 (arXiv:2401.04088); DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report, 2024 (arXiv:2412.19437).

Definition 5.1 — Sparse MoE layer Each feed-forward block is replaced by N experts E_1, \dots, E_N and a router: y \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}(g(x))} g_i(x)\, E_i(x), \qquad g(x) = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(W_g\,x\bigr), only the k strongest experts are computed (k = 1-2 typically).

The total parameters grow (capacity), the active parameters per token remain bounded (cost): Mixtral 8×7B = ~47B parameters, ~13B active; DeepSeek-V3 = 671B of which ~37B active.

5.1. Balancing the experts: the auxiliary loss

Proposition 5.2 — Auxiliary load-balancing loss (Switch) With f_i = fraction of tokens routed to expert i and P_i = average router probability mass on i:

Key result — load-balancing loss \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \;=\; \alpha\, N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i\, P_i, \qquad \sum_i f_i = \sum_i P_i = 1.

If (f_i) and (P_i) are similarly ordered (co-monotone: the most loaded experts are also the most probable), then \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \ge \alpha, with equality at uniform routing f = P = \mathbf{1}/N. Without this condition the bound can fail: LLMs ch. 2 and its Ex. 7 give a counterexample.

The loss penalizes the degenerate routing where a few experts capture everything — otherwise the router gradient collapses (the unpicked experts receive no gradient: the rich get richer).

Proof. At the uniform point, \sum_i f_iP_i = N \cdot \frac1N \cdot \frac1N = \frac1N, \quad \text{so} \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha N \cdot \frac1N = \alpha.

To show that this is indeed a minimum in the targeted regime, we use Chebyshev’s sum inequality: if the sequences (f_i) and (P_i) are similarly ordered (co-monotone — the likely experts are also the most loaded, exactly the degenerate regime that we want to penalize), then \frac1N \sum_i f_i P_i \;\geq\; \Bigl(\frac1N \sum_i f_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac1N \sum_i P_i\Bigr) = \frac{1}{N^2}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \sum_i f_i P_i \;\geq\; \frac1N, with equality if and only if one of the sequences is constant. Hence \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \geq \alpha, equality at uniform.

Finally, the dynamic effect: \partial (f_iP_i)/\partial P_i = f_i \geq 0 — the gradient pulls P_i down proportionally to the load f_i: a negative feedback loop against concentration. ∎

Critical analysis — read the hidden assumption The lower bound holds only for co-monotone (f, P), as the statement says: an anti-monotone pair (a very likely but lightly loaded expert) can have a product below 1/N — the loss is not a symmetric divergence in the strict sense; it is a load proxy whose gradient dynamics does most of the work.

This is exactly the kind of detail a critical paper reading must catch: what quantity does the loss actually measure, under what assumption, and what does its gradient do?

DeepSeek-V3 goes as far as replacing the loop with auxiliary-loss-free balancing: a per-expert bias term, dynamically adjusted to the observed load — direct control rather than a differentiable penalty, with reported quality gains. Less elegant, more effective: up for debate.

5.2. What the experts learn

The Mixtral analysis (the “routing” figure) shows syntactic and domain-level specialization (code, math, languages) rather than fine-grained topics — and substantial redundancy across experts: the MoE mostly democratizes capacity, not the division of labor.

DeepSeek-V3 pushes two structural innovations: fine-grained experts (more numerous, smaller) + shared experts (always active, capturing cross-cutting knowledge — reducing the redundancy), and FP8 training at scale (14.8T tokens) — the technical report is a systems-engineering course in itself (expert-parallel communication, cf. Emerging ML module ch. 2 on distributed computing).

Critique in practice — what are the “active parameters” for? Comparing a 47B/13B-active MoE to a 13B dense model is misleading: the MoE keeps 47B in memory (cf. ML Ops module ch. 3: the KV cache and the weights reside in full in VRAM) but ~13B of compute per token. MoE wins provided large batches (amortizing memory through throughput): excellent for shared serving, debatable for single-user. The LLGA reading grid: every architecture must be evaluated at full cost (memory, compute, communication) — not in advertising “parameters”.

6. Alternative architectures: SSM, Mamba, RWKV

Attention costs grow with the square of the sequence length and its cache grows linearly. This session reads the architectures that replace it with a recurrent state of fixed size — state space models, Mamba, RWKV — and the hybrids that keep a little attention for exact recall.

Today’s readings A. Gu, K. Goel, C. Ré, Efficiently Modeling Long Sequences with Structured State Spaces (S4), ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2111.00396); A. Gu & T. Dao, Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces, 2023 (arXiv:2312.00752); B. Peng et al., RWKV: Reinventing RNNs for the Transformer Era, Findings of EMNLP 2023 (arXiv:2305.13048); S. De et al., Griffin: Mixing Gated Linear Recurrences with Local Attention for Efficient Language Models (Hawk / Griffin), ICML 2024 (arXiv:2402.19427); AI21, Jamba: A Hybrid Transformer-Mamba Language Model, 2024 (arXiv:2403.19887).

6.1. From RNNs to state space models

Definition 6.1 — Discrete SSM A state space model maps the input u_t to the output y_t via a linear hidden state x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d: x_{t} = \bar A\, x_{t-1} + \bar B\, u_t, \qquad y_t = C\, x_t, where (\bar A, \bar B) come from the (zero-order) discretization of the continuous system \dot x = A x + B u with step \Delta: \bar A = \exp(\Delta A), \qquad \bar B = (\Delta A)^{-1}\bigl(\exp(\Delta A) - I\bigr)\,\Delta B. The classic RNN reappears with a structured state matrix (S4: diagonal + low-rank terms, inherited from HiPPO to memorize the long history).

Proposition 6.2 — LTI ⇒ convolution (S4) If (\bar A, \bar B, C) are constant (independent of time and input — LTI), then the system is a convolution product: y_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \overline{K}_s\, u_{t-s}, \qquad \overline{K} = \bigl(C\bar B,\; C\bar A \bar B,\; C\bar A^2 \bar B,\; \dots\bigr), computable in \mathcal{O}(L \log L) via FFT (Signal Processing module): parallel training over the whole sequence, like a transformer.

Proof. Let us unroll the recurrence from x_{-1} = 0 (zero initial state): x_0 = \bar B u_0; \qquad x_1 = \bar A \bar B u_0 + \bar B u_1; by immediate induction, x_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \bar A^{\,t-s}\, \bar B\, u_s. Then y_t = C x_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} (C \bar A^{\,t-s} \bar B)\, u_s = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \overline K_{t-s}\, u_s , a discrete convolution with kernel \overline K_s = C \bar A^s \bar B, where the structure of \bar A (diagonal) makes each term computable in \mathcal{O}(d). ∎

6.2. Mamba: making the state selective

The LTI SSM is a fixed filter: it cannot choose to memorize or forget depending on the content. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) makes \Delta_t, \bar B_t, C_t functions of the input (small MLPs on u_t): the selection.

Cascade consequence: the convolution (prop. 6.2) is destroyed (the kernel depends on position) → back to a recurrence, but parallelizable via the prefix scan with fused GPU kernels (“hardware-aware”): \mathcal{O}(L) training, \mathcal{O}(1) inference per token and constant state memory — the selling point against the transformer’s KV cache (LLMs Prop. 6.2).

The scan in two lines: each step x_t=\bar A_t x_{t-1}+\bar B_t u_t is the affine map (A_t, b_t)=(\bar A_t, \bar B_t u_t), and affine maps compose associatively, (A_2, b_2)\circ(A_1, b_1) = (A_2A_1,\; A_2b_1 + b_2). A prefix scan computes all the prefixes of an associative operation in \mathcal{O}(L) work and \mathcal{O}(\log L) parallel steps (Blelloch, 1990: an up-sweep of pairwise products over a binary tree, then a down-sweep).

Finally, Mamba embeds the SSM inside a gated MLP-style block. Mamba-2 (SSD) will reformulate the SSM as a form of linear attention — the loop with attention comes full circle.

6.3. RWKV and the hybrids

Proposition 6.3 — RWKV: linearizing attention into an RNN (Peng et al., 2023) RWKV rewrites attention as a recurrence with per-channel decay (time-mixing): w_t = \sigma(W_t)\odot w_{t-1} + k_t, \qquad y_t = r_t \odot \frac{v_t}{w_t + \epsilon}, (schematic form): the state w_t accumulates past values channel-wise with a learned exponential decay (r_t = receptance, v_t the step’s value) — a linear attention normalized over time, trainable in parallel (like a transformer), executable as an RNN (constant memory).

At scale: models up to 14B — the largest RNNs ever trained at its publication.

Hybrids: pure SSMs lose on exact recall tasks (copying an association seen a long time ago: the compressed state has no room to keep everything — a recurring critique, cf. the associative recall studies).

Hence mixed architectures: Jamba (AI21, 2024) interleaves one attention layer for every seven Mamba layers, with MoE in every other MLP, and shows that this one layer in eight suffices to restore recall; Griffin/De et al. (2024) alternate recurrence (RG-LRU) and local attention.

The 2024-2026 consensus: attention is irreplaceable for precise retrieval; the SSM excels at long context on a constant memory budget; the hybrid beats both.

Critical analysis

  1. The “Mamba matches transformers at X times less memory” curves are established at calibrated data and compute sizes; transformers have an ecosystem (serving, quantization, ch. 7) that SSMs lack — the real adoption cost exceeds the paper.
  2. Exact recall is a limit case (one key among millions): needle-in-a-haystack-style benchmarks are highly sensitive to it, comprehension tasks far less so.
  3. RWKV illustrates the virtue of deployable simplicity (no KV cache, no keys/values); Mamba that of algorithmic design (the scan).
  4. A favorite exam question: “why is the transformer KV cache a feature and not just a cost?” — because it stores the history exactly (perfect recall), where the SSM state summarizes it.

7.1. Speculative decoding: the small proposes, the large disposes

Definition 7.1 — Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) A draft model q (small, fast) generates \gamma tokens; the target model p (large, slow) verifies them in a single parallel pass (the \gamma positions are checked simultaneously — prefilling); each accepted token is free, the first rejected one is corrected by a residual sample.

The distribution of p is preserved exactly (proved in LLMs Thm 6.3: correction by sampling according to \mathrm{norm}(\max(0, p - q)) at the rejection point). Typical ×2-3 speedups at identical quality.

Theorem 7.2 — Expected number of tokens per iteration If \alpha is the (average) probability that a token of q is accepted by p, the expected number of tokens generated per full iteration (verification + correction) is

Key result — speculative decoding \mathbb{E}[\#\text{tokens}] \;=\; \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma + 1}}{1 - \alpha}.

With \alpha = 0.8 and \gamma = 4: ≈ 3.36 tokens per target pass instead of 1.

Proof. Let A be the number of tokens accepted before the first rejection. The acceptance of each token is modeled as independent with probability \alpha; then A \sim \mathrm{Geo}(\alpha) truncated at \gamma:

  • in case of rejection at position j < \gamma, the iteration produces j tokens accepted plus the corrected token, i.e. j+1 tokens;
  • if all \gamma are accepted, the pass produces \gamma tokens plus one bonus token sampled from p (position \gamma{+}1 is directly generated by the target in the same pass).

In both cases: j+1 tokens for all j \leq \gamma. Hence \mathbb{E} = \sum_{j=0}^{\gamma-1} (1-\alpha)\,\alpha^{j}\,(j+1) \;+\; \alpha^{\gamma}\,(\gamma + 1). We recognize the truncated series of the geometric expectation: by using \sum_{j=0}^{m} \alpha^j = \frac{1-\alpha^{m+1}}{1-\alpha} and its derivative for the (j+1) factor, the sum reduces exactly to \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}. ∎

Critical analysis The gain depends on three clocks: \alpha (small/large agreement — high on ordinary text, low on tricky code or creative outputs); the cost ratio c (verifying \gamma tokens costs more than one decoding step — prefilling); \gamma (too large = many rejections at the last rank).

Leviathan et al. give the optimal \gamma as a function of (\alpha, c) — a lab exercise. Descendants: self-speculation (the model verifies itself via skipped layers), multiple heads (Medusa), trees — to be documented in your tech watch.

7.2. Quantization: GPTQ and AWQ

Proposition 7.3 — GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023): layer by layer, Hessian in hand Quantize W into \hat W (e.g. 4 bits, with an error \Delta = W - \hat W) by minimizing the error on the outputs actually produced: \min_{\hat W}\; \bigl\| W X - \hat W X \bigr\|_F^2 \;=\; \min_{\Delta}\; \mathrm{tr}\bigl(\Delta\, X X^\top\, \Delta^\top\bigr), \qquad H = 2 X X^\top, where X is a sample of calibration activations.

The algorithm proceeds column by column (greedy): quantize a block of weights, compute the error on the outputs, propagate it onto the remaining columns via the Cholesky inverse of H — a GPU-friendly approximation of the Optimal Brain Surgeon.

OBS (Hassibi & Stork, 1993) removes one weight w_q and corrects all the others by the second-order optimal update \delta w = -\frac{w_q}{[H^{-1}]_{qq}}\,H^{-1}e_q , which raises the quadratic loss by w_q^2/(2[H^{-1}]_{qq}); GPTQ applies the same update with the quantization error w_q-\hat w_q in place of w_q.

Models with billions of parameters are quantized to ~4 bits in a few GPU-hours, with low perplexity losses.

Proposition 7.4 — AWQ (Lin et al., 2024): protecting the salient channels Observation: ~1% of activation channels (per matrix) carry extreme amplitudes; quantizing them naively destroys performance.

AWQ does not quantize them separately — it rescales: multiplying an input channel by s and dividing the weight column by s preserves the exact mathematics of the layer while moving the quantization error away from the salient channels (the optimal scale is estimated by search over the calibration activations, without backpropagation).

Result: robust 4 bits, no exotic per-group hybrid reconstruction, commodity deployment.

Method Idea Cost/advantage Limitation

Critical analysis Perplexity is a poor downstream proxy: a 4-bit model nearly identical in PPL can lose points on long reasoning or safety (learned refusals are subtle — cf. Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 6); always re-evaluate after quantization on the golden set (ML Ops module ch. 2). Energy: quantization is the #2 green ops lever (ML Ops ch. 10), after functional sobriety.

8.1. The “bare” LLM facing serialized graphs

Pivotal result — NLGraph (Wang et al., 2023) 29,370 problems in natural language covering 8 tasks (connectivity, shortest path, maximum independent set… up to the Hamiltonian path and max flow), increasing difficulty (graph size, number of steps). GPT-3/GPT-4 clearly beat chance on the easy tasks… and collapse on the hard ones; the authors propose Build-a-Graph (making the graph explicit in the prompt) and a graph translator (converting the output into structure), which help — without closing the gap with a few-line algorithms.

Measured conclusion: LLMs have a preliminary graphical intuition, not an algorithmic skill.

Proposition 8.1 — Encoding is a first-order variable (Fatemi et al., 2024) “Talk like a Graph” systematically studies how to serialize a graph: edge lists, matrix, incident relations, degree-based ordering, etc. Findings:

  1. the encoding choice shifts accuracy by tens of points — more than the model choice in some regimes;
  2. the degree-based order helps overall;
  3. performance decays with graph size far faster than with text length — it is the structure, not the length, that costs.

Practical takeaway: serializing a graph is an engineering design act (and of research), not a formality.

8.2. Hybridizing: GNN-encoder + LLM-decoder

Definition 8.2 — G-Retriever (He et al., NeurIPS 2024) To answer questions over a large textual graph (scenes, knowledge maps):

  1. retrieve the relevant subgraph by formulating retrieval as a prize-collecting Steiner tree (PCST — “relevant” nodes carry prizes, edges carry costs: maximize prize − cost of the connected subtree);
  2. encode the subgraph with a GNN (message passing — Graph ML module ch. 5);
  3. inject node embeddings as soft tokens into the LLM (learned adapter);
  4. joint fine-tuning.

Reported effects: better answers and reduced hallucinations on ExplaGraphs/SceneGraphs/WebQSP, scalable to graphs of hundreds of thousands of nodes (one never sends the whole graph in the prompt).

9. Agents: planning, tools, multi-agents

An agent is an LLM that acts in a loop: it reasons, calls a tool, reads the result and continues. This session reads the papers that defined this loop, taught models when to call tools, let them learn from their failures in context, and organized several agents together — and asks how reliable long chains of actions can be.

Schick et al., Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2302.04761); N. 9.1. ReAct: reasoning and acting

The ReAct prompt alternates reasoning traces (Thought) and actions (Action / Observation) within a single trajectory: the model unrolls “thought → action → observation → thought → …”. On HotpotQA and ALFWorld, ReAct beats pure chain-of-thought (fewer hallucinations of impossible steps — the world answers) and pure acting (the observations are interpreted).

It becomes the default grammar of agents — that of the native “function calls” of current LLMs. RL link ch. 7: ReAct is a policy over an interaction tree; the chain-of-thought, its narrative value function.

9.2. Toolformer: learning where to call the tools

Proposition 9.1 — Toolformer’s self-training criterion For each candidate position t, insert an API call annotated c(t) (the call and its result); compare the next-token prediction loss without the call vs with its result provided: I_t \;=\; -\log p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}\bigr) \;+\; \log p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}, c(t)\bigr), (schematic form): I_t > 0 — the call reduces the loss — flags a useful call.

The model itself filters its own retained calls, trains on them, and learns to decide when to compute (calculator, retrieval, translation, calendar…) — the direct precursor of the native tool-calls of current LLMs (LLMs module ch. 8).

9.3. Reflection, multi-agent, and the question of emergence

  • Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023): after a failure, a verbal feedback (self-generated or from the environment) is stored and re-injected at the next attempt — “verbal RL”: improvement happens in the context, not in the weights. Striking gains on HumanEval and ALFWorld at zero training cost.
  • CAMEL (Li et al., 2023): two agents role-play (user/assistant) to generate instruction data — the multi-agent conversation as a data synthesis engine.
  • AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023): a framework where configurable agents (LLM, human, tool) converse programmatically — the industrial design pattern of multi-agent teams (orchestrator, critics, executors), to be connected to the guardrails and the tracing of the module ML Ops ch. 8.

Proposition 9.2 — The unforgiving arithmetic of chains A k-step task with per-step success p succeeds with p^k (independence): p = 0.98, \quad k = 50 \quad\to\quad 36\%. For a reliable agent, three levers: shorten the chains (composite tools), make each step reliable (Reflexion, retries, deterministic tools), and avoid long chains where an algorithm exists — an agent that plans a topological sort step by step is an architecture mistake (ch. 8: NLGraph).

Critical analysis

  1. Evaluation: agent gains are measured on environments (ALFWorld, HumanEval, SWE-bench-like) whose fidelity to the real world is debated — unmodeled costs (ML Ops ch. 4/8), noisy environment, absent adversary.
  2. Multi-agent emergence: when “several agents” beat “one agent”, is it emergence — or the effect of a better prompt / more compute? Several ablation studies suggest the second reading: demand honest ablations (a single agent, same token budget).
  3. Safety: every tool is an injection surface (Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 5–6); multi-agent environments multiply implicit trusts — the least-privilege pattern (ML Ops ch. 8-9) remains the only serious defense.
  4. RL link: the LLM agent as a policy — agentic “behaviors” are now trained by RLVR on trajectories (ch. 2): the prompt/training boundary is shifting under our eyes.

10. Open problems

The seminar ends with problems that no one has solved yet: the finite stock of human text, the risk of training on generated data, and updating a model without retraining it. Each is a source of internship and thesis topics.

Today’s readings P. Villalobos, J. Sevilla, L. Heim, et al., Will we run out of data? Limits of LLM scaling based on human-generated data, 2022 (arXiv:2211.04325); I. Shumailov et al., AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data (the “curse of recursion”), Nature 2024; H. Yu et al., Continual Learning of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey, 2024 (arXiv:2402.01344).

10.1. Data exhaustion

Proposition 10.1 — The Villalobos et al. (2022) window The stock of “quality” public text produced by humans is finite (on the order of a few 1014 tokens actually usable); training set sizes grow exponentially (~2 to 2.5×/year at the time of the study).

By extrapolating the two curves, the effective stock would be exhausted between 2026 and 2032 — high-quality data first. This is not a prediction of progress stopping but a regime change: the constraint shifts to the data side.

The answers in progress:

  • synthetic data (models generate their own training material — with the risk of model collapse: Shumailov et al. show, on toy models and Gaussians, that recursive training on generated data erodes the distribution tails — diversity collapses generation after generation);
  • data efficiency (tokens counted, curriculum, “quality over quantity”);
  • new sources (audio, video, interactions);
  • rights — the question becomes legal (AI Act: copyright policy of general-purpose models — LLMs 9.6 and Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 10) before being purely technical.

10.2. Continual learning

LLMs are frozen at publication: any change requires a new training run or a fine-tune (with its train: catastrophic forgetting of earlier capabilities, safety regression — ML Ops module ch. 2 and 7).

Continual learning targets incremental updating: replay of a core of experiences, regularization toward the previous weights, additive modules (successive LoRAs, merging), model merging (combining the weights of specialists — simple averages, TIES: surprising and under-theorized results).

The Yu et al. (2024) survey maps the field; the honest assessment: no scheme dominates yet, and the success criterion itself is blurry (remember what, from how many versions back?).

10.3. Other fronts — and conclusion of the seminar

  • Memory vs context vs weights (ch. 3-4): where should knowledge be stored — parameters, KV, external index? The marginal cost of each byte differs by three orders of magnitude; the answer will depend on serving economics (ML Ops module ch. 3-4);
  • Reasoning beyond the verifiable (ch. 2): extending reliable rewards beyond math/code;
  • Safety at scale: statistical alignment facing competing objectives (Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 5–6);
  • Interpretability as a science: from probing tools (linear probes, sparse autoencoders) to verifiable mechanisms (Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs ch. 1–3);
  • LLGA: the LLM × graphs confrontation (ch. 8) remains an open construction site — serialization, GNN-LLM hybrids, incremental GraphRAG: your internship topics.

This is the skill the master wants to install: quickly read the essentials, doubt methodically, and connect every advance to the complete system (data → training → serving → evaluation → safety) described by the LLMs, ML Ops and Explainability/Security/Privacy modules.

Sample questions. Five board questions worth 20 points in total; set as a written check, they take 1 h.

Q1 (4 pts) — Self-consistency. A model samples a chain z\sim p(z\mid x), then an answer y\sim p(y\mid x,z). Show that the vote frequency of an answer over k independent samples is an unbiased estimator of p(y\mid x) (Prop. 2.1). Why must the vote be taken on the extracted final answer, and what does it cost?

Proof. Marking points

  • Each pair (y_i,z_i) follows the joint law, so \mathbb{P}(y_i=y)=\sum_z p(z\mid x)\,p(y\mid x,z)=p(y\mid x) (2 pts).
  • The frequency is a mean of k indicators, unbiased; by the law of large numbers the majority answer converges to \arg\max_y p(y\mid x) when that maximiser is unique (1 pt).
  • Two chains almost never coincide word for word, so a vote on raw chains is meaningless; the cost is k times the generation tokens (1 pt). Common error: saying the vote estimates p(z\mid x).

Q2 (4 pts) — Choosing the draft length. With acceptance rate \alpha=0.8 and a draft step costing c=0.05 target forward, compute the expected tokens per target pass (Thm 7.2) and the speed-up for \gamma\in\{2,4,8\}. What changes when \alpha drops to 0.5?

Proof. Marking points

  • Tokens per pass (1-\alpha^{\gamma+1})/(1-\alpha): 2.44, 3.36 and 4.33 (1.5 pts).
  • Speed-up, tokens per pass divided by 1+\gamma c: 2.22, 2.80 and 3.09, so \gamma=8 is the best of the three (the optimum is flat between 6 and 10) (1.5 pts).
  • At \alpha=0.5 the speed-ups are 1.59, 1.62 and 1.43: long drafts are wasted once a rejection is likely, and the gain shrinks to about 1.6 (1 pt). Common error: forgetting the draft cost \gamma c.

Q3 (4 pts) — Reading the hidden assumption. Two experts, top-1 routing: 90 % of the tokens have router probabilities (0.51, 0.49) and 10 % have (0, 1). Compute f, P and \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}/\alpha (Prop. 5.2). What does this say about the statement, and what does auxiliary-loss-free balancing do instead?

Proof. Marking points

  • f=(0.9, 0.1) and P=(0.459, 0.541) (1 pt).
  • N\sum_i f_iP_i=2\times0.467=0.934<1: below the value at uniform routing (1 pt).
  • Here f and P are oppositely ordered, so Chebyshev’s inequality does not apply: the bound of Prop. 5.2 needs the co-monotone condition, and the loss is a load proxy that works through its gradient (1 pt).
  • DeepSeek-V3 adds a per-expert bias to the routing scores, raised or lowered according to the observed load, instead of a differentiable penalty (1 pt). Common error: claiming the uniform point is a global minimum without condition.

Q4 (4 pts) — Agent reliability. An agent needs k=50 steps, each succeeding independently with p=0.98 (Prop. 9.2). Compute the task success rate, the per-step reliability needed for 90 % task success, and the success rate when every step may be retried once, independently, after a detected failure.

Proof. Marking points

  • 0.98^{50}\approx0.364 (1 pt).
  • p\ge0.9^{1/50}\approx0.9979 (1 pt).
  • With one retry a step fails with probability 0.02^2, so the task succeeds with (1-0.0004)^{50}\approx0.980 (1 pt).
  • Retries only help when failures are detected and independent; shortening chains and deterministic tools act on k itself (1 pt). Common error: adding the step failure rates instead of multiplying the successes.

Q5 (4 pts) — A contaminated score. A model scores 85 % on the public GSM8K test and 71 % on re-generated variants of the same problems; 18 % of the test items have a 13-gram overlap with a web corpus, and the model gets 97 % of those right. Compute its accuracy on the items without overlap and interpret the three numbers (Prop. 1.2).

Proof. Marking points

  • Solve 0.85=0.18\times0.97+0.82\,x: x\approx0.824 (1.5 pts).
  • The detected overlap explains only about 3 of the 14 points of the raw–variant gap; the rest comes from contamination that n-grams miss (paraphrases, translated copies) or from variants being harder (1.5 pts).
  • Report raw score, variant score and overlap together, and treat the gap as an interval for the true capability, not as noise (1 pt). Common error: concluding from the overlap rate alone that the score is clean.
