How to study this module

  • Redo the proofs on scratch paper: every theorem fits on one page, and it is by rewriting them that one retains the exact hypotheses.
  • Implement Laplace noise and “split” conformal yourself (a few dozen lines): the guarantees become tangible when you see the coverage histogram.
  • Whenever an \varepsilon appears, ask yourself which pair of neighboring datasets it distinguishes — that is the only way to give it meaning.
  • Work through the two parts in order but in parallel in your head: chapters 6–12 replay in UQ the same moves as in privacy (guarantee, budget, utility trade-off).
  • Do the exercises before reading the solutions, and compare your intervals with those of others: coverage is verified, not declared.

0. Reminders: probability, concentration, DP as seen in M1

Prerequisites (M1 modules)

  • Concentration inequalities (cf. Machine Learning module, ch. 0): Markov \mathbb{P}(Z \geq t) \leq \mathbb{E}Z/t, Chernoff/Hoeffding for sums of Bernoullis — they provide the guarantees of the mechanisms and conformal coverage;
  • Differential privacy, first encounter (cf. Emerging ML module, chapter 5): \varepsilon-DP definition, Laplace noise, DP-SGD as a conclusion of federated learning — the present module takes up this basis and develops it: mechanisms (ch. 2), fine composition (ch. 3), industrial implementations (ch. 4–5);
  • Generalization theory (cf. Machine Learning module, ch. 7): bias-variance trade-off, error decomposition — the UQ part (ch. 6–12) answers the dual question: not just “what risk on average”, but “what guarantee on this prediction”;
  • Training of networks (cf. Deep Learning module, ch. 3 and 10): SGD, mini-batches, dropout — DP-SGD (ch. 4) and MC dropout (ch. 8) modify them marginally but change their nature;
  • Statistics of tests and intervals (cf. Refresher Statistics module, ch. 4 and 6; Statistics in Action module, ch. 7 and 10): frequentist guarantees, p-values, bootstrap — the language of the coverage guarantees of ch. 9.

Why these two parts in a single module? Privacy and UQ are the two faces of the “right to error” of learning systems: privacy quantifies what a model can reveal about the training data, UQ quantifies what it can assert about a new input. Both escape evaluation by accuracy alone; both provide formal guarantees (inequalities, theorems) rather than empirical promises; and both converge on LLMs (ch. 11): models large enough to memorize and confident enough to mislead. The thread: make the uncertainties computable, then arbitrate them.

1. Differential privacy: the foundation

Concrete scene: a medical classifier shows 0.97 confidence on an OOD case. Without conformal / calibration, the clinic cannot set a reject threshold.

What breaks Releasing with no DP budget; mistaking softmax for calibrated probability; ignoring composition of privacy mechanisms.

The historical motivation (reconstructed by Dinur-Nissim, 2003): publishing “anonymized” statistics about a database is often enough to reconstruct the individual data by cross-referencing enough queries — naive anonymization (removing name/SSN) does not resist. The answer of Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith (2006): define protection as a property of the mechanism (the randomized algorithm), not of the data.

Definition 1.1 — Neighboring and differential privacy Two databases x, x' are neighboring if they differ in the data of a single individual (one row added/removed, or modified: one of the two conventions will be specified). A randomized mechanism M is \varepsilon-DP if for every pair of neighboring databases and every subset S of outputs: \mathbb{P}(M(x) \in S) \;\leq\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \mathbb{P}(M(x') \in S). (\varepsilon, \delta) version: the inequality may be violated with probability \leq \delta (a figure to be read as “cryptographically improbable”, \delta \ll 1/n). Reading: changing the data of one person changes the distribution of the published conclusions by at most (multiplicatively) e^\varepsilon — so these conclusions reveal little about that person, whatever the adversary’s side knowledge.

Proposition 1.2 — Three structural properties (i) Post-processing: if M is \varepsilon-DP, any g \circ M (subsequent analysis, training, selective publication) still is — privacy does not degrade through processing of the outputs. (ii) Group privacy: if x, x' differ on k individuals, \mathbb{P}(M(x) \in S) \leq e^{k\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(M(x') \in S). (iii) Convexity: a mixture of \varepsilon-DP mechanisms is \varepsilon-DP.

Proof. (i) For every section A, \mathbb{P}(g(M(x)) \in S) = \mathbb{P}(M(x) \in g^{-1}(S)) \leq e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(M(x') \in g^{-1}(S)) = e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(g(M(x')) \in S) — the direct image of a dominated measure preserves the Radon-Nikodym ratio. (ii) A chain of k intermediate neighboring databases and multiplication of the e^\varepsilon. (iii) For a mixture with weights \lambda_j over M_j all \varepsilon-DP: \sum_j \lambda_j \mathbb{P}(M_j(x) \in S) \leq \sum_j \lambda_j e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(M_j(x') \in S) = e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(\text{mixture}(x') \in S). ∎

Definition 1.3 — Sensitivity The \ell_1 (resp. \ell_2) sensitivity of a query f on neighboring databases: \Delta_1 f = \max_{x \sim x'} \\|f(x) - f(x')\\|_1, \qquad \Delta_2 f = \max_{x \sim x'} \\|f(x) - f(x')\\|_2. Computed examples: counting (how many individuals satisfy Q?) \Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1 (one row changes the count by at most 1); mean over n rows in [0, 1] \Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1/n (adding one row shifts the mean by at most 1/n); histogram with k bins \Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1 (one row falls in one bin — under the add/remove convention); maximum \Rightarrow \Delta_1 unbounded (hence the systematic clipping in practice, ch. 4). Sensitivity measures “how much one individual can weigh” — it is what will set the noise.

Choosing \varepsilon — the embarrassing question No universal rule, but industry benchmarks: 2020 US Census: a global \varepsilon on the order of twenty in total for the person file (Abowd, 2018); Apple (keyboard telemetry): \varepsilon on the order of 4–8 per day; Google (RAPPOR): \varepsilon \approx \ln 3 per report, cumulative budgets; academic literature: \varepsilon \leq 1 “strict”, 1–10 “reasonable”. The essential point: \varepsilon is a budget that one spends (ch. 3), not a moral grade.

2. Mechanisms: Laplace, Gaussian, exponential

Theorem 2.1 — The Laplace mechanism (Dwork et al., 2006) Let f have sensitivity \Delta_1. The mechanism M(x) = f(x) + (Z_1, \dots, Z_k), \qquad Z_i \sim \mathrm{Laplace}\big(0, \tfrac{\Delta_1}{\varepsilon}\big)\ \text{i.i.d.} is \varepsilon-DP. The Laplace law with parameter b has density p(z) = \frac{1}{2b} e^{-\|z\|/b}.

Proof. For an output z \in \mathbb{R}^k, the ratio of densities equals \frac{p_x(z)}{p_{x'}(z)} = \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\!\Big( -\frac{\varepsilon}{\Delta_1}\big( \|z_i - f_i(x)\| - \|z_i - f_i(x')\| \big) \Big) = \exp\!\Big( \frac{\varepsilon}{\Delta_1} \sum_i \big( \|z_i - f_i(x')\| - \|z_i - f_i(x)\| \big) \Big). By the triangle inequality, \sum_i \big( \|z_i - f_i(x')\| - \|z_i - f_i(x)\| \big) \leq \sum_i \|f_i(x) - f_i(x')\| \leq \Delta_1, hence p_x(z)/p_{x'}(z) \leq e^{\varepsilon} for all z — integrating over S gives the definition (and symmetrically by exchanging x, x'). ∎

Utility, computed. For a counting query (\Delta_1 = 1): \mathbb{P}(\|Z\| \geq t) = e^{-\varepsilon t}. With \varepsilon = \ln 20 \approx 3.0 and b = 1/\varepsilon \approx 0.33: the published answer is shifted by at least 1 unit with probability e^{-\varepsilon} = 5\%, by at least 3 units with probability 20^{-3} \approx 0.01\% — a modest noise at the scale of the single individual one seeks to mask.

Theorem 2.2 — The Gaussian mechanism, analytic form For f with \ell_2 sensitivity \Delta_2, M(x) = f(x) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_k) is (\varepsilon, \delta)-DP as soon as \sigma \;\geq\; \Delta_2\, \frac{\sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon} \qquad (\varepsilon \leq 1). Numerically, for \varepsilon = 1, \delta = 10^{-6}: \sqrt{2\ln(1.25 \times 10^6)} = \sqrt{28.1} \approx 5.3, hence \sigma \approx 5.3\,\Delta_2. Why prefer Gaussian over Laplace in ML: (i) Gaussian noise composes very well (Rényi DP, ch. 3); (ii) it is isotropic and gradient-compatible; (iii) the Gaussian tail is lighter for large k. Proof: computation of the ratio of Gaussian densities bounded by e^{\varepsilon} except on a set of probability \delta — analysis in Dwork & Roth (2014), lemma A.1 (the factor 1.25 is a convenience constant of the upper bound).

Definition 2.3 — The exponential mechanism (McSherry-Talwar, 2007) For a non-numeric response (choosing a median, an item, a model): a score function u(x, h) (to be maximized) with sensitivity \Delta_u = \max_h \max_{x \sim x'} \|u(x, h) - u(x', h)\|, and the output law \mathbb{P}(M(x) = h) \;\propto\; \exp\!\Big( \frac{\varepsilon\, u(x, h)}{2\Delta_u} \Big). Utility guarantee: if h^\* = \arg\max_h u(x, h) and u(x, h^\*) - u(x, h) \geq \mathrm{GAP}, then \mathbb{P}(u(x, M(x)) \leq u(x, h^\*) - \mathrm{GAP}) \leq \|\mathcal H\| \exp(-\varepsilon\, \mathrm{GAP}/2\Delta_u) — the gap to the optimum decreases exponentially with \varepsilon and the gap. This is the mechanism of discrete choices: model selection, private top-k, auction mechanisms.

Example 2.4 — Private median: publishing a robust statistic A “median age” query has unbounded sensitivity (shifting the median by one row can make it jump far if the data are concentrated). The exponential mechanism with u(x, h) = -\\|x - h\\|_0 (minus the number of individuals on the wrong side) publishes an \varepsilon-DP median with controlled utility: this is the canonical example of the structural choice of mechanism according to the query — the privacy engineer spends most of their time here, not on the noise itself.

3. Composition: advanced and Rényi DP

Theorem 3.1 — Basic composition, proved If M_1 is \varepsilon_1-DP and M_2, applied after M_1 and allowed to depend on its output, is \varepsilon_2-DP, then (M_1, M_2) is (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)-DP — adaptive composition accumulates linearly.

Proof. By conditioning then post-processing: for measurable S, \mathbb{P}(M_2(M_1(x), x) \in S_2 \mid M_1(x) = m) \leq e^{\varepsilon_2} \mathbb{P}(M_2(M_1(x'), x') \in S_2 \mid M_1(x') = m) — caution: conditioning only makes sense via the regular version; the clean proof handles densities: p_x(m, s) = p_x(m)\, p_x(s \mid m) \leq e^{\varepsilon_1} p_{x'}(m) \cdot e^{\varepsilon_2} p_{x'}(s \mid m) = e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2} p_{x'}(m, s), then one integrates. Adaptivity breaks nothing because e^{\varepsilon_2} bounds the ratio for every value of the history. ∎

Theorem 3.2 — Advanced composition (Dwork, Rothblum, Vadhan, 2010) The composition of k adaptive (\varepsilon_0, \delta_0)-DP mechanisms is (\varepsilon^\\*, \, k\delta_0 + \delta')-DP for every \delta' \> 0 with \varepsilon^\\* = \sqrt{2 k \ln(1/\delta')}\; \varepsilon_0 \;+\; k\, \varepsilon_0 \big( e^{\varepsilon_0} - 1 \big). In \sqrt k rather than k — the idea (sketch: the proof bounds the deviation of the sum of log-ratios via Azuma, then converts a sub-Gaussian concentration into an (\varepsilon, \delta)-type threshold). Numerical. k = 100 releases, \varepsilon_0 = 0.01, \delta' = 10^{-5}: \sqrt{200 \times 11.5} \times 0.01 = 0.48, second term 100 \times 0.01 \times 0.0101 = 0.010, hence \varepsilon^\\* \approx 0.49 versus \varepsilon = k\varepsilon_0 = 1.0 under basic composition — twice as good; the gain explodes for large k (ch. 4, DP-SGD: k \sim 10^4 steps).

Definition 3.3 — Rényi DP (Mironov, 2017) The Rényi divergence of order \alpha \> 1 between two laws P, Q: D_\alpha(P \\| Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \ln\, \mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\Big[ \Big( \frac{dP}{dQ} \Big)^{\alpha} \Big]. M is (\alpha, \varepsilon)-RDP if D_\alpha(M(x) \\| M(x')) \leq \varepsilon for all neighboring databases. Three computational virtues: (i) Gaussian mechanism: for f with sensitivity \Delta_2, the noise \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) is exactly \big(\alpha, \frac{\alpha \Delta_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)-RDP for every \alpha; (ii) composition = addition at fixed \alpha: k mechanisms (\alpha, \varepsilon_i)-RDP compose into (\alpha, \sum_i \varepsilon_i)-RDP (proof: conditional Hölder inequality applied to the joint density — the global likelihood ratio raised to the exponent \alpha factorizes into a product of individual ratios, each bounded by e^{\varepsilon_i \alpha}, and the expectation of the product is bounded term by term); (iii) conversion: (\alpha, \varepsilon)-RDP \Rightarrow (\varepsilon + \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}, \delta)-DP — one then optimizes \alpha a posteriori.

Example 3.4 — The RDP account, computed end to end 64 successive releases of the same Gaussian counting query, noise \sigma = 8\Delta_2, target \delta = 10^{-6}. (a) Classical route. Each release: \varepsilon_0 = \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta_0)}\,\Delta/\sigma \approx 5.3/8 = 0.66. Advanced composition over k = 64: \sqrt{128 \times 13.8} \times 0.66 + 64 \times 0.66 \times (e^{0.66} - 1) \approx 27.8 + 39.3 \approx \mathbf{67}. Ruinous. (b) RDP route. Each release is (\alpha, \alpha/128)-RDP (\alpha\Delta^2/2\sigma^2 = \alpha/128); the 64 compose into (\alpha, \alpha/2)-RDP. Conversion: \varepsilon(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha}{2} + \frac{13.8}{\alpha - 1}, minimized at \alpha - 1 = \sqrt{2 \times 13.8} \approx 5.3, i.e. \varepsilon^\\* \approx 3.1 + 2.6 = \mathbf{5.7}.
Same budget, same \delta — RDP accounting saves a factor of ~10. This is exactly what the “moments accountants” of DP-SGD do (ch. 4); the later improvement (amplification by subsampling, Wang et al. 2019) adds another significant factor when each step touches only a mini-batch.

The family of generalized DP z-CDP (Bun & Steinke, 2016 — Rényi divergence parameterized by \rho = \varepsilon/\alpha), f-DP (Dong, Roth, Su, 2019 — binary tests, exact frontier): same program, different representations; the practitioner retains: divergences with exact additivity (Rényi) give the tightest composition bounds, and the interface (Opacus, TF Privacy, OpenDP) performs the conversion to (\varepsilon, \delta) for governance.

4. Local versus global, and DP-SGD

Definition 4.1 — Trust model: global (curator) vs local Global (centralized) DP: a trusted curator holds the raw data and randomizes the outputs. Better utility; single target (losing the database = catastrophe). Local DP: each individual randomizes their own data before sending — the server never sees the truth; no third party to trust; price: much more noise (typically an extra error of order 1/\sqrt n). Deployment: telemetry (Apple, Google; cf. Emerging ML module ch. 3 for the link with federated learning).

Proposition 4.2 — Warner’s randomized response (1965), the local prototype Sensitive question “X \in \{0,1\}”; the client answers the truth with probability p, the opposite with 1 - p. The channel is \varepsilon-DP with e^{\varepsilon} = \frac{p}{1-p} \qquad \text{(e.g. } p = 0.75 \Leftrightarrow \varepsilon = \ln 3 \approx 1.1).

Proof. Transition matrix T = \begin{pmatrix} p & 1-p \\ 1-p & p \end{pmatrix}; for any response y, \frac{\mathbb{P}(y \mid X{=}1)}{\mathbb{P}(y \mid X{=}0)} equals p/(1-p) or (1-p)/p depending on y, whose maximum is p/(1-p) = e^\varepsilon. ∎

Estimation and cost, computed. With \pi the true proportion and \hat p_n the proportion of 1 responses: \pi = \frac{\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y] - (1-p)}{2p-1} (debiasing), and the variance of the estimator is inflated by (2p-1)^{-2}: at p = 0.75, a factor of 4 — one needs four times as many users for the same precision as in the clear. RAPPOR (Google Chrome): per-bit randomized response on a Bloom register + cohorts — the same arithmetic, at scale.

Definition 4.3 — DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016), summary At every step: (1) draw a mini-batch B (sampling rate \gamma = \|B\|/n); (2) clip each individual gradient \tilde g_i = g_i / \max(1, \\|g_i\\|_2 / C) — bounds the sensitivity at C; (3) aggregate and add noise: \tilde g = \frac{1}{\|B\|}\Big( \sum_{i \in B} \tilde g_i + \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I) \Big); (4) standard SGD step. The final budget combines RDP composition (ch. 3) and amplification by subsampling — each step sees only a fraction \gamma of the individuals: the RDP bound of a step noised with variance \sigma^2 C^2 is roughly reduced by a factor \gamma^2 (regime \gamma \varepsilon_{\text{step}} \lesssim 1), exact computation in Wang, Balle, Kasiviswanathan (2019). The “moments accountant” of Abadi et al. is the ancestor of this computation; implementations: Opacus (PyTorch), TensorFlow Privacy. Typical settings: \sigma \in [0.5; 1.5], C set to the median of the gradient norms, \varepsilon_{\text{final}} \in [1; 10] for modestly sized models — LLMs with 10¹¹ parameters remain an open frontier (ch. 11).

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from opacus import PrivacyEngine

model = nn.Linear(32, 2)
loader = torch.utils.data.DataLoader(train_ds, batch_size=256)
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.05)

privacy_engine = PrivacyEngine()
model, opt, loader = privacy_engine.make_private_with_epsilon(
    module=model, optimizer=opt, data_loader=loader,
    target_epsilon=3.0,          # targeted (epsilon, delta=1e-5) budget
    target_delta=1e-5, epochs=20,
    max_grad_norm=1.0,           # C: per-example clipping
)
# Ordinary training loop; at each step:
#   grad_i clipped to C, noise N(0, sigma^2 C^2), sigma set by the
#   RDP accountant + amplification by subsampling.
for epoch in range(20):
    for x, y in loader:
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
        loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(privacy_engine.get_epsilon(delta=1e-5))   # epsilon actually spent

What DP-SGD does not solve Utility drops with the dimension of the model (the noise is isotropic while gradients live in privileged directions); tuning C is an art; rare features (a unique user!) weigh heavily after clipping; and DP protects the data, not the outputs (an incorrectly calibrated model can be private and misleading — that is the job of the UQ part).

5. DP in industry and attacks

Example 5.1 — Three real deployments (a) 2020 US Census (Abowd, KDD 2018). All published statistical tables go through the TopDown algorithm (optimization + Gaussian noise, exponential mechanism for the invariants); direct motivation: reconstruction attacks on the 2010 census tabulations — an internal attack exactly reconstructed a substantial fraction of the individual records (on the order of half); per-domain public ε budget, intense public debates with demographers. (b) Apple (2017–). “Learning with Privacy at Scale”: keyboard/emoji/QuickType telemetry in local DP (count-mean sketch + Hadamard); millions of devices, daily ε on the order of 4–8 per domain — an illustration of the “budget” that is spent every day. (c) Google RAPPOR (Erlingsson et al., CCS 2014). First large academic-industry production of local DP; chain: signal (Bloom register) → permanent randomized response → instantaneous RR → cohorts; real discoveries (dominant Chrome settings).

Landmark 5.2 — Two families of attacks that motivate the guarantees Membership inference (Shokri et al., IEEE S&P 2017). The adversary queries the model (confidences) and classifies “was in the train?”; AUC typically well above 0.5 on rare/atypical points — the risk grows with the model’s overconfidence: calibration (ch. 7) is also a defense. Unintended memorization (Carlini et al., 2019 — “The Secret Sharer”). Insert a canary (“secret key”) into the train of a generator; exposure measured by the relative perplexity of the canary: LLMs memorize and exfiltrate verbatim sequences seen only once — a founding result for LLM privacy (ch. 11); follow-up: training extraction on GPT-2 by the same group (2021). DP is the defense with a guarantee: an \varepsilon-DP mechanism bounds the membership likelihood ratio at e^{\varepsilon} (hence the AUC of any adversary); the private trained model remains useful (ch. 4) provided one pays in accuracy.

Regulation GDPR (anonymization “reasonably impossible to re-identify”): DP is the most accepted formal reading of this criterion; sectoral frameworks (health, banking) and emerging standards (NIST, ISO/IEC 27559). For the engineer: keep a budget ledger (which query, which ε spent, which composition) as one keeps an incident log — the frameworks (OpenDP, Google DP library) enforce this discipline.

6. Uncertainty: sources and vocabulary

Definition 6.1 — Aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty Aleatoric (data): the irreducible noise of generation — two patients identical on observed features, different outcomes; one can only measure it. Epistemic (model): what the model ignores because it has not learned enough in that region — reducible by data; one can reduce it. In heteroscedastic regression: y = f(x) + \sigma(x)\varepsilon: \sigma(x) = aleatoric; the variance between models trained on resamples = epistemic (ch. 8 will make it operational via ensembles). Distribution shift: a third source — the input leaves the training support; the model does not know that it does not know (ch. 9 provides a safety net; cf. Machine Learning module ch. 9 for data drift in production).

Definition 6.2 — The frequentist contract of calibration A probabilistic predictor \hat p(y \mid x) is calibrated if among all the times it announces 80%, the truth occurs ~80% of the time: \mathbb{P}\big(y = 1 \mid \hat p(x) = c\big) = c \qquad \text{for (almost) all } c \in [0, 1]. Calibrated ≠ accurate: always predicting the prevalence 0.3 is perfectly calibrated and useless; calibrated ≠ certain. Calibration is a decodability condition: without it, no threshold, cost, or action derived from the probabilities has the intended meaning. The vocabulary of guarantees extends to sets: marginal coverage (ch. 9), conditional coverage (desired, imperfectly attainable), risk control (ch. 12).

7. Calibration: diagrams, ECE, Brier

Definition 7.1 — Reliability diagram, bins, ECE Partition [0, 1] into B bins; bin b = predictions with mean confidence \mathrm{conf}_b, observed accuracy \mathrm{acc}_b, count n_b. The reliability diagram plots \mathrm{acc}_b against \mathrm{conf}_b (the diagonal = perfect calibration); the expected calibration error: \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{n} \big\| \mathrm{acc}_b - \mathrm{conf}_b \big\|. Precautions on binning: the partition influences the measure (10 bins is the de facto standard); kernel / isotonic-regression variants preferable in research (Naeini et al. 2015; see also the Machine Learning module ch. 9 for evaluation curves).

Example 7.2 — ECE computed by hand 100 binary predictions, 5 bins (mean confidences are given):

This is the signature measured by Guo et al. (2017) on modern networks: more depth/capacity ⇒ more overconfidence, not less.

Definition 7.3 — Brier score and its decomposition (Brier 1950; Murphy 1973) \mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat p_i - y_i)^2, \qquad \text{decomposition:} \quad \mathrm{BS} = \underbrace{\text{reliability}}_{\text{calibration}} - \underbrace{\text{resolution}}_{\text{discrimination}} + \underbrace{\text{uncertainty}}_{\text{irreducible}}. The reliability term = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}(\mathrm{conf}_b - \mathrm{acc}_b)^2 (the squared, weighted ECE); resolution measures the deviation of the \mathrm{acc}_b from the prevalence; uncertainty = \bar y (1 - \bar y) does not depend on the model. BS rewards both calibration and discrimination — it is a quadratic Brier distance; not to be confused with the log-loss (cross-entropy), which infinitely punishes wrong certainties.

Example 7.4 — Brier computed, and the mirage of confidence Four predictions: (0.9; y{=}1), (0.9; y{=}1), (0.9; y{=}0), (0.3; y{=}1): \mathrm{BS} = \frac{0.1^2 + 0.1^2 + 0.9^2 + 0.7^2}{4} = \frac{0.01 + 0.01 + 0.81 + 0.49}{4} = 0.33. The constant predictor equal to the prevalence 3/4 = 0.75 achieves \mathrm{BS} = 0.75 \times 0.25 = 0.1875 \< 0.33: without information, it is better to state the base rate than to assert 90% — exactly the problem of overenthusiastic models.

Procedure 7.5 — Temperature scaling (Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger, 2017) A single scalar T \> 0 recalibrates the logits: \hat p_T(y \mid x) = \mathrm{softmax}(z/T); T fitted by NLL on a validation set (a 1D optimization — which does not change the argmax, hence not the accuracy). Computed. Two classes, logits z = (4; 0): \mathrm{softmax}(z) = (0.982; 0.018); T = 2: \mathrm{softmax}(2, 0) = (0.881; 0.119); T = 4: \mathrm{softmax}(1, 0) = (0.731; 0.269). If the true frequency of class 1 in this neighborhood is ~75%, T \approx 4 is the right calibration — the ranking of the predictions is intact, only the confidence scale is corrected. Limits: T is global, calibrated on a fixed distribution; local recalibration: isotonic, Platt per bin, or conformalized calibration (ch. 9-10).

import numpy as np
def ece(conf, y, bins=15):
    edges = np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1); idx = np.digitize(conf, edges[1:-1])
    n = len(conf); total = 0.0
    for b in range(bins):
        m = idx == b
        if m.sum() == 0: continue
        total += (m.sum()/n) * abs(y[m].mean() - conf[m].mean())
    return total

def fit_temperature(logits, y, grid=np.linspace(0.5, 10, 200)):
    # NLL on validation, one dimension, non-parametric and sufficient for labs
    best_T, best = 1.0, np.inf
    for T in grid:
        z = logits / T
        p = np.exp(z - z.max(1, keepdims=True))
        p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        nll = -np.log(np.clip(p[np.arange(len(y)), y], 1e-12, None)).mean()
        if nll < best: best, best_T = nll, T
    return best_T

8. Bayesian deep learning: MC dropout versus ensembles

Definition 8.1 — The Bayesian framework for networks A prior p(w) on the weights, a likelihood p(y \mid x, w); the predictive integrates epistemic uncertainty: p(y \mid x, \mathcal D) = \int p(y \mid x, w)\, p(w \mid \mathcal D)\, dw. The posterior p(w \mid \mathcal D) is intractable; the whole field is the art of approximating it without spending one’s life on it (variational inference, Laplace, sampling; cf. Refresher Statistics module ch. 8 for the Bayesian foundation).

Result 8.2 — MC dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) Train with dropout (Deep Learning module ch. 3) then, at inference, keep dropout active and average T forward passes: \hat p(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} p(y \mid x, \hat w_t), \qquad \hat w_t \sim \text{dropout masks}. Gal & Ghahramani show that this procedure approximates a Gaussian variational inference: dropout is a Bernoulli prior on the weights, and the dropout loss minimizes a KL bound toward the true posterior (spirit: like the substitution theorem of the bootstrap in Statistics in Action ch. 7 — an asymptotic justification for a massive procedure). Cost: T forwards per example (typically T \in [10; 100]), zero retraining. The variance between passes measures the epistemic part.

Result 8.3 — Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, Blundell, 2017) Train M independent networks (different initializations; option: adversarial training) and aggregate: average of the probabilities (classification), mixture of the predicted Gaussians (regression — the aggregated variance includes the dispersion of the means). Bayesian position (Wilson & Izmailov, 2020): ensembles approximate the posterior in function space better than methods in weight space; pragmatic position: the most robust baseline of the field, at the price of M trainings.

Example 8.4 — Compared on the dimensions that matter

Criterion MC dropout Deep ensembles

Inference cost T forwards M forwards
Epistemic out of distribution correct, sometimes underestimated good (diversity of minima)

The famous “deep ensembles work better than one would expect from a Bayesian perspective” (a position defended by Lakshminarayanan et al.) and the counterpoint “ensembles ARE approximate Bayes model averaging” (Wilson & Izmailov): the disagreement is real, practice decides: with a free training compute budget, ensembles; otherwise MC dropout; and in all cases, evaluate the calibration (ch. 7) — not just the RMSE.

Usage warning These methods give uncertainties without guarantee: they can be silently overconfident out of distribution. Ch. 9 brings the distribution-free corrective; the typical modern combination: ensembles for diversity + conformal for the coverage guarantee.

9. Conformal prediction: theorem and algorithm

Question: can we build prediction sets with coverage guaranteed without any distributional assumption? Answer (Vovk): yes, provided one has exchangeable data and accepts marginal coverage. This is the most useful result of the UQ part.

Definition 9.1 — Non-conformity scores and exchangeability Any score s(x, y) (small = conforming): for classification, s = 1 - \hat p(y \mid x) (softmax of the true class); for regression, s = \|y - \hat f(x)\|. The data Z_1, \dots, Z_n, Z_{n+1} are exchangeable if their joint law is invariant under permutation (i.i.d. ⇒ exchangeable; cf. Statistics in Action ch. 11 for the de Finetti connection — exchangeability, not i.i.d., is the true foundation). Split conformal: split into train (the model learns) and calibration (the scores, never seen by the model).

Theorem 9.2 — Exact marginal coverage (proved) Let s_1, \dots, s_n be the calibration scores and s_{\text{new}} the score of a new pair, exchangeable at (n+1) with continuous values. For \alpha \in (0, 1), set k = \lceil (n+1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil and let \hat q be the k-th smallest calibration score. Then \mathbb{P}\big( s_{\text{new}} \leq \hat q \big) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha \qquad \text{(exactly } k/(n+1) \text{ for continuous scores).}

Proof. By exchangeability, the rank of s_{\text{new}} in the multiset \{s_1, \dots, s_n, s_{\text{new}}\} is uniform over \{1, \dots, n+1\}. Justification: for every permutation \sigma of \{1, \dots, n+1\}, the law of (Z_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, Z_{\sigma(n+1)}) is that of (Z_1, \dots, Z_{n+1}); the event “the rank of the last coordinate equals r” is the image by permutation of “the rank equals r'” for all r, r' — same probability, and the sum of the n+1 probabilities equals 1: each equals 1/(n+1). With continuous scores, ties are almost surely absent, and the event \{s_{\text{new}} \leq \hat q\} is exactly \{\text{rank} \leq k\}, of probability k/(n+1) \geq 1 - \alpha by definition of k. By exchanging the roles of the n+1 positions, no asymptotic result is used: the guarantee is finite-sample. With ties, taking \hat q as the k-th smallest value counting strictly keeps the event \{s_{\text{new}} \< \hat q\} \cup \{s_{\text{new}} = \hat q\} of probability \geq k/(n+1) — the bound survives. ∎

Example 9.3 — Split conformal, algorithm and worked example Algorithm. (1) train \hat f on the train set; (2) compute the scores s_i on the n calibration points; (3) k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil, \hat q = k-th smallest score; (4) predict, for a new x, the set C(x) = \{y : s(x, y) \leq \hat q\} — in regression: [\hat f(x) - \hat q, \hat f(x) + \hat q]. Example (regression). n = 9, \alpha = 0.1 ⇒ k = \lceil 10 \times 0.9 \rceil = 9. Absolute calibration residuals: 0.02 · 0.05 · 0.08 · 0.11 · 0.14 · 0.19 · 0.25 · 0.34 · 0.51 — the 9th (largest) is \hat q = 0.51; the prediction for any new x is \hat f(x) \pm 0.51, with \mathbb{P}(\text{cover}) \geq 90\% — exactly 9/10 = 90\% if the future residuals resemble the calibration ones. Classification: same steps with s = 1 - \hat p(\text{candidate predicted class}); the set C(x) contains the classes with sufficient softmax; typical size 1 where the model is confident, 2-3 at the boundaries — the map of set sizes is a free ambiguity detector. Effect of n: the empirical (1-\alpha)-quantile with finite-sample correction \lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil — the reason for the +1: without it, \alpha \> 1/(n+1) would be impossible (e.g. \alpha = 0.1, n = 5: one needs k = \lceil 5.4 \rceil = 6 \> n… in that case the set is all of \mathbb R: the algorithm honestly admits its ignorance).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

n_cal, alpha = 3000, 0.1
Xtr, Xcal, Xte = ...           # exchangeable split
model.fit(Xtr, ytr)            # never the calibration set

What the theorem says — and does not say Coverage is marginal: on average over the draws of the calibration set and of new pairs. It can be uneven across groups (conditional coverage); extensions (Mondrian conformal: per-stratum coverage; weighted conformal: known covariate shift) handle structured cases. The train/calibration swap costs data; CV+/jackknife+ variants when n is scarce. Internal link: the Statistics in Action module ch. 7 applies the same “empirical quantile + finite-sample correction” logic to bootstrap intervals.

10. Quantile and conformalized regression

Definition 10.1 — Distributional quantile, quantile regression, pinball loss (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) The quantile q_\tau(x) of Y \mid X = x satisfies \mathbb P(Y \leq q_\tau(x) \mid x) = \tau. One estimates it by minimizing the pinball loss: \ell_\tau(y, \hat q) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - \hat q) & y \geq \hat q \\ (1 - \tau)\,(\hat q - y) & y \< \hat q \end{cases} \qquad \text{(subgradients } \in [\tau - 1, \tau]\text{)}. This is a convex problem (a linear program for the linear model; gradient descent for networks); training two heads \hat q_{\text{lo}} = \hat q_{\alpha/2}, \hat q_{\text{hi}} = \hat q_{1 - \alpha/2} gives an adaptive prediction band — wide where the data are dispersed (heteroscedastic aleatoric, ch. 6). Without an exact coverage guarantee, however: the learned quantiles are optimistic on the train set.

Theorem 10.2 — Conformalized Quantile Regression (Romano, Patterson, Candès, 2019) Non-conformity score on calibration: s_i = \max\big( \hat q_{\text{lo}}(x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x_i) \big). Taking \hat q = the \lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil-th smallest score, the band C(x) = \big[ \hat q_{\text{lo}}(x) - \hat q,\; \hat q_{\text{hi}}(x) + \hat q \big] has coverage \geq 1 - \alpha in finite samples (thm 9.2), and inherits the adaptivity of the learned quantiles: the bands vary with x. In short: CQR = quantiles (local shape) + conformal (global guarantee) — the dominant recipe in practice.

Example 10.3 — CQR on a synthetic case, verified y = 2x + \sin(2x)\, (1 + 0.5x)\,\varepsilon (increasing variance). In the lab: (1) a two-head pinball network (\tau \in \{0.05, 0.95\}); (2) 1000 calibration points, scores s_i; (3) \hat q \approx corrected empirical quantile; (4) over 100 repetitions, mean coverage 89.8% (target 90%), bands ~3× narrower than the constant-residual split conformal in calm regions — the “hourglass” plot of the bands flaring at large x is the visual deliverable of the lab (exercise 7).

11. UQ for LLMs

LLMs add three difficulties to the calibration problem: the output space is combinatorial (sequences, not classes); RLHF training seems to degrade verbal calibration; and generative use (a written answer) does not naturally expose token probabilities. The M2 module “Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs” will complete this part (security, jailbreaks, training extraction); cf. also the Large Language Models module, ch. 9.

Definition 11.1 — Verbalized confidence Ask the model for its confidence in words: “What’s the probability that your answer is correct? Answer with a number in [0, 100]” (Lin, Hilton, Evans, 2022). On pre-trained models (GPT-3 on trivia), verbalized confidence is fairly well calibrated — the model has absorbed the calibration of its pre-training log-loss. But: Tian et al. (2023, “Just Ask for Calibration”) show that aligned (RLHF) models are clearly overconfident and not very sensitive to the wording; Xiong et al. (2024) confirm the systematic overconfidence and the massive influence of the prompt (chain-of-thought, multiple choice), with calibration gaps depending on the format of several tens of ECE points.

Definition 11.2 — Three native sources of generative uncertainty (1) Token logprobs: entropy of the distribution of the next token, or sequence probability (product); biased (length, temperature); local calibration per bin (as in ch. 7) on a labeled set. (2) Self-consistency: sample K trajectories (temperature > 0) and measure agreement: the frequency of the majority answer is a proxy for confidence (direct link with the ensemble diversity of ch. 8 — prediction averaging vs variance); Kuhn, Gal, Farquhar (2023) refine this into semantic uncertainty: cluster the semantically equivalent responses (mutual implications predicted by an NLI) and compute the entropy over the clusters — because “Paris”, “paris”, and “the capital” are the same answer for the user; separates semantic uncertainty (is the answer determined?) from lexical uncertainty. (3) Generative conformal: apply ch. 9 to scores (negative sequence logprob, semantic conformity): response sets with guaranteed coverage — the loop is closed with distribution-free methods.

The privacy wall on the LLM side LLMs memorize (ch. 5, Secret Sharer); DP-SGD at the scale of 10¹¹ parameters still costs too much utility for general-purpose pre-training; current industrial leads: DP-SGD on the alignment phases (the most sensitive data), private RAG (the data stay server-side with per-query budgets), federated learning + DP (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 3), and the systematic evaluation of extraction (canaries). An open topic — precisely the crossroads targeted by this module.

12. Applications: deciding under uncertainty

Example 12.1 — Medicine: calibration as a condition of care A risk model announcing “30% complications” triggers a costly protocol: if the 30% announcers actually operate at 45% (underconfidence), we overtreat; at 15% (overconfidence), we undertreat — both kill. Van Calster et al. (2019) document the chronic miscalibration of published clinical models and call it the “Achilles heel of predictive analytics”; remedy: mandatory reliability diagrams (ch. 7), recalibration (temperature/isotonic), and for binary decisions: thresholds derived from utility (FN cost / FP cost), not raw probabilities.

Definition 12.2 — Expected-utility decision, abstention, risk control With costs c_{\text{FP}}, c_{\text{FN}}, the optimal classifier will predict 1 only if \hat p \geq c_{\text{FP}} / (c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}) — an exact formula if \hat p is calibrated (ch. 6); hence the sequence of the module: calibrate (7), aggregate the sources (8-10), then decide. Selective abstention: the model may answer “I don’t know” when the conformal set has > k elements or the CQR band is wider than a business threshold (delegation to a human); the risk-coverage curve is plotted in the lab. Risk control (Angelopoulos, Bates, Candès, Jordan, Lei, 2021): choose the threshold itself on a calibration set to control an expectation of arbitrary risk (error rate > budget; false negatives < budget); it is the same empirical-quantile equation as ch. 9, applied to a decision threshold — the final building block of the “guarantee” chain.
