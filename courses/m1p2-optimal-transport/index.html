0. Review: convexity and LP duality

L3 background used

  • Convex sets and functions: for convex f, any stationary point is a global minimum; for strictly convex f, the minimizer (if it exists) is unique — entropic OT in ch. 3 takes advantage of this;
  • Linear programming: minimize c^\top x subject to Ax = b, x \geq 0;
  • Measure theory: probability, expectation \mathbb{E}_\mu[f] = \int f\,d\mu, pushforward measure;
  • Entropy: Kullback-Leibler divergence, Jensen’s inequality — the engine of entropic regularization.

Theorem 0.1 — LP duality: weak (proved), strong, complementarity (proved) Primal: \min\{c^\top x \ : \ Ax = b,\ x \geq 0\}; dual: \max\{b^\top y \ : \ A^\top y \leq c\}.

  • Weak duality: for any pair of feasible points, c^\top x \geq b^\top y.
  • Strong duality: if the primal is feasible and bounded, the dual attains its optimum and the values coincide.
  • Complementarity: at the optima x^\star, y^\star: x^\star_j > 0 \Rightarrow (A^\top y^\star)_j = c_j.

Proof. Weak: c^\top x \geq (A^\top y)^\top x = y^\top (Ax) = y^\top b (summing the dual constraints weighted by x_j \geq 0). Strong: admitted (polyhedron separation). Complementarity: at the optimum, 0 = c^\top x^\star - b^\top y^\star = (c - A^\top y^\star)^\top x^\star = \sum_j \underbrace{(c - A^\top y^\star)_j}_{\geq 0}\, \underbrace{x^\star_j}_{\geq 0}: every term is zero, hence the implication.∎

Proposition 0.2 — Pushforward measure and transfer lemma (proved) For measurable T : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d, the pushforward measure is T_\#\mu(A) = \mu\big(T^{-1}(A)\big) (“the mass of what is sent into A”). Transfer lemma: for every positive or bounded measurable f, \int f\,d(T_\#\mu) = \int f \circ T\,d\mu.

Proof. For f = \mathbf{1}_A this is the definition. Both sides are linear in f: the equality passes to positive simple functions, then to all positive functions by monotone convergence, then to bounded ones by taking differences. This “moving the change of variables into the measure” is used on every page of this course.∎

Proposition 0.3 — Strict convexity: uniqueness (proved) If a strictly convex F attains its minimum at x^\star, then x^\star is the unique minimizer.

Proof. If x \neq x^\star were also a minimizer, the midpoint m = \tfrac12(x + x^\star) would give F(m) < \tfrac12F(x) + \tfrac12F(x^\star) = \min F — a contradiction.∎

Proposition 0.4 — KL ≥ 0 (proved by Gibbs/Jensen), with equality case For discrete distributions p, q (or densities): \mathrm{KL}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log\frac{p_i}{q_i} \ \geq\ 0, \qquad \text{equality} \iff p = q.

Proof. \mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = -\sum_i p_i \log\frac{q_i}{p_i}. The \log function is concave: by Jensen weighted by p (Gibbs’ inequality), \sum_i p_i \log\frac{q_i}{p_i} \leq \log\sum_i p_i\frac{q_i}{p_i} = \log\sum_i q_i = 0. Equality in Jensen (strict concavity of \log) requires q_i/p_i constant on \{p_i > 0\}, and the constant equals 1 because both are probabilities.∎

1. The transport problem

Concrete scene: two 1D histograms (source / target), 20 bins. You code a greedy “nearest bin” coupling: cost 12.4. The LP solver (POT) gives 7.1 — and on a 1→2 mass case, your deterministic map fails while a Kantorovich plan exists. This chapter states Monge vs Kantorovich before Sinkhorn.

What breaks Demanding a Monge map when mass must split; dropping marginal constraints (it is no longer transport); over-regularizing (plan too blurry, metric useless). All three sabotage domain adaptation as much as a 3-point toy.

Definition 1.1 — Monge’s problem (1781) Given two probability measures \mu, \nu on \mathbb{R}^d and a cost c(x, y) (typically \|x - y\|^2), find a transport map T : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d that moves \mu onto \nu (T_\#\mu = \nu) while minimizing the total cost: \min_T \ \int c\big(x, T(x)\big)\, d\mu(x). Historical note: cut and fill (Monge, 1781); the problem is non-convex in T, and a solution may not exist.

Example 1.2 — Non-existence of a Monge map: 1 to 2 (detailed) Take \mu = \delta_0 (unit mass at the origin) and \nu = \tfrac12\delta_{-1} + \tfrac12\delta_{+1} (two half-masses). A map T sends 0 to a single point T(0), so T_\#\delta_0 = \delta_{T(0)}: the pushforward measure is atomic at a single point — it is impossible to obtain \nu, which has two. The mass must be “split”: the random pair (X, Y) with X = 0 and Y = \pm1 by a coin flip realizes the transport, but a map does not. This is exactly what Kantorovich formalizes: allowing splitting.

Kantorovich’s formulation (1942) — the pivot of the course Relax “each source goes to a single destination” into a transport plan \gamma — the law of a pair (X, Y) with marginals \mu, \nu: W_c(\mu, \nu) \;=\; \min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \ \int c(x, y)\, d\gamma(x, y), \Pi(\mu,\nu) = \big\{\gamma \geq 0 \ :\ \gamma(\cdot \times \mathbb{R}^d) = \mu,\ \gamma(\mathbb{R}^d \times \cdot) = \nu\big\}. Discrete case (\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}, \nu = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}, \mathbf{1}^\top a = \mathbf{1}^\top b = 1): a linear program on the transport matrix \Gamma: \min_{\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m}} \ \langle C, \Gamma\rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \Gamma \mathbf{1} = a,\ \Gamma^\top \mathbf{1} = b, where C_{ij} = c(x_i, y_j).

Theorem 1.3 — Existence (by compactness) and structure of solutions

  • \Pi(\mu,\nu) is a non-empty convex compact (discrete: a bounded polytope defined by closed constraints; general: compactness in the sense of narrow convergence, by tightness). The cost being continuous, the minimum is attained (Weierstrass).
  • Extreme solutions: there exists an optimal solution that is a vertex of the polytope, and such a vertex has at most n + m - 1 non-zero coefficients. Proof (sketch): the n + m equality constraints are dependent (the sum of the row constraints equals that of the columns: rank \leq n + m - 1); a vertex of a polyhedron in dimension nm is defined by nm independent active constraints, of which at least nm - (n+m-1) are bounds \Gamma_{ij} = 0.

Why the relaxation is “lossless” Example 1.2 shows it is sometimes necessary (mass splitting). It is also sufficient in the sense that, as soon as \mu has a density with respect to Lebesgue (the case of all data clouds), the optimal Kantorovich plan is induced by a map T: \gamma^\star = (\mathrm{Id}, T)_\#\mu — this is Brenier’s theorem, proved in ch. 2. In the discrete case, on the other hand (n sources, m targets), the vertices with \leq n+m-1 non-zeros are “almost” deterministic: each row carries at most… we return to this (exercise 5). Logistic interpretation: mines and coke ovens (Hitchcock, Kantorovich) — nowadays, comparing two data distributions.

2. Duality and the Wasserstein distance

Theorem 2.1 — Kantorovich duality (complete proof in the discrete case) W_c(\mu, \nu) \;=\; \max_{(f, g) \in \Phi_c} \ \int f\, d\mu + \int g\, d\nu, \qquad \Phi_c = \big\{(f, g) \ :\ f(x) + g(y) \leq c(x, y)\ \forall (x,y)\big\}. In the discrete case: \max_{f,g} \langle a, f\rangle + \langle b, g\rangle s.t. f_i + g_j \leq C_{ij}.

Proof. Step 1 (the discrete problem is an LP). The constraints \Gamma \geq 0, \Gamma\mathbf{1} = a, \Gamma^\top\mathbf{1} = b are affine; the primal is feasible (e.g. \Gamma = ab^\top) and bounded (\langle C,\Gamma\rangle \geq 0). Step 2 (the dual). Write the dual of th. 0.1 by associating a multiplier f_i with the row constraint \sum_j \Gamma_{ij} = a_i and g_j with the column constraint \sum_i \Gamma_{ij} = b_j: the dual of \min_{\Gamma \geq 0}\{\langle C, \Gamma\rangle\} under these equalities is \max_{f,g}\{\langle a, f\rangle + \langle b, g\rangle\ :\ f_i + g_j \leq C_{ij}\ \forall i,j\} — each variable \Gamma_{ij} \geq 0 yields one dual inequality constraint. Step 3 (strong duality). The primal is feasible and bounded ⇒ strong duality (th. 0.1): the optima exist and coincide. Step 4 (reading the complementarity). At the optimal triple (\Gamma^\star, f^\star, g^\star): \Gamma^\star_{ij} > 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ f^\star_i + g^\star_j = C_{ij}, i.e. mass travels only on saturated routes — it is this condition that ties the plan to the potentials (corollary 2.3).∎

Definition 2.2 — c-transform and Kantorovich potentials For a potential f: f^c(y) = \inf_x\, \big[c(x,y) - f(x)\big] (c-transform); f is c-concave if f = (f^c)^{\bar c} (bitransform). At the optima of theorem 2.1, one may always assume g^\star = (f^\star)^c and f^\star = (g^\star)^{\bar c}: replacing g by f^c \geq g can only increase the dual objective while remaining admissible. The optimal pair then reduces to one c-concave potential f^\star (and its dual).

Corollary 2.3 — Brenier’s theorem (precise statement) and the quadratic case If \mu \ll \mathrm{Leb} and c(x,y) = \tfrac12\|x-y\|^2:

  • the optimal plan is unique and induced by a map: \gamma^\star = (\mathrm{Id}, T)_\#\mu;
  • T = \nabla\varphi, the gradient of a convex function \varphi; conversely, every admissible map of this form is optimal (cyclical monotonicity);
  • link with the dual: \varphi(x) = \tfrac12\|x\|^2 - f^\star(x), i.e. \ T(x) = x - \nabla f^\star(x) on the transported support.

Reading: optimal transport is a gradient — it deforms space continuously, without tearing; in 1D, T = F_\nu^{-1} \circ F_\mu (monotone rearrangement, prop. 2.6). For two Gaussians, T is affine (th. 2.7). In ML: T = the “mapping” we learn to adapt a domain (ch. 4) — cf. the Deep Learning module for the networks that approximate it.

Definition 2.4 — Wasserstein distance of order p With c = d^p on a metric space (E, d): W_p(\mu, \nu) = \Big( \min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int d(x,y)^p\, d\gamma \Big)^{1/p} on the set of probabilities with finite moment of order p.

Theorem 2.5 — W_p is a distance (proved: separation and triangle inequality by gluing)

Proof. Symmetry: if \gamma is admissible for (\mu,\nu), its transpose is admissible for (\nu,\mu), at the same cost. Separation: W_p(\mu,\nu) = 0 ⇒ there exists an optimal \gamma^\star with zero cost, supported on \{x = y\} (since d^p > 0 off the diagonal); its marginals are then equal: \mu = \nu. Triangle inequality (discrete): let \gamma^{12} be optimal between \mu^1, \mu^2 and \gamma^{23} optimal between \mu^2, \mu^3. Gluing. Set \pi_{i\ell} \;=\; \sum_j\ \frac{\gamma^{12}_{ij}\,\gamma^{23}_{j\ell}}{\mu^2_j}, the law of the pair (X, Z) where we first draw Y \sim \mu^2, then X \sim \gamma^{12}(\cdot\|Y) and Z \sim \gamma^{23}(Y, \cdot). Marginals: \sum_\ell \pi_{i\ell} = \sum_j \gamma^{12}_{ij}\frac{\sum_\ell\gamma^{23}_{j\ell}}{\mu^2_j} = \sum_j \gamma^{12}_{ij} = \mu^1_i, and likewise \sum_i \pi_{i\ell} = \mu^3_\ell. So \pi is admissible for (\mu^1, \mu^3), and by conditional Minkowski (the inequality \big(\mathbb{E}[d(X,Z)^p]\big)^{1/p} \leq \big(\mathbb{E}[d(X,Y)^p]\big)^{1/p} + \big(\mathbb{E}[d(Y,Z)^p]\big)^{1/p} is proved by applying Minkowski conditionally on Y, then integrating): W_p(\mu^1,\mu^3) \leq \Big(\sum_{i\ell} \pi_{i\ell}\, d(x_i, z_\ell)^p\Big)^{1/p} \leq W_p(\mu^1,\mu^2) + W_p(\mu^2,\mu^3).\qquad \square∎

Narrow topology: what W_p “sees” On probabilities with finite moment of order p, W_p(\mu_k \to \mu) ⇔ narrow convergence (against every bounded continuous test function) + convergence of the moments of order p. Comparison with the usual divergences: \mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu) = \infty as soon as the supports move apart, whereas W_p stays finite and continuous — two clouds shifted by \varepsilon are at distance \varepsilon (not “infinity”). It is this “flat” geometry (no support jump) that makes W usable in ML: informative gradients, interpolation \mu_t = ((1-t)\,\mathrm{Id} + tT)_\#\mu in the physical sense of displacement.

Proposition 2.6 — W_p in dimension 1: coupling of quantiles (proved in the discrete case) W_p(\mu, \nu) = \Big(\int_0^1 \big\|F_\mu^{-1}(u) - F_\nu^{-1}(u)\big\|^p\, du\Big)^{1/p}, where F^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x \ : \ F(x) \geq u\} is the quantile. The optimal plan matches the u-quantile of \mu with the u-quantile of \nu; computable by sorting in \mathcal{O}(n\log n).

Proof. Discrete case, n equal masses. Step 1 (uncrossing). Consider a plan putting mass on (x_1, y_2) and (x_2, y_1) with x_1 \leq x_2 and y_1 \leq y_2 (“crossed routes”); move this mass to (x_1, y_1) and (x_2, y_2): the marginals are preserved and the cost does not increase, since \|x_1 - y_2\|^p + \|x_2 - y_1\|^p \ \geq\ \|x_1 - y_1\|^p + \|x_2 - y_2\|^p — setting a = x_1 - y_1 \leq b = x_2 - y_2 and d = y_2 - y_1 \geq 0, the right-hand side reads \|a\|^p + \|b\|^p, the left one \|a - d\|^p + \|b + d\|^p: moving two real numbers further apart when one is already the smaller one can only increase the sum of the p-th powers (convexity of \|{\cdot}\|^p: the function t \mapsto \|t-d\|^p + \|t+d\|^p… one checks that the derivative vanishes at the midpoint and that the expression is minimal when the two quantities are “brought closer together”): a convex rearrangement inequality. Step 2. By iterating the uncrossing, an optimal plan exists that crosses no pair: it is the monotone coupling x_i \leftrightarrow y_{\sigma(i)} with increasing \sigma — the quantile matching. Step 3 (general case, statement). For arbitrary laws, the plan (F_\mu^{-1}(U), F_\nu^{-1}(U)) with U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1] has the right marginals and is monotone, hence optimal (1D Brenier).∎

Theorem 2.7 — W_2 between Gaussians: the Bures metric (proved) W_2^2\big(\mathcal{N}(m_1,\Sigma_1), \mathcal{N}(m_2,\Sigma_2)\big) = \|m_1 - m_2\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\Big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2\big(\Sigma_1^{1/2}\,\Sigma_2\,\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big).

Proof. Step 1 (centering). For every coupling (X, Y), \mathbb{E}\|X - Y\|^2 = \|m_1 - m_2\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\tilde X - \tilde Y\|^2 with \tilde X, \tilde Y centered (expand the square, the cross terms cancel): means and covariances transport separately, and the plan (m_1 + \tilde X,\ m_2 + \tilde Y) attains the bound. Assume m_1 = m_2 = 0. Step 2 (an affine candidate). Set M = \Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2\Sigma_1^{1/2} and A \;=\; \Sigma_1^{-1/2}\, M^{1/2}\, \Sigma_1^{-1/2}. A is symmetric positive definite (a symmetric conjugate of M^{1/2}). The coupling (X, Y = AX) with X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_1) is admissible: Y is a centered Gaussian with covariance A\Sigma_1A^\top = \Sigma_1^{-1/2}M\Sigma_1^{-1/2} = \Sigma_2. Step 3 (cost of the candidate). \mathbb{E}\|X - AX\|^2 = \mathrm{tr}\big((I - A)\Sigma_1(I - A)^\top\big) = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(A\Sigma_1) + \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_2). Now \mathrm{tr}(A\Sigma_1) = \mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma_1^{-1/2}M^{1/2}\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big(M^{1/2}\big) (cyclicity): cost = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(M^{1/2}) — the announced formula. Step 4 (optimality via Brenier). T(x) = Ax = \nabla\big(\tfrac12 x^\top A x\big) is the gradient of a convex function (A symmetric ≥ 0) that sends \mu onto \nu; by the converse of corollary 2.3 (sufficiency of cyclical monotonicity), this coupling is optimal. In 1D, A = \sigma_2/\sigma_1: cost (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2, consistent with prop. 2.6.∎

Corollary 2.8 — Wasserstein barycenter of 1D Gaussians (proved) The barycenter \displaystyle\arg\min_\nu \sum_k w_k\, W_2^2(\nu, \mu_k) of \mu_k = \mathcal{N}(m_k, \sigma_k^2), \sum w_k = 1, is the Gaussian \mathcal{N}\Big(\sum_k w_k m_k,\ \ \big(\sum_k w_k \sigma_k\big)^{2}\Big).

Proof. By prop. 2.6, minimizing over \nu amounts to minimizing \sum_k w_k \int_0^1 \|F_\nu^{-1}(u) - F_k^{-1}(u)\|^2du in the quantile function F_\nu^{-1}. Pointwise in u, the optimization is a simple regression: the optimum is F_\nu^{-1}(u) = \sum_k w_k F_k^{-1}(u) (zero derivative — strictly convex function). Now F_k^{-1}(u) = m_k + \sigma_k\,\Phi^{-1}(u): F_\nu^{-1}(u) = \underbrace{\sum_k w_k m_k}_{m} + \underbrace{\sum_k w_k\sigma_k}_{\sigma}\,\Phi^{-1}(u), which is exactly the quantile of \mathcal{N}(m, \sigma^2). Note that the “barycenter variance” \sigma^2 = (\sum w_k\sigma_k)^2 is the square of the mean of the standard deviations — not the mean of the variances.∎

Bridge with the Signal Processing module The Bures formula of theorem 2.7 is exactly the Fréchet distance (FID) used to evaluate image generators and audio models: cf. the Signal Processing module M1P1, chapter 8, which prepares this reading — and whose “spectral loss” is a Gaussian W_2 loss on spectrograms. The same mathematical object runs through both modules.

3. Entropic regularization: Sinkhorn

Definition 3.1 — Entropic OT (Cuturi, 2013) W_\varepsilon(a, b) \;=\; \min_{\Gamma \in U(a, b)}\ \langle C, \Gamma\rangle + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\Gamma\,\big\|\, a b^\top\big), (U(a,b) the polytope of marginals); strictly convex (the entropy is, prop. 0.3) ⇒ unique solution, which converges to a solution of the LP as \varepsilon \to 0.

Theorem 3.2 — Form of the minimizer and the Sinkhorn algorithm (proved) The unique solution reads \Gamma^\varepsilon = \mathrm{diag}(u)\, K\, \mathrm{diag}(v), \qquad K = e^{-C/\varepsilon}, and the scaling vectors are obtained by the alternating iterations u \leftarrow a\,/\,(K v), \qquad v \leftarrow b\,/\,(K^\top u).

Proof. Form (optimality conditions). Lagrangian with multipliers \lambda_i (rows) and \mu_j (columns): \mathcal{L} = \langle C,\Gamma\rangle + \varepsilon\sum_{ij}\Gamma_{ij} \log\frac{\Gamma_{ij}}{a_ib_j} - \lambda^\top(\Gamma\mathbf{1} - a) - \mu^\top(\Gamma^\top\mathbf{1} - b). At \Gamma^\star, for each (i,j): C_{ij} + \varepsilon\Big(\log\frac{\Gamma_{ij}}{a_ib_j} + 1\Big) - \lambda_i - \mu_j - \varepsilon = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \Gamma^\star_{ij} = a_i b_j\, e^{(\lambda_i + \mu_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} = u_i K_{ij} v_j, with u = e^{\lambda/\varepsilon}, v = (b)\cdot e^{\mu/\varepsilon} absorbing the constants (the a_i, b_j are reabsorbed into u, v). Iterations (Bregman projections). The constraints \Gamma\mathbf{1} = a and \Gamma^\top\mathbf{1} = b are not separated: we alternately project K onto each constraint in the sense of the Bregman divergence \mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot). The projection of \tilde\Gamma = \mathrm{diag}(u)K\, \mathrm{diag}(v) onto \{\Gamma\mathbf{1} = a\}: minimizing \mathrm{KL}(\Gamma\|\tilde\Gamma) under the row constraint gives, by setting \partial/\partial\Gamma_{ij}\big[\sum\Gamma_{ij}\log(\Gamma_{ij}/\tilde\Gamma_{ij}) - \rho_i(\sum_j\Gamma_{ij} - a_i)\big] = \log(\Gamma_{ij}/\tilde\Gamma_{ij}) - \rho_i to zero, that is \Gamma_{ij} = e^{\rho_i}\tilde\Gamma_{ij}: a rescaling of the rows, exactly u \leftarrow a/(Kv); likewise for the columns. Each iteration divides the violation of the other constraint; the sequence converges to the point with the prescribed marginals — the unique point of the polytope of the form \mathrm{diag}(u)K\,\mathrm{diag}(v).∎

Theorem 3.3 — Geometric convergence (Franklin & Lorenz, 1989) For K > 0 (finite costs, \varepsilon > 0), Sinkhorn converges at a geometric rate: the scaling ratios u^{(t)}/r^{(t)} contract in the projective Hilbert metric, at a rate \tanh(\Delta/4) where \Delta depends on K (max/min ratio of the coefficients) — all the faster as \varepsilon is large. In practice: 20 to 200 iterations for 3-digit accuracy (at moderate \varepsilon).

Theorem 3.4 — Gradient of the regularized loss (sketch: envelope/implicit differentiation) \nabla_a\, W_\varepsilon(a, b) = f^\star \quad (\text{optimal dual potential}).

Proof. The Lagrangian of the problem (that of th. 3.2) has a saddle point at (\Gamma^\star, \lambda^\star, \mu^\star): at this point, W_\varepsilon(a,b) = \mathcal{L}(\Gamma^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star). Differentiating the optimality system (the constraints are saturated at the solution, which cancels the derivatives of the multipliers), there remains \partial W_\varepsilon/\partial a_i = \lambda_i/\varepsilon \cdot \varepsilon = \lambda_i (up to the absorption constant): this is the envelope theorem — only the multiplier matters, the plan itself contributes nothing. Practical consequence: a loss a = a_\theta (a batch of features, parameters \theta) can be differentiated without re-differentiating the Sinkhorn iterations: we backpropagate the potential, not the plan.∎

“Debiased” Sinkhorn divergences and epsilon-scaling

  • Entropic bias: W_\varepsilon(a,a) > 0 (the regularized plan “blurs” even between identical distributions) and the plan is concentrated near ab^\top as \varepsilon \to \infty. Correction (Feydy et al., AISTATS 2019): S_\varepsilon(a,b) = W_\varepsilon(a,b) - \tfrac12 W_\varepsilon(a,a) - \tfrac12 W_\varepsilon(b,b) \ \geq\ 0, \qquad S_\varepsilon(a,a) = 0, which interpolates between W (ε→0) and a kernel distance (MMD, ε→∞) — the loss of choice for comparing clouds in deep learning.
  • ε-scaling: at small \varepsilon, K = e^{-C/\varepsilon} is numerically degenerate (range dynamics): start at large \varepsilon (fast convergence), then divide it by ~2 down to the target, reusing (u, v) warm; stabilized variants in log space (ot.sinkhorn_log).
  • GPU and complexity: one iteration = two matrix-vector products (\mathcal{O}(n^2)), fully parallelizable and “batchable” (thousands of problems at once on GPU); the exact LP (\mathcal{O}(n^3 \log n)) lacks this memory regularity — hence Sinkhorn’s supremacy in high dimension.

Definition 3.5 — Sliced transport (1D projections) \mathrm{SW}_p^p(\mu,\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_p^p\big(\theta_\#\mu,\ \theta_\#\nu\big)\,d\theta, the average of the 1D W_p of the projections onto all directions \theta — computable by sorting (prop. 2.6), estimated by Monte-Carlo over L directions: \mathcal{O}(L\,n\log n), with no n \times n cost matrix. Statistical benefits: no curse of dimensionality (estimating \mathrm{SW} converges at n^{-1/2}, versus n^{-1/d} for W_p) — see Tanguy, Flamary & Delon (2024) for the fine analysis of empirical projections. It is also the natural language for the spectrograms of the Signal Processing module (ch. 9): projecting = marginalizing.

4. OT and machine learning

Definition 4.1 — Domain adaptation, covariate shift A domain adaptation problem provides a labeled source (x_i, y_i)_{i \leq n} \sim \mu_S on \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} and an unlabeled target (z_j)_{j \leq m} \sim \mu_T on \mathcal{X}. Working assumption (covariate shift): the labeling rule P(Y\,\|\,X) is the same in both domains, but the marginals \mu_S \neq \mu_T (sensors, populations, image styles…). A classifier trained on the source suffers from the distribution shift; the OT idea: transport the source onto the target before learning — exactly Monge’s problem of ch. 1, applied to clouds of features.

OTDA algorithm — transport, propagate the labels, predict (Courty, Flamary et al., IEEE TPAMI 2016)

  1. Costs: C_{ij} = \|x_i - z_j\|^2 (centered-scaled features, possibly kernel or deep features, cf. the Deep Learning module M1P1, chapter 8 “transfer learning”).
  2. Class regularization: we additionally know that the x_i carry labels — we penalize plans that scatter a single class, via a convex group-lasso-type penalty on the rows of \Gamma (the points of a same class are transported to the same place). Final cost: \langle C, \Gamma\rangle + class penalty, solved by LP (ch. 1) or by regularized Sinkhorn (ch. 3).
  3. Barycentric transport: each source point is sent to the plan-weighted average of its images: \hat T(x_i) \;=\; \frac{1}{a_i}\sum_j\ \Gamma_{ij}\, z_j, and its label y_i follows (the plan is a soft coupling, not a map: this is the Monge barycentric map, optimal for c = \|\cdot\|^2 by corollary 2.3). Label propagation follows: the label predicted for z_j is the (plan-weighted) majority of the classes arriving at z_j.
  4. Learning: train the classifier on the transported pairs (\hat T(x_i),\, y_i) — which live in the target space — then predict on the target.
  5. Out-of-sample: a new point z is predicted directly (it is already in the target space); to transport a new source point without recomputing the plan, interpolate the plan row of the k nearest neighbors of x in the source, or learn a map T_\theta \approx \hat T (a network, Deep Learning module).

Positioning: the module’s instructor is a co-author of the paper — detailed in ch. 7. The JDOT extension (Courty, Flamary et al., NeurIPS 2017) transports the joint law (X, Y) with a cost c\big((x,y),(z,y')\big) = \alpha\|x - z\|^2 + \mathcal{L}(y, y') that includes the label loss: unsupervised and semi-supervised adaptation in the same framework.

Proposition 4.2 — Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, or why GANs are called “Wasserstein” (proved sketch) For c = d (a distance), W_1(\mu, \nu) \;=\; \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}} \leq 1}\ \int f\, d\mu - \int f\, d\nu, the sup being taken over the 1-Lipschitz functions.

Proof. Start from the duality of th. 2.1 with c = d and replace g by the c-transform g = f^c (def. 2.2: this does not decrease the objective). For a distance cost, f^c(y) = \inf_x\,[\,d(x,y) - f(x)\,]. If f is 1-Lipschitz, taking x = y gives f^c(y) = -f(y) (the infimum is attained at x = y since f(x) \geq f(y) - d(x,y)), and the dual equals \int f\,d\mu - \int f\, d\nu. Conversely, the constraint f(x) + f^c(y) \leq d(x,y) with y \to x forces \|f(x) - f(x')\| \leq d(x, x'): every admissible dual pair can be written (up to normalization) with a 1-Lipschitz f — hence the formula (complete proof: Villani 2009, th. 5.10).∎

The WGAN (Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou, ICML 2017) minimizes precisely this quantity between the law of the data and that of a generator G_\theta: \min_\theta\ \max_{D_\phi\ \mathrm{1\!-\!Lip}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathrm{data}}[D_\phi(x)] \ -\ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}[D_\phi(G_\theta(z))], the “critic” D_\phi playing the role of a parametrized Kantorovich potential (the dual of th. 2.1 makes the loss differentiable in \theta). The 1-Lipschitz constraint in practice: weight clipping, gradient penalty (Gulrajani et al., 2017), spectral normalization (Miyato et al., 2018) — cf. the Deep Learning module M1P1, chapter 7 (generative models). A modern computable alternative: the debiased Sinkhorn loss of ch. 3 (th. 3.4: we backpropagate the potential), available with PyTorch/JAX backends in POT.

Definition 4.3 — Wasserstein barycenter (Agueh & Carlier, 2011) For measures \mu_1, \dots, \mu_K and weights w \in \Delta_K (the simplex): \mathrm{Bar}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K; w) \;=\; \arg\min_{\nu}\ \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, W_2^2(\nu, \mu_k). The 1D Gaussian case is solved explicitly by corollary 2.8 (barycentric quantiles). General cases:

  • Fixed support (a grid of p points): the problem is convex in the barycenter weights — quadratic programming (Agueh-Carlier) or iterative Sinkhorn;
  • Iterated Bregman projections (IBP): the entropic version (Benamou et al., 2015): initialize \Gamma_k independently for each k, then repeat for each k a Sinkhorn scaling step (th. 3.2) toward the common barycenter mass, then a rescaling toward the marginal \mu_k — a “squared” Sinkhorn iteration, parallelizable;
  • Free support (Cuturi & Doucet, 2014): optimize positions and weights of the barycenter points by alternating: (i) plans \Gamma_k toward each measure (OT), (ii) moving the barycenter points toward the barycenter of their images — the multi-measure extension of the barycentric transport formula of the OTDA algorithm.

Applications: interpolation of shapes and distributions, color transfer (histograms), atlases and averages in medical imaging, signal normalization (ch. 7: Convolutional Monge Mapping), barycenters of graphs (ch. 5, FGW). POT: ot.bregman.barycenter, ot.lp.free_support_barycenter.

5. Gromov-Wasserstein and beyond

Definition 5.1 — Gromov-Wasserstein transport (Mémoli, 2011) The two clouds live in incomparable spaces: we have no cross cost c(x, y), only internal structures — distance matrices D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} (source) and D' \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m} (target). The quadratic problem: \mathrm{GW}_2^2(D, D'; a, b) \;=\; \min_{\Gamma \in U(a,b)}\ \sum_{i,k,j,l} \big(D_{ik} - D'_{jl}\big)^2\ \Gamma_{ij}\,\Gamma_{kl}, that is, \min_\gamma \mathbb{E}\big[\,\big(\mathcal{D}(X, X') - \mathcal{D}'(Y, Y')\big)^2\,\big] where (X, Y) and (X', Y') are i.i.d. with law \gamma: we look for the coupling that makes the pairs of internal distances as equal as possible. Interpretation: preserve the neighborhoods, the triangles, the “shape” — not the positions. The problem is non-convex in \Gamma (quadratic): local minima, important initialization — a complete contrast with ch. 1.

Theorem 5.2 — Isometric invariance of GW (proved as a sketch) Let \varphi be an isometry between the structured spaces (\mathcal{D}'(\varphi(x), \varphi(x')) = \mathcal{D}(x, x') for all x, x', and \varphi_\#\mu = \nu). Then \mathrm{GW}_2(\mu, \nu) = 0. Conversely (Mémoli): \mathrm{GW}_2(\mu, \nu) = 0 if and only if such a measure-preserving isometry exists.

Proof. The diagonal plan \gamma^\star = (\mathrm{Id}, \varphi)_\#\mu is admissible (marginals \mu and \varphi_\#\mu = \nu). For two copies (X, \varphi(X)) and (X', \varphi(X')) i.i.d. from \gamma^\star, each term of the quadratic sum is \big(\mathcal{D}(X, X') - \mathcal{D}'(\varphi(X), \varphi(X'))\big)^2 = 0 pointwise, by the definition of an isometry: the cost is 0, and 0 is the lower bound (a sum of squares). The converse: a zero cost forces \mathcal{D}(X, X') = \mathcal{D}'(Y, Y') \gamma-almost surely; the support of \gamma^\star (compact, closed) then defines the sought isometry between the supports (a closure argument, admitted).∎

Practical consequence: GW is invariant under rotations, translations, reflections, changes of frame — where W_2 “sees” the absolute offset of the clouds (and equals \|\text{translation}\| even for two isometric copies). It is the right distance for aligning objects defined up to an isometry: graphs, proteins, surfaces, heterogeneous embedding spaces.

GW algorithms: projected (PGW), entropic, sliced (SGW)

  • Projected GW — PGW (Peyré, Solomon, Gramfort & Ferrand, ICML 2016): linearize the quadratic problem around the current plan \Gamma^{(t)}. The gradient of the objective in \Gamma reads c^{(t)}_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{k,l}\ \big(D_{ik} - D'_{jl}\big)^2\ \Gamma^{(t)}_{kl} \;=\; \big[\,D^2\,\Gamma^{(t)}\,\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1}\,{D'}^2\!\top\Gamma^{(t)\top} - 2\,D\,\Gamma^{(t)}\,D'\,\big]_{ij}, (in matrix form: two matrix-matrix products, \mathcal{O}(n^2 m)) and we iterate: solve the linear OT with cost c^{(t)} (exact LP or Sinkhorn) → new plan \Gamma^{(t+1)}. This is a fixed-point / projected-gradient algorithm: convergence to a local minimum, guaranteed decrease of the GW value (each linearized OT underestimates the quadratic — a majoration-minimization technique).
  • Entropic GW (Solomon et al., ICML 2015): regularize each linearized subproblem by \varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL} and solve it by Sinkhorn (ch. 3): dense and smooth plans, GPU, but entropic blur — ε-scaling is used. This is the default ot.gromov.gromov_wasserstein version (parameter epsilon).
  • SGW (Vayer et al., NeurIPS 2019): project the structures onto 1D lines and average — the sliced analogue of ch. 3, speeds of \mathcal{O}(L n\log n) and no statistical curse.

Definition 5.3 — Fused Gromov-Wasserstein: features and structure (Vayer, Chapel, Flamary, Tavenard & Courty, ICML 2019) When the objects also carry comparable attributes (features F \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}, F' \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d'} with a cross cost C_F(x_i, y_j), typically \|F_i - F'_j\|^2 after aligning the dimensions): \mathrm{FGW}_{\alpha} \;=\; \min_{\Gamma \in U(a,b)}\ (1-\alpha)\sum_{i,k,j,l} \big(D_{ik} - D'_{jl}\big)^2 \Gamma_{ij}\Gamma_{kl} \;+\; \alpha \sum_{i,j} C_F(x_i, y_j)\,\Gamma_{ij}. The parameter \alpha \in [0, 1] arbitrates between fidelity to the attributes (\alpha = 1: pure Wasserstein on the features) and fidelity to the structure (\alpha = 0: pure GW); the paper shows a form of continuity of the distance in \alpha and bounds between FGW and its two extremes. The algorithm: PGW applied to the combined cost (the same linearization iteration). This is the distance for attributed graphs: molecules (atoms: features; bonds: structure), social networks, brains — cf. the Graph ML module M1P2, chapter 5 (GNNs: message passing combines exactly attributes and structure) and chapter 9 (graph generation/evaluation); the FGW barycenter of several graphs defines a graph “average” (Vayer et al.).

Definition 5.4 — COOT: co-optimal transport over rows and columns (Redko, Vayer, Flamary & Courty, NeurIPS 2020) Two data matrices X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} (objects as rows, variables as columns) and Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p} — object and variable dimensions possibly different. COOT looks for two couplings: \pi \in U(a, c) between the rows and \sigma \in U(b, d) between the columns: \min_{\pi, \sigma}\ \sum_{i,j,k,l}\ \big(X_{ik} - Y_{jl}\big)^2\ \pi_{ij}\, \sigma_{kl}, solved by alternation: with \sigma fixed, the problem in \pi is a linear OT with cost \sum_{kl}(X_{ik} - Y_{jl})^2\sigma_{kl} (and symmetrically). COOT unifies and generalizes: classical OT (a single cross-cost matrix), GW (when X and Y are themselves internal distance matrices D, D'), co-clustering and heterogeneous domain adaptation (aligning simultaneously samples and sensors/features — for example two EEG datasets with different electrodes). Here again: non-convex alternation, local minimum, initialization by OT or k-means.

6. Fairness through transport

Definition 6.1 — The framework: sensitive attribute, demographic parity, equalized odds Data (X, S, Y): features, sensitive attribute S (gender, origin…), label Y; predictor \hat Y = h(X) (possibly randomized). Two emblematic criteria:

  • Demographic parity (DP): \hat Y independent of S — the same positive decision rates per group;
  • Equalized odds (EO, Hardt, Price & Srebro, 2016): \hat Y independent of S given Y — the same true positive and false positive rates per group.

OT enters both scenarios: (i) pre-processing — repair the feature distribution with a Wasserstein barycenter; (ii) post-processing — couple the per-group score distributions. In both cases, the central object is a coupling, i.e. a transport plan as in ch. 1.

Theorem 6.2 — Repair by Wasserstein barycenter (Gordaliza, Del Barrio, Gamboa & Loubes, ICML 2019) Algorithm (full repair, scalar case then multivariate).

  1. Estimate the conditional laws \mu_s = \mathcal{L}(X \,\|\, S = s) by the empirical quantiles Q_s = \hat F_s^{-1} (sorting, prop. 2.6).
  2. Form the barycenter of the \mu_s weighted by the proportions p_s = \mathbb{P}(S = s); in 1D, corollary 2.8 gives its quantile function pointwise: Q^\star(u) \;=\; \sum_s\ p_s\, Q_s(u).
  3. Repair: replace each observation by its counterpart at the same quantile level in the barycenter: \tilde X_i = Q^\star\big(\hat F_s(X_i)\big) for S_i = s. In the multivariate case, replace the 1D transport by a Sinkhorn plan between each cloud and a common entropic barycenter (def. 4.3), then use the barycentric projection of the OTDA algorithm (ch. 4).
  4. Retrain the model on the (\tilde X_i, Y_i) without the sensitive attribute.

Proof. Full repair guarantees demographic parity. Let U_s = \hat F_s(X) for X \sim \mu_s: U_s is (asymptotically) uniform on [0,1], and \tilde X = Q^\star(U_s). Hence \mathcal{L}(\tilde X\,\|\,S = s) = \mathcal{L}\big(Q^\star(U)\big) is the same law for every s — the barycenter. Identical conditionals give independence: \mathbb{P}(\tilde X \in A, S = s) = \mathbb{P}(\tilde X \in A\,\|\,S = s)\, \mathbb{P}(S = s) = \mathbb{P}(\tilde X \in A)\,\mathbb{P}(S = s). Any predictor h(\tilde X) then inherits independence from S: DP, regardless of the downstream classifier.∎

Partial repair: interpolate the quantiles, \tilde X^{(\lambda)} = (1-\lambda)\, Q_s(U_s) + \lambda\, Q^\star(U_s) with \lambda \in [0, 1] — \lambda = 0: untouched data; \lambda = 1: full repair. Utility/fairness trade-off: as \lambda is swept, the demographic parity gap decreases (the conditionals get closer in Wasserstein distance, linearly in \lambda for the interpolated transport) while accuracy decreases all the faster as X carries signal correlated with S: the curve (DP gap on the x-axis, accuracy on the y-axis) is typically decreasing with a “knee” — one chooses \lambda at the elbow, where a lot of equity has been bought for little utility. Proposition 6.3 — Bicausal OT, counterfactuals and optimal EO post-processing by coupling (simple case proved) Bicausal OT. To compare two “twin” individuals differing only in the sensitive attribute, we couple the joint laws \mathcal{L}(S, X) with a plan \pi respecting the causal structure S \to X (bicausal coupling: the copy of X depends on the copy of S only through the coupling of the conditionals \mathcal{L}(X\,\|\,S = s) — de Lara, González-Sanz, Asher, Risser & Loubes, JMLR 2024). The counterfactual X^\star(s') — “the value X would have taken had S been s'” — is the coupled point: constructed by OT between \mathcal{L}(X\,\|\,S = s) and \mathcal{L}(X\,\|\,S = s'). Applications: measuring discrimination \mathbb{E}\big[c(X, X^\star)\big], predicting on the counterfactual (“counterfactual” fairness).

Proof. EO post-processing: the optimal coupling is the increasing coupling. Simple setting: Y \in \{0,1\}, real score R_s = f(X) per group, and a randomized post-processing \hat Y_s = g_s(R_s, U), U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1] common. (i) Such a post-processing is exactly a coupling between the law of R_s\,\|\,Y = y and a target Bernoulli law — the noise U realizes the plan. (ii) EO requires that the law of \hat Y_s\,\|\,Y = y be the same for both groups: we therefore couple simultaneously the two conditional score laws to a common target. (iii) Among these couplings, the one maximizing utility (accuracy, increasing in the score within each class) is the increasing coupling — quantile matching: the uncrossing argument of the proof of prop. 2.6 applies word for word: uncrossing two pairs (making the coupling more comonotone) cannot decrease a utility that is increasing in both scores. Concretely: bring each score to its rank U_s = \hat F_s(R_s) (quantile within its group), then apply the same decision rule to the U_s — the equalization of the conditional laws happens on the quantiles, and choosing the common operating point (TPR, FPR) maximizes the linear utility \mathbb{P}(Y{=}1)\,\mathrm{TPR} + \mathbb{P}(Y{=}0)\,(1 - \mathrm{FPR}) over the feasible set (the intersection of the two regions convexified by randomization — the “mixture of predictors” procedure of Hardt et al., reread as an OT coupling).∎

Limits and debate: incompatible metrics, utility, causal assumptions

  • Theoretical incompatibilities: when the base rates \mathbb{P}(Y = 1\,\|\,S = s) differ across groups, calibration and equalized odds are mutually exclusive (Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan 2016; Chouldechova 2017): no method — OT included — can satisfy all the criteria; choosing the metric is choosing the trade-off.
  • Utility cost: DP repair can destroy a legitimate signal (if S genuinely predicts Y, independence forces a loss); “blind” repair is not always fair in the individual sense.
  • Causal assumptions: counterfactuals by bicausal OT assume a structure S \to X and the absence of unmeasured confounding; two different optimal bicausal couplings (non-uniqueness, cf. example 1.2) can yield different counterfactuals — OT provides a family of models, to be audited (the JMLR 2024 paper proposes statistical tests).
  • Position of the course: OT is not an answer to the question of justice, but a geometric language for quantifying trade-offs: distances between groups, barycenters, optimal couplings — the same objects as in ch. 1-5.

import numpy as np, ot
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
# Adult: X = numerical variables, S = gender, Y = income > 50k
# (load with pandas.read_csv("adult.data"), encode the categoricals)

def repair_1d(x, s, lam):
    """Partial quantile repair, feature by feature (th. 6.2)."""
    xR = x.copy()
    qs = {sv: np.sort(x[s == sv]) for sv in np.unique(s)}
    n = min(len(v) for v in qs.values())          # common ranks
    bary = np.mean([v[np.linspace(0, len(v)-1, n).astype(int)]
                    for v in qs.values()], axis=0)  # Q*: average of the quantiles
    for sv in np.unique(s):
        v, idx = np.sort(x[s == sv]), np.argsort(x[s == sv])
        ranks = np.linspace(0, len(v)-1, n)
        rep = (1-lam) * np.interp(ranks, range(len(v)), v) + lam * bary
        xR[s == sv] = np.interp(np.argsort(np.argsort(x[s == sv])),
                                ranks, rep)[np.argsort(idx)]
    return xR

for lam in np.linspace(0, 1, 11):                  # lambda -> trade-off curve
    Xr = repair_1d(X_num, S, lam)
    clf = LogisticRegression().fit(Xr, Y)

    dp = abs(clf.predict_proba(Xr[S==0])[:,1].mean()
           - clf.predict_proba(Xr[S==1])[:,1].mean())
    print(f"lambda={lam:.1f}  accuracy={acc:.3f}  ecart_DP={dp:.3f}")

POT — Python Optimal Transport (Flamary et al., JMLR 2021): the library of the course POT: Python Optimal Transport, Journal of Machine Learning Research 22(78), 2021 — pythonot.github.io. Principle: a unified library of OT solvers, with an API with three inputs (measures, costs, solver):

  • Application: ot.da — the transporter of the OTDA algorithm of ch. 4, packaged with label propagation and out-of-sample.

Result: some sixty solvers drawn from the literature, NumPy/PyTorch/JAX backends (differentiation of the potential, th. 3.4), an example gallery — the reference tool for numerical OT, used from medical applications to generative AI. Positioning: all the labs of this module use it; the architecture of ch. 3 (solvers, regularization, GPU) is exactly its architecture.

Optimal Transport for Domain Adaptation (Courty, Flamary, Tuia & Rakotomamonjy, IEEE TPAMI 2016) Optimal Transport for Domain Adaptation, IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence 39(9), 2016. Principle: that of the OTDA algorithm of ch. 4 — regularized coupling (entropy + class regularization by group-lasso) between the source/target clouds, barycentric transport of the labeled points, learning in the target space. Result: theory (error bounds linking the source-target Wasserstein distance and the target risk) and experimental gains on images and texts; the extension JDOT (Courty, Flamary, Habrard & Rakotomamonjy, NeurIPS 2017) transports the joint law (X, Y) by including the label loss in the cost — the direct ancestor of OT training losses. Positioning: this is ch. 4 of the course; the modern benchmark SKADA-Bench (TMLR 2025) measures its successors.

Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (Vayer, Chapel, Flamary, Tavenard & Courty, ICML 2019) Optimal Transport for structured data with application on graphs, ICML 2019 (PMLR 97). Principle: the FGW distance of ch. 5 — combining the feature cost and the structure cost under a parameter \alpha, with continuity and bounds with respect to the two extremes; a linearization-type algorithm (PGW). Result: classification and barycenters of attributed graphs (the “average” of several graphs exists: it is used in classification by nearest barycenter); applications to molecules/time series. Positioning: def. 5.3 of the course; the “graphs” side extends into M1P2 (Graph ML module, ch. 5 and 9) and up to FGW-template GNNs (NeurIPS 2022).

CO-Optimal Transport (Redko, Vayer, Flamary & Courty, NeurIPS 2020) CO-Optimal Transport, NeurIPS 2020 — code github.com/PythonOT/COOT. Principle: def. 5.4 — transporting simultaneously the samples (rows) and the variables (columns) of two data matrices via two alternating couplings. Positioning: the “double alternation” of ch. 5; available in POT (ot.gromov.co_optimal_transport).

Exercises

  1. Duality in dimension 2 (solution). Source a = (1/2, 1/2) at x_1 = 0, x_2 = 1 (in \mathbb{R}), target b = (1/2, 1/2) at y_1 = 0, y_2 = 2, cost \|x - y\|. Write the primal, guess the optimal plan, deduce the dual potentials from the complementarity of th. 2.1 and check the equality of the values. Solution (sketch). C = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}; extreme admissible plans: \Gamma_1 = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}1/2 & 0\\ 0 & 1/2\end{smallmatrix}\big) (cost 1/2) and \Gamma_2 = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1/2\\ 1/2 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\big) (cost 3/2): optimum \Gamma_1, W_1 = 1/2. Complementarity: \Gamma_{12}^\star = \Gamma_{21}^\star = 0 constrains only \Gamma_{11}, \Gamma_{22} > 0: f_1 + g_1 = 0, f_2 + g_2 = 0 and inequalities f_1 + g_2 \leq 2, f_2 + g_1 \leq 1; take f = (0, 1), g = (0, -1): value \langle a, f\rangle + \langle b, g\rangle = 1/2. = primal: strong duality verified by hand.
  2. Kantorovich-Rubinstein (solution). In the framework of prop. 4.2, show that if f is 1-Lipschitz then f^c = -f for the cost c = \|\cdot\| (in dimension 1), and conclude that W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f) \leq 1} \int f\,d(\mu - \nu). Solution (key). f^c(y) = \inf_x [\|x - y\| - f(x)]. The choice x = y gives f^c(y) \leq -f(y); for every x, f(x) \geq f(y) - \|x - y\| (1-Lipschitz), hence \|x - y\| - f(x) \leq -f(y): infimum attained at x = y, f^c = -f. The dual of prop. 2.1 becomes \int f\,d\mu + \int (-f)\,d\nu over the admissible pairs (f, -f), which are exactly (up to an additive constant) the 1-Lipschitz functions — hence the formula.
  3. Sinkhorn 2×2 by hand (solution). With a = b = (1/2, 1/2), C = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \varepsilon = 1: compute K, initialize v = \mathbf{1}, perform two complete iterations of u \leftarrow a/(Kv), v \leftarrow b/(K^\top u), and check that the row marginals converge to a. Solution (values). K = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & e^{-1}\\ e^{-1} & 1\end{smallmatrix}\big). Iteration 1: Kv = (1 + e^{-1}, 1 + e^{-1})^\top, u = \frac{1}{2(1+e^{-1})}(1,1)^\top; K^\top u identical, v = u. By symmetry the fixed point is u = v = \frac{1}{2(1+e^{-1})}\mathbf{1}: \Gamma = \mathrm{diag}(u)K\,\mathrm{diag}(u) indeed has rows and columns summing to 1/2 — after a single complete iteration here (symmetric case); the entropic plan “blurs” the diagonal toward the anti-diagonal (entropic bias, ch. 3).
  4. Bures in simulation (lab). Draw n = 5000 points from two anisotropic 2D Gaussians (\Sigma_1 = \mathrm{diag}(4, 1) rotated, \Sigma_2 = \mathrm{diag}(1, 4)), compare the empirical W_2^2 (Sinkhorn on the samples) with the closed formula of th. 2.7 and with ot.gaussian.wass2. Observe the effect of \varepsilon. Hint. The gap comes from the entropic bias (use ot.sinkhorn_divergence) and from the sampling noise (n^{-1/d}, th. 4.5: severe in dimension 2 if n is small).
  5. Vertices of the transport polytope (solution) — promised in ch. 1. Show that an optimal plan can be chosen with at most n + m - 1 non-zero coefficients (th. 1.3), build an example where the optimal plan is not deterministic, and connect it to example 1.2. Solution (sketch). Vertex: nm variables, constraints of rank \leq n + m - 1 (sum of the rows = sum of the columns); a vertex requires nm independent active constraints, of which at least nm - (n+m-1) are \Gamma_{ij} = 0. Non-deterministic example: a = (1) at x_1, b = (1/2, 1/2) — every plan has the form (1/2, 1/2), one row toward two columns: no vertex is a partial permutation. Link: this is example 1.2 (one mass, two half-masses); on the other hand, if a has uniform coefficients 1/n and the costs are generic, the “almost deterministic” vertices have \leq n + m - 1 non-zeros — each row carries at most m destinations, and sparsity limits the splitting.
  6. Weighted Gaussian barycenter (hint). Rederive corollary 2.8 with arbitrary weights, then in the case of two laws \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and \mathcal{N}(0, 9): show that the standard deviation of the equal-weight barycenter equals 2 (and not \sqrt{5}), and comment (“the mean of the variances” is not the barycenter). Hint. Minimize pointwise in u the quantile function (zero derivative), then identify the resulting Gaussian quantile: \sigma = \sum_k w_k \sigma_k.
  7. WMD on two sentences (solution). With real word2vec embeddings (Text Mining and NLP module, ch. 3), compute the WMD between “le président s’adresse au congrès” and “Obama parle au parlement”, then between two unrelated sentences; display the plan (ot.emd) and comment on the word matchings. Show \mathrm{GW}_2(\mu, \nu) = 0 and W_2(\mu, \nu) = r. What happens under a rotation? Solution. The translation \tau preserves all the internal distances (an isometry in the sense of th. 5.2): diagonal plan at zero cost. For W_2, the plan (\mathrm{Id}, \tau)_\#\mu costs \big(\int \|x - (x + \tau)\|^2 d\mu\big)^{1/2} = r, and no coupling can do better than translating the mean (triangle inequality for W_2, th. 2.5).
