0. Background: probability and graphs

The module’s guiding thread: a social network is a random graph (who follows whom?), traversed by stochastic processes (what spreads, and how fast?). The two mathematical pillars are therefore discrete probability — distributions, moments, convergence — and graph theory. This chapter puts back in place the definitions and the three basic tools: the first and second moment methods, branching processes, and random walks.

L3 review — probabilistic toolbox (cf. Refresher Statistics module)

  • Standard distributions (ch. 3): binomial \mathrm{Bin}(n, p) with expectation np; Poisson \mathrm{Pois}(c) with \mathbb{P}(X = k) = e^{-c} c^k / k!, variance = mean; exponential with parameter \lambda, memoryless — the waiting time of Poisson processes.
  • Convergence and concentration (ch. 4): weak law of large numbers, CLT, Markov’s inequality \mathbb{P}(X \geq a) \leq \mathbb{E}[X]/a and Chebyshev’s; this is the machinery behind the thresholds of ch. 1.
  • Estimation (ch. 5): maximum likelihood, bias, consistency — it will be used on the SBM (ch. 6) and on power laws (ch. 2).
  • Testing (ch. 6): hypothesis testing, type I/II errors — the detection threshold of ch. 6 is exactly the boundary where every test becomes powerless.

L3 review — graph toolbox (cf. Graph ML module, ch. 0)

  • Vocabulary: G = (V, E), degrees d_v, neighbors \mathcal{N}(v), adjacency matrix A, paths, connected components, diameter, clustering coefficient c_v = \frac{2\,|\text{edges in } \mathcal{N}(v)|}{d_v(d_v - 1)}.
  • Spectrum (spectral theorem: Graph ML, Theorem 0.3): eigenvalues of A, spectral radius \rho(A) = largest eigenvalue in absolute value (connected graph: simple, positive, Perron–Frobenius vector with strictly positive coordinates).
  • Random walks (Graph ML, Proposition 0.6): P = D^{-1}A, stationary distribution \pi_v = d_v / 2m, mixing time — the underpinning of rumor protocols (ch. 4) and distributed algorithms (ch. 5).
  • Graph algorithms (Refresher CS module, ch. 7): BFS/DFS in O(n + m), connected components — the basic building block of the simulations in ch. 8.

Definition 0.1 — Random graph A random graph is a random variable taking values in the set of graphs (simple, undirected, unless stated otherwise) on a fixed set of n vertices. Two equivalent specifications dominate: by measure (a distribution over the 2^{\binom{n}{2}} possible graphs) or by generative procedure (a randomized algorithm that builds the graph edge by edge, or vertex by vertex). Chapters 1, 2 and 6 give the three canonical generative models: Erdős–Rényi, configuration/Barabási–Albert, and the stochastic block model.

Definition 0.2 — Branching process (Galton–Watson) Starting from one initial individual, each individual has an i.i.d. random offspring with distribution (p_k), of mean m = \sum_k k\, p_k. If Z_t denotes the size of generation t: \mathbb{E}[Z_t] = m^t. The process dies out almost surely if m \leq 1 and survives with strictly positive probability if m > 1.

The extinction probability q is the smallest solution in [0,1] of s = G(s), where G is the generating function of the offspring; the survival probability is 1 - q.

This is the universal tool for “early stages of processes”: giant component (ch. 1), epidemic (ch. 3), rumor spreading (ch. 4).

Proposition 0.3 — First moment method For any nonnegative integer random variable X: \mathbb{P}(X \geq 1) \leq \mathbb{E}[X] \quad \text{(Markov)}. Consequently, if \mathbb{E}[X_n] \to 0 then X_n = 0 with probability tending to 1.

And if \mathrm{Var}(X_n) = o(\mathbb{E}[X_n]^2) with \mathbb{E}[X_n] \to \infty, then X_n > 0 with probability tending to 1 (second moment, via Chebyshev): \mathbb{P}(X_n = 0) \leq \mathbb{P}(|X_n - \mathbb{E}X_n| \geq \mathbb{E}X_n) \leq \mathrm{Var}(X_n)/\mathbb{E}[X_n]^2 \to 0. All of ch. 1 rests on this pair.

1. Random graphs: Erdős–Rényi

Concrete scene: a “community” detected in a network turns out to be a mere density artifact. To tell structure from chance, one needs a model of a graph with no structure at all; this chapter sets up that model, G(n,p), and its threshold phenomena.

What breaks No null model; over-interpreting a hub; confusing structural correlation with social causation.

The G(n, p) model of Gilbert–Erdős–Rényi is the primitive atom: n vertices, each pair connected independently with probability p. It has no structure — and that is precisely what makes it the touchstone: a phenomenon observed on a real network is “significant” only if it is improbable under G(n, p) at the same size and density (this is the role of the null model in modularity, ch. 7).

Definition 1.1 — The two Erdős–Rényi models G(n, p): each of the \binom{n}{2} edges is present independently with probability p. G(n, m): a uniform choice among graphs with exactly m edges.

For m \approx p \binom{n}{2}, the two are interchangeable to within o(m) edges (we will move from one to the other without notice). Immediate expectations: \mathbb{E}[|E|] = p \binom{n}{2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{deg}(v)] = (n-1) p =: d \quad (\text{mean degree}).

Proposition 1.2 — Degree distribution: binomial, then Poisson In G(n, p), the degree D_v follows the law \mathrm{Bin}(n-1, p).

In the sparse regime p = c/n (mean degree c constant), D_v converges in law to \mathrm{Pois}(c): \binom{n-1}{k} \Big( \frac{c}{n} \Big)^k \Big( 1 - \frac{c}{n} \Big)^{n-1-k} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} e^{-c}\, \frac{c^k}{k!}.

Proof. First factor: \binom{n-1}{k} \sim \frac{(n-1)^k}{k!}, and (n-1)^k (c/n)^k \to c^k.

Second: (1 - c/n)^{n-1-k} = \exp\big( (n - 1 - k) \ln(1 - c/n) \big) = \exp\big( -(n-1-k) (c/n + O(1/n^2)) \big) \to e^{-c}, since k is fixed and (n-1-k)/n \to 1. ∎

Theorem 1.3 (emergence of the giant component, p = c/n) Let C_1 be the largest connected component of G(n, c/n).

  1. if c \leq 1: |C_1| = O(\log n) with high probability; all components are trees or “trees plus one edge”;

  2. if c > 1: |C_1| = \rho\, n + o(n) with high probability, where \rho \in (0, 1] is the unique solution of

    Key result — size of the giant component \rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho}.

    All other components have size O(\log n).

We call c = 1 the percolation threshold — the most famous phase transition of networks.

Proof. (Sketch by exploration and branching.) Let us explore the component of a vertex v by queue (BFS): each discovered vertex presents X not-yet-seen neighbors, where X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n - O(\text{seen}), c/n) \approx \mathrm{Pois}(c), and these laws remain nearly independent as long as the exploration stays small compared to n.

The component of v therefore behaves like a Galton–Watson process of mean c (Definition 0.2):

  • almost-sure extinction if c \leq 1 (components O(\log n); in the critical regime c = 1 the size is n^{2/3}; outside the syllabus);
  • survival with probability \rho if c > 1, where \rho is the survival probability of the branching: \rho = 1 - G(1 - \rho) with G the generating function of \mathrm{Pois}(c), that is, \rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho}.

The fraction of vertices in “infinite” components equals \rho — this is the relative size of C_1. ∎

Theorem 1.4 (connectivity threshold) — proved in sketch form For p = (\ln n + \omega(1))/n (i.e. np = \ln n + \omega(1)), G(n, p) is connected with probability tending to 1. For p = (\ln n - \omega(1))/n, it is disconnected with probability tending to 1. More precisely, for p = (\ln n + t)/n with t fixed: \mathbb{P}(G(n,p) \text{ connected}) \longrightarrow \exp\big( -e^{-t} \big).

The connectivity threshold coincides with the threshold for the disappearance of isolated vertices: at the moment the last isolated vertex disappears, the graph becomes connected.

Proof. (Sketch, first and second moments — cf. Proposition 0.3.) Let X be the number of isolated vertices: \mathbb{E}[X] = n (1 - p)^{n-1} = n \exp\big( (n-1)\ln(1 - p) \big) \approx n\, e^{-np} = e^{-t} \quad \text{for } np = \ln n + t.

Regime np = \ln n - \omega(1) (so t = -\omega(1), \mathbb{E}[X] = e^{-t} \to \infty): an isolated vertex exists. The events “v isolated” are not independent, but nearly: \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X](1 - o(1)) + \text{cross terms}\, o(\mathbb{E}[X]^2) The covariances contribute only O(\mathbb{E}[X]^2/n), since \mathbb{P}(u, v \text{ both isolated}) \leq (1-p)^{2(n-1)-1}. Chebyshev gives \mathbb{P}(X = 0) \leq \mathrm{Var}(X)/\mathbb{E}[X]^2 \to 0 : an isolated vertex exists, the graph is not connected.

Regime np = \ln n + \omega(1) (\mathbb{E}[X] \to 0): no more isolated vertices. By Markov, \mathbb{P}(X \geq 1) \leq \mathbb{E}[X] \to 0. It remains to see that “no isolated vertex” \Rightarrow “connected” — this is the structural theorem: if all components have at least 2 vertices, each contains a path or a cycle, and the arithmetic deficit forces components of size O(\log n). Their number is then O(n/\log n) \cdot (1-p)^{\Omega(\log n)} \to 0 (each pair of components of size \geq 2 not connected costs a factor (1-p)^{s s'} that is very small when s, s' \geq 2): a single component remains.

The limit \exp(-e^{-t}) is the classical Gumbel law of extremes — the first isolated vertex to disappear is an extreme event. ∎

Proposition 1.5 — Diameter in the dense regime If np \geq \ln n \cdot \omega(1) (in particular np = n^{a}, a > 0), the diameter of G(n, p) equals \mathrm{diam} = (1 + o(1)) \frac{\ln n}{\ln (np)} with high probability.

Intuition: the neighborhood of a vertex grows like (np)^r after r BFS steps (each new vertex brings \approx np neighbors), and covers n vertices when (np)^r \approx n.

For p = c/n, c > 1, the giant component has diameter \Theta(\log n) and typical distances \frac{\log n}{\log c} — the “six degrees of separation” phenomenon (ch. 2).

Theorem 1.6 (thresholds for subgraph appearance — triangles) For p = o(1/n): G(n,p) contains no triangle with probability tending to 1. For p = \omega(1/n): it contains at least one with probability tending to 1.

More generally (Erdős–Rényi 1960), for H strictly balanced, the threshold for the appearance of a copy of H is n^{-1/m(H)}, where m(H) = \max_{J \subseteq H} e_J / v_J (maximum density of a subgraph); for the triangle m = 1, for K_4, m = 3/2.

Proof. (First moment, in full.) Let T be the number of triangles: \mathbb{E}[T] = \binom{n}{3} p^3 \sim \frac{(np)^3}{6}. If np \to 0, then \mathbb{E}[T] \to 0 and Markov concludes: \mathbb{P}(T \geq 1) \to 0.

(Second moment, sketch.) If np \to \infty, we compute \mathrm{Var}(T) = \sum_{T_1, T_2} \big( \mathbb{P}(T_1 \wedge T_2) - p^6 \big). Pairs of triangles that are disjoint or share a vertex are independent or nearly so: their contribution is zero, or \binom{n}{3}\binom{n}{2} p^5 = O(\mathbb{E}[T] \cdot n^2 p^2 / np) \ldots ; only pairs sharing an edge contribute, in the amount \binom{n}{3} O(n) p^5 = o(\mathbb{E}[T]^2) as soon as np \to \infty.

Chebyshev concludes: T \geq 1 with probability tending to 1. Exercise 7 asks for the complete computation. ∎

Regime of p Structure of G(n,p) Network analogy
p = c/n, c < 1 components O(\log n), tree-like fragmented graph, subcritical
p = 1/n critical: component of size n^{2/3}, spread out percolation point

What G(n,p) cannot do Three systematic gaps with real social networks, which motivate ch. 2:

  1. real degrees are heterogeneous (heavy tails), not Poisson;
  2. real clustering is high (my friends know each other), whereas in G(n,c/n) the clustering coefficient equals p = c/n \to 0;
  3. real networks grow (edges do not all appear at the same time).

But to test a heuristic, generate a null, or calibrate a threshold, G(n,p) remains the first reflex — it is also the generator of the labs (Graph ML module, ch. 9).

2. Prescribed degrees, small worlds, scale-free

Real networks have hubs and many triangles, two features G(n,p) cannot produce. This chapter builds three models that add them one at a time — imposed degrees, local clustering with a few shortcuts, growth by preferential attachment — and checks what each one gains.

Definition 2.1 — Configuration model (Bollobás; Molloy–Reed) Take a degree sequence d_1, \dots, d_n with even sum 2m. The configuration model attaches d_i half-edges (stubs) to vertex i, then pairs the 2m half-edges uniformly at random ((2m - 1)!! possible pairings).

The result is a multigraph: loops and multiple edges are possible, O(1) on average when the d_i remain o(\sqrt n); in practice one erases them. The point: the degree distribution is imposed — it can be fitted to a real network — while everything else remains “maximally random”.

Theorem 2.2 (Molloy–Reed criterion, statement) Let D be the degree distribution (empirical limit p_k = \frac{1}{n}|\{i : d_i = k\}|). The configuration graph admits a giant component of strictly positive fraction if and only if

Key result — Molloy–Reed criterion \sum_{k} k (k - 2)\, p_k > 0 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \mathbb{E}[D^2] > 2\, \mathbb{E}[D].

The heuristic: when exploring the component of a vertex, each traversed edge leads to a new vertex whose excess degree follows the biased distribution \tilde p_k = \frac{(k+1) p_{k+1}}{\mathbb{E}[D]} (size bias: an edge arrives more often at a high-degree vertex). The exploration branching has mean \frac{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D]}, and the threshold is this mean crossing above 1.

This same \frac{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D]} will be the R_0 of epidemics on graphs with heterogeneous degrees (ch. 3).

Example 2.3 — Power law: p_k \sim C k^{-\gamma} For \gamma \in (2, 3) (the regime of real social networks — Twitter, the web, collaborations): \mathbb{E}[D] is finite but \mathbb{E}[D^2] diverges — criterion 2.2 is widely positive: robust giant component. Cascading consequences (ch. 3): epidemic threshold tending to 0, robustness to random failures but fragility to targeted attacks on hubs. For \gamma > 3, everything is finite and the world becomes Poissonian again to first approximation.

Definition 2.4 — Watts–Strogatz small world (1998) Start from a ring of n vertices, each connected to its K nearest neighbors (a highly clustered, very “wide” graph: diameter \Theta(n/K)).

Each edge is rewired independently with probability \beta, to a uniform destination (no duplicates). The model interpolates: \beta = 0 gives the “circle” world, \beta = 1 gives a graph close to Erdős–Rényi.

The spectacular result of Watts and Strogatz: as soon as \beta \approx 0.01, the diameter drops to \Theta(\log n) while the clustering coefficient stays close to its \beta = 0 value — the two quantities become decoupled. This is the formalization of Milgram’s experiments (1967): very local networks, crossed via a few random links — “six degrees of separation”.

Kleinberg (2000) shows the sharp condition for such a network to be navigable (finding a short path in a decentralized way, without a global map): the distribution of shortcut links must scale as r^{-2} — exactly what social networks rebuild.

Theorem 2.5 — Barabási–Albert: preferential attachment produces p_k \sim k^{-3} (master equation) The model: vertices are added one at a time; vertex t arrives with m edges, each connected to an existing vertex i with probability \Pi(i) = d_i(t) / \sum_j d_j(t) — “the rich get richer” (Matthew effect). Then the degree distribution converges to p_k \;\sim\; \frac{2 m^2}{k^{3}} \qquad (k \geq m), with an exponent independent of m.

Proof. (Continuous method — the master equation sketched.) Let d_i(t) denote the degree of vertex i (arrived at time t_i) at time t, with \sum_j d_j(t) = 2mt. The master equation reads, in unit time steps, \mathbb{E}[d_i(t+1) - d_i(t)] = m \cdot \frac{d_i(t)}{2 m t}, hence the differential equation \frac{\partial d_i}{\partial t} = \frac{d_i}{2t}, with solution d_i(t) = m \Big( \frac{t}{t_i} \Big)^{1/2}. Old vertices (t_i small) are thus proportionally richer — exactly the heterogeneity missing from Erdős–Rényi.

Now for the distribution: for k \geq m, \mathbb{P}(d_i(t) < k) = \mathbb{P}\Big( t_i > \frac{m^2 t}{k^2} \Big) = 1 - \frac{m^2 t}{k^2 (t - m)} \approx 1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2}, using that the arrival time t_i is (asymptotically) uniform on [0, t]. Differentiating: p_k = -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} k} \Big( 1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2} \Big) \Big/ 1 = \frac{2 m^2}{k^3}, exponent \gamma = 3, natural cutoff k_{\max} \sim \sqrt n (the degree of the oldest vertex d_1(n) = m \sqrt n). ∎

Estimating an exponent — the pitfall Regressing \log p_k against \log k on a log-log plot is biased (noisy tails, empty bins); the reference is Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (SIAM Review 2009): Hill estimator on the threshold k_{\min} chosen by a KS test, then a likelihood-ratio test against the log-normal/exponential alternative (Refresher Statistics module, ch. 6). Many published “power laws” do not survive this protocol.

3. Epidemic processes on graphs

A meme, a virus, a rumor: the same mathematics. The canonical model is the SIR epidemic (Susceptible → Infectious → Recovered) or its relapse version SIS, posed no longer on a homogeneous population but on a contact graph. The central question: is there a threshold below which the epidemic dies out quickly — and what does this threshold become when the graph is heterogeneous?

Definition 3.1 — SIR and SIS on a graph Each vertex is in a state; the infection and recovery clocks are independent exponentials:

  • SIR: an infectious vertex transmits the infection to each healthy neighbor at rate \beta, and recovers at rate \delta (permanent immunity). Key parameter: the ratio \tau = \beta / \delta.
  • SIS: same transmission, but the vertex becomes susceptible again at rate \delta — the epidemic can become endemic (survive indefinitely).
  • Discrete-time version (used in the labs): each edge uv with u infected transmits at each step with probability p = 1 - e^{-\beta}, each infected vertex recovers with probability 1 - e^{-\delta}.

On a homogeneous population (perfect mixing, no graph), the classical SIR gives the basic reproduction number R_0 = \beta S_0 / \delta and the threshold R_0 > 1 — this is the model of epidemiology courses; the graph replaces the mass term S_0.

Theorem 3.2 — Branching approximation: the threshold on G(n, c/n) On G(n, c/n) with a single initial infective, if c\,\frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} \leq 1, the epidemic reaches o(n) vertices. If c\,\frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} > 1, it reaches a strictly positive fraction of the vertices with strictly positive probability (a “massive” epidemic), and the final fraction is the size of the giant component of a G\big(n, c\frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta}/n\big) (cf. Theorem 3.4).

With \tau = \beta/\delta, \frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} = \frac{\tau}{1+\tau} \approx \tau when \tau is small, so the threshold is often written c\tau \approx 1.

Proof. (Sketch.) At the very beginning, infection attempts on distinct edges are independent exponential races: on an edge (u, v) with u infected, the infection of v wins the race against the recovery of u with probability \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}.

Each infective therefore has, to first approximation (no collisions — the targets are all distinct), an infectious offspring \mathrm{Bin}\big( \mathrm{Pois}(c), \frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} \big) \approx \mathrm{Pois}\big( c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta} \big) of mean R_0^{\text{graph}} = c\, \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta} = \frac{c \tau}{1 + \tau}, threshold R_0^{\text{graph}} = 1, i.e. \tau = \frac{1}{c - 1}, close to c\tau = 1 when c is large.

The subcritical branching extinguishes the epidemic after O(\log n) infectives; the supercritical one survives with positive probability, and then grows until it has seen a positive fraction of the n vertices. ∎

Theorem 3.3 (Ganesh, Massoulié, Towsley, INFOCOM 2005) — the spectral threshold of SIS For SIS on a fixed graph G with adjacency matrix A, spectral radius \rho(A):

  1. if \beta / \delta < 1 / \rho(A): the epidemic dies out in O(\log n) time with probability tending to 1 (fast extinction);
  2. conversely, as soon as \beta/\delta exceeds 1/\rho(A) by a factor depending on the geometry of the graph, the survival time is exponential in n: the epidemic becomes endemic. Later works (Chakrabarti et al., 2008) identify 1/\rho(A) as the threshold of the mean-field approximation: the epidemic threshold of a network is spectral.

Proof. (Proof of (i), sketch — spectral supermartingale.) Let u be the Perron–Frobenius vector of A (u > 0, A u = \rho u) and \Phi_t = \sum_i u_i x_i(t), where x_i(t) \in \{0, 1\} is the infection indicator.

Each infective i recovers at rate \delta, a drift -\delta u_i, and infects each neighbor j at rate \beta, a drift +\beta u_j.

Writing x for the state vector: \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathbb{E}[\Phi_t \mid x] \;\leq\; \sum_i x_i \big( -\delta u_i + \beta \sum_{j \sim i} u_j \big) = \big( \beta \rho(A) - \delta \big) \sum_i x_i u_i = \big( \beta \rho - \delta \big)\, \mathbb{E}[\Phi_t \mid x]. If \beta \rho < \delta, then \mathbb{E}[\Phi_t] \leq \Phi_0\, e^{-(\delta - \beta \rho) t}. Since \Phi_t = 0 as soon as the epidemic is extinct and \Phi_t \geq (\min_i u_i) \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{at least one infective}\}}, we get \mathbb{P}(\text{not extinct at } t) \;\leq\; \frac{\Phi_0}{\min_i u_i}\; e^{-(\delta - \beta \rho) t}, hence extinction in O(\log n) (the prefactor is polynomial in n). ∎

Theorem 3.4 — Final SIR = percolation: the final size is a giant component For SIR on G(n, c/n), the final size of the epidemic started from a random vertex has the same law as the size of the connected component of that vertex in the percolated graph G(n, c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}/n), where each edge is kept with probability \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta} (probability that transmission wins the race, cf. 3.2).

In particular: massive epidemic \iff giant component of the percolated graph — Theorem 1.3 applies again with parameter c \tau' = c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}, and the final fraction of the infected population is \rho solving \rho = 1 - e^{-c \tau' \rho}.

Proof. (Coupling.) Order the events: for each edge (u, v), the “transmission vs recovery” race can be drawn in advance, independently of everything else: mark the edge “open” with probability \frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} (transmission clock faster than the recovery clock, all independent). The strong Markov property of the process then shows that the final set of infectives is exactly the set of vertices reachable from the origin in the subgraph of open edges — the percolated connected component. This is the classic epidemic \leftrightarrow percolation bridge (Grassberger 1983; Newman 2002). ∎

Theorem 3.5 (Pastor-Satorras–Vespignani, 2001) — vanishing threshold on scale-free networks On a configuration graph whose degree distribution is p_k \sim C k^{-\gamma}:

  1. for SIR, the threshold on the transmissibility T = \beta/(\beta+\delta) of Theorem 3.4 is T_c = \frac{\mathbb{E}[D]}{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}, the reciprocal of the branching mean of Theorem 2.2; for SIS, the heterogeneous mean-field threshold of Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani is \tau_c = \mathbb{E}[D]/\mathbb{E}[D^2] on \tau = \beta/\delta;
  2. if 2 < \gamma \leq 3, \mathbb{E}[D^2] diverges with n: both thresholds tend to 0 — every contagion becomes epidemic on an infinite scale-free network.

Engineering reading: vaccinating at random does not change the divergence; targeting hubs (“acquaintance” or degree-based vaccination) reintroduces a positive threshold. This is the quantitative argument behind the moderation of very high-audience accounts on platforms (and behind targeted vaccination strategies).

From model to data: R_0 in practice Estimating \beta, \delta or R_0 from cascade data (retweet timestamps): maximum likelihood on infection times (self-exciting Hawkes processes, not taught on this site; Refresher Statistics, ch. 5 for the likelihood); confidence intervals by bootstrap. Lab 2 of ch. 8 runs the reverse experiment: simulate, measure the final size, and check the thresholds 3.2 and 3.5 on ER vs Barabási–Albert.

4. Rumors, push-pull and influence

Spreading information to n machines without a directory or a coordinator: the gossip protocol is the elegant solution — each node calls a random neighbor every round. It is the building block of rumors (Karp et al. 2000), of distributed aggregation (ch. 5) and the distant ancestor of blockchain blocks disseminated by “flooding”. The quantitative question: how many rounds until everyone is informed?

Definition 4.1 — Push, pull, push-pull protocols (telephone call model) Discrete time; at each round, each node chooses an interlocutor uniformly at random among the other n - 1 (complete graph):

  • push: informed nodes call to transmit;
  • pull: uninformed nodes call to ask;
  • push-pull: both at once — the call transmits as soon as either side knows.

Theorem 4.2 (Karp, Schindelhauer, Shenker, Vöcking, FOCS 2000) On the complete graph K_n, the push protocol informs all n nodes in \log_2 n + \ln n + O(1) \quad \text{rounds with high probability} (Frieze–Grimmett 1985; Pittel 1987), and the push-pull protocol in \log_3 n + O(\log \log n) \quad \text{rounds,} while a variant with an age-based stopping rule sends only O(n \log \log n) messages in total (Karp et al.).

Proof. (Sketch in three phases — where the constants come from.) Let k_t be the number of informed nodes and s_t = n - k_t the ignorant ones.

  1. Doubling (k \leq n/2): the term \log_2 n. Each informed node pushes to a uniform target; the probability of hitting an uninformed node is 1 - k_t/n, close to 1 while k_t = o(n), and collisions between two pushes are rare, so k_{t+1} \approx 2k_t : the informed set doubles at each round, hence \log_2 n + O(1) rounds to go from 1 to n/2 (the ratio stays \geq 3/2 up to n/2, and the deviation from the expectation is controlled by a supermartingale).

  2. Exponential decay of ignorance (k \geq n/2): the term \ln n. An ignorant node survives a push round if none of the k_t informed nodes has called it: \mathbb{P}(\text{survival}) = (1 - \frac{1}{n})^{k_t} \approx e^{-k_t/n}. When k_t = n - s_t \approx n, each ignorant node survives with probability \approx e^{-1} per round: s_t decays geometrically with ratio \approx e^{-1}.

    It takes \ln n rounds to go from \Theta(n) ignorants down to the last ones — the signature of the coupon collector (about n \ln n uniform draws are needed to see every item): each round “covers” k_t random vertices, and covering all n vertices takes \ln n rounds.

  3. The role of pull. Pull changes both phases. At the start, each informed node pushes once and is called on average once by an ignorant node, so k roughly triples per round: \log_3 n rounds instead of \log_2 n.

    At the end, an ignorant node stays ignorant only if nobody pushes to it and its own call fails: probability \approx e^{-1} \cdot s_t/n, so the ignorant fraction is roughly squared at each round, s_{t+1}/n \lesssim (s_t/n)^2, and the last phase lasts O(\log \log n) rounds instead of \ln n. The technical difficulty of the paper — beyond this sketch — is controlling the deviations of all phases simultaneously.

∎

General graphs, and communication costs On a connected graph of maximum degree \Delta and diameter D, push informs everyone in O(\Delta (D + \log n)) rounds with high probability (Feige, Peleg, Raghavan, Upfal, 1990) — the information must cross the bottlenecks; on the random graphs of ch. 1–2 above the connectivity threshold, \Theta(\log n) rounds suffice.

Karp et al. also show that push-pull can be made message-efficient — O(n \log \log n) transmissions with an age-based stopping rule, whereas plain push sends of the order of n \log n — hence its use in peer-to-peer systems (ch. 5). Modern variants (pull with lists of new messages, protocols of Doerr et al.) achieve the same speed with robustness to failures.

Definition 4.3 — Influence maximization (Kempe, Kleinberg, Tardos, 2003) Independent cascade (IC) model: each edge (u,v) is “active” independently with probability p_{uv}; content starting from set S propagates along active edges (once per edge). The influence function is \sigma(S) = \mathbb{E}[|R(S)|], the expected size of the set reachable from S.

Problem: choose S of cardinality \leq k maximizing \sigma(S) — what “seeding” budget for a viral campaign? (The linear threshold model — each vertex activates when the sum of weights of active neighbors exceeds a random threshold — is the classic alternative, with the same complexity.)

Theorem 4.4 — \sigma is submodular, and greedy is (1 - 1/e)-optimal

  1. \sigma is monotone (S \subseteq T \Rightarrow \sigma(S) \leq \sigma(T)) and submodular — marginal returns decrease: for S \subseteq T, \sigma(S \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(S) \geq \sigma(T \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(T);
  2. the greedy algorithm (iteratively add the vertex of maximal marginal gain) returns S_g such that \sigma(S_g) \;\geq\; \Big( 1 - \frac{1}{e} \Big) \sigma(S^\ast), where S^\ast is the optimum (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher bound, 1978);
  3. the exact optimization problem is NP-hard (reduction from Set Cover); achieving (1 - 1/e + \varepsilon) for any \varepsilon > 0 is NP-hard as well.

Proof. Submodularity (live-edge coupling). Draw in advance, once and for all, the “live/dead” state of each edge (coupling of Theorem 3.4); in this world, R(S) is the set of vertices reachable from S in the live subgraph, and reachability is clearly monotone and submodular.

Indeed, take a vertex w reachable from S \cup \{v\} but not from S.

Every live path from S \cup \{v\} to w then goes through v, so w is reachable from \{v\}; hence R(S \cup \{v\}) = R(S) \cup R(\{v\}), and the marginal gain |R(S \cup \{v\}) \setminus R(S)| = |R(\{v\}) \setminus R(S)| can only shrink when S grows to T \supseteq S. Taking the expectation over the live-edge worlds preserves the inequality. \square

Greedy. Let S_i be the greedy set after i steps. By monotonicity, then submodularity, \sigma(S^\ast) \leq \sigma(S_i \cup S^\ast) \leq \sigma(S_i) + \sum_{v \in S^\ast} \big(\sigma(S_i \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(S_i)\big) \leq \sigma(S_i) + k\,\big(\sigma(S_{i+1}) - \sigma(S_i)\big), since greedy adds the vertex of largest marginal gain. Rearranged: \sigma(S^\ast) - \sigma(S_{i+1}) \leq (1 - 1/k)\big(\sigma(S^\ast) - \sigma(S_i)\big). Iterating k times from \sigma(\emptyset) = 0 gives \sigma(S^\ast) - \sigma(S_g) \leq (1 - 1/k)^k \sigma(S^\ast) \leq e^{-1} \sigma(S^\ast). ∎

From theory to the platform Monte-Carlo estimators of \sigma are costly (10,000 simulations per candidate); the CELF trick (Leskovec et al., 2007) divides the cost by ~700 by exploiting the decrease of marginal gains to evaluate lazily. Applications: viral marketing (choice of seeders), early detection of contagions (sensors), vaccination (ch. 3). The dark symmetry: the same algorithms optimize disinformation — hence the ongoing research on inoculation (pre-bunking, “curative” bots) and models of competition between cascades.

5. Large-scale distributed systems

Behind every feed: millions of requests per second, caches everywhere, load balancers. These systems consume exactly the same mathematics as the previous chapters — balls into bins, Poisson processes, random walks — and part of the advances come from the French school (Massoulié, ch. 9). This chapter gives its three building blocks: load balancing, caching, and resource sharing.

Theorem 5.1 — Balls into bins: the power of two choices Throw n balls independently into n bins uniformly: the maximum load is \frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n}\big( 1 + o(1) \big) with high probability (Azar, Broder, Karlin, Upfal, 1999; Mitzenmacher, 2001). If each ball chooses two bins at random and goes to the less loaded one, the maximum load drops to

Key result — the power of two choices \frac{\ln \ln n}{\ln 2} + O(1) \approx \log_2 \log_2 n + O(1).

This is an exponential gain for twice the information.

Proof. (Sketch — density differential equations, the mean-field method.) Let u_j(t) be the fraction of bins of load at least j after t balls.

One choice: a ball lands on a bin of load j-1 with probability u_{j-1} - u_j (first time at level j), so \frac{\mathrm{d} u_j}{\mathrm{d} t} \approx u_{j-1} - u_j . Hence, after t = 1 unit of time (n balls), u_j \approx 1/j! — and 1/j! falls to 1/n (a single bin) for j \approx \frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n}.

Two choices: the ball rises to level j only if both sampled bins are at least j - 1: \frac{\mathrm{d} u_j}{\mathrm{d} t} \approx u_{j-1}^2 - u_j^2 ; so u_j \lesssim u_{j-1}^2: the fraction is squared at each level.

Starting from a level j_0 where u_{j_0} \leq 1/2, after k more levels u_{j_0 + k} \leq 2^{-2^{k}}, which falls below 1/n as soon as 2^{k} \geq \log_2 n, i.e. k \geq \log_2 \log_2 n. ∎

From the model to the data center The “supermarket” model (queues + choice of the least loaded) is the modeling of load balancers: at load \rho < 1, the typical waiting delay goes from \Theta\big(\frac{1}{1 - \rho}\big) (one choice) to \Theta\big(\log \frac{1}{1 - \rho}\big) (two choices) — the tail behavior changes scale (Mitzenmacher; the JOIN-IDLE-QUEUE regime handles giant systems, the “power-of-d choices” variant with d > 2). The same idea serves hash tables (cuckoo hashing); the book by Mitzenmacher and Upfal covers it all.

Definition 5.2 — Caching: LRU, LFU, and the hierarchy of contents A cache of capacity k stores k objects; requests arrive according to a process (ideally Poisson), object i with probability \lambda_i (popularity, typically power law — ch. 2); a hit if the object is in the cache, a miss otherwise (cost: fetching it from further away).

Classical policies:

  • LRU (evict the least recently used — Che’s approximation: the “characteristic time” t_C gives \mathbb{P}(\text{hit}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda_i t_C});
  • LFU (evict the least frequently requested);
  • random and FIFO.

Structural result: against an online adversary, LRU is k-competitive — its cost is at most k times that of the offline optimum (Sleator–Tarjan); under i.i.d. popularities, Che’s approximation makes it analytic. The distributed question: who caches what, when users serve one another — work of the team in ch. 9.

5.3 — Bandwidth sharing and proportional fairness (Massoulié–Roberts, 1999-2002) Flows share links; x_r = rate of flow r, capacity constraints A x \leq c. \alpha-fairness objectives: maximize \sum_r \frac{x_r^{1 - \alpha}}{1 - \alpha}; \alpha = 1 (limit) gives proportional fairness \max \sum_r \log x_r, \alpha \to \infty gives max-min.

The theorem of Massoulié and Roberts (INFOCOM 1999 / IEEE-ACM ToN 2002): networks stabilized by simple distributed algorithms (AIMD window à la TCP, load evolving at the RTT timescale) converge to the proportionally fair point — a bridge between the mechanics of protocols and convex optimization: “TCP does decentralized optimization without knowing it”. This framework founded a whole literature (> 900 citations) on the stability of quality-of-service networks.

Gossip and distributed learning — the modern bridge The push-pull protocols of ch. 4 also compute aggregates: the mean of a field of values by random-walk iterations (asymptotic consensus), approximate quantiles, distinct counting (HyperLogLog). The revival: asynchronous SGD on graphs — workers exchange gradients via gossip instead of a central server; convergence guarantees depend on the spectral gap of the communication graph (recent work of the team, ch. 9). For an LLGA master: federated learning is a constrained gossip protocol — the module’s theory applies to it directly.

6. The stochastic block model

Communities: groups of vertices better connected within themselves than between each other. The stochastic block model (SBM) is its canonical generative model — the equivalent, for graphs, of Gaussians for point clouds. It makes it possible to ask the right questions: when is the block structure detectable? Which algorithm achieves it? The answers involve exact thresholds — two of which are due to the module’s instructor (ch. 9).

Definition 6.1 — SBM Parameters: n vertices, q blocks, proportions (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_q), connectivity matrix P = (p_{ab}) \in [0, 1]^{q \times q}.

Generation: each vertex receives a block \sigma(v) \sim \pi independently; each pair (u, v) is connected with probability p_{\sigma(u)\sigma(v)}, independently.

The textbook case of “two symmetric blocks”: q = 2, \pi = (1/2, 1/2), p_{\mathrm{in}} = a/n, p_{\mathrm{out}} = b/n (two communities of mean degree \frac{a+b}{2}, all the more separated as a > b). The SBM with heterogeneous degrees within blocks (DC-SBM, Karrer–Newman 2011) corrects the bias “hubs become blocks”.

Definition 6.2 — The three difficulty regimes

  • Detection (sparse, degrees O(1)): a partition \hat\sigma better correlated with \sigma than chance (more than 50% of correct pairs in q = 2). The realistic regime for large networks.
  • Partial recovery: error fraction \to 0 asymptotically.
  • Exact recovery (dense, degrees \Theta(\log n)): \mathbb{P}(\hat\sigma = \sigma) \to 1. For two symmetric blocks with p_{\mathrm{in}} = a \log n/n, p_{\mathrm{out}} = b \log n / n: the exact threshold is \sqrt a - \sqrt b > \sqrt 2 (Abbe, Bandeira, Hall; Mossel, Neeman, Sly): above it, the maximum likelihood — NP-hard in general — is achievable there by semidefinite relaxation.

Theorem 6.3 — Kesten–Stigum and detection thresholds (statements) For two symmetric blocks (a, b)/n:

  1. Impossibility below KS. If (a - b)^2 \leq 2(a + b), no method does better than chance (the model is “contiguous” to Erdős–Rényi of degree \frac{a+b}{2}) — conjectured by Decelle, Krzakala, Moore, Zdeborová (2011); impossibility is proved by Mossel, Neeman, Sly.
  2. Feasibility above. If (a-b)^2 > 2(a+b), detection is possible — this is Massoulié’s theorem (STOC 2014, ch. 9): random graphs there satisfy the weak Ramanujan property, and a non-backtracking spectral algorithm detects.

The quantity \frac{(a-b)^2}{2(a+b)} plays the role of a signal-to-noise ratio; the threshold is where information survives the spectral noise of the degree.

Definition 6.4 — The non-backtracking (Hashimoto) matrix Index the oriented edges (u \to v) of the graph (there are 2m of them). The non-backtracking matrix B \in \{0,1\}^{2m \times 2m} is defined by B_{(u \to v),\, (v \to w)} = \mathbf{1}\{ w \neq u \}, \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise: a path that does not retrace its steps.} B encodes non-backtracking walks: the power B^r counts walks without U-turns of length r.

Connection with the course: it is the companion operator of the adjacency A, insensitive to the “degree noise” that pollutes A — a walk without U-turns cannot go back and forth on a hub.

Theorem 6.5 (Bordenave, Lelarge, Massoulié, FOCS 2015 / Ann. Probab. 2018) — non-backtracking spectrum of the SBM For the SBM (a, b)/n and its extension to q blocks, with high probability:

  1. the spectrum of B consists of a bulk contained in the disk of radius \sqrt{d} (where d is the mean degree), plus real eigenvalues carrying information;
  2. an informative eigenvalue escapes the bulk (|\lambda| > \sqrt d) if and only if the Kesten–Stigum threshold is crossed;
  3. consequently, the non-backtracking spectral algorithm — compute the leading eigenvectors of B, run k-means on them — detects the blocks all the way down to the Kesten–Stigum threshold; the same matrix proves that random graphs are asymptotically “non-regular Ramanujan”.

Why A fails where B succeeds: in the \log-free regime (degrees O(1)), the highest degree is \Theta(\frac{\log n}{\log \log n}) and creates parasitic eigenvalues of the same order as the signal; the operator B precisely cancels the contribution of these local back-and-forths. This is the theoretical answer to the practical problem of spectral clustering on sparse networks with hubs (Graph ML module, ch. 4; Machine Learning module, ch. 7).

Example 6.6 — MLE and the EM connection Write the log-likelihood of the SBM for a candidate partition \sigma (latent blocks unknown): \ell(\sigma, P) = \sum_{r \leq s} \Big[ m_{rs}(\sigma) \log p_{rs} + \big(N_{rs}(\sigma) - m_{rs}(\sigma)\big) \log(1 - p_{rs}) \Big], where m_{rs}(\sigma) counts the edges between blocks r and s and N_{rs}(\sigma) the pairs of vertices between them; the MLE is the partition (with its P) maximizing \ell — a combinatorial problem (discretization of cuts).

EM (Machine Learning, ch. 7) approaches this MLE in its variational version, since the exact posterior over partitions does not factorize: the E step computes mean-field block responsibilities per vertex, the M step re-estimates P and \pi, and a spectral initialization (6.5) helps escape saddle points. This is the pipeline of the graph-tool / sbm libraries.

7. Community detection and applications

The SBM says when communities can be detected; practitioners also need an algorithm that runs on millions of vertices without knowing the model. This chapter presents the most used one, modularity maximization, compares it with SBM inference, and reviews what community structure is used for.

Definition 7.1 — Modularity (Newman–Girvan, 2004) For a partition \mathcal{C} of a graph G with m edges, modularity compares the observed internal density with that expected under the configuration model (ch. 2) — the null model “same degrees, edges at random”:

Key result — modularity Q(\mathcal{C}) = \sum_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \Big( \frac{e_C}{m} - \Big( \frac{d_C}{2m} \Big)^2 \Big).

Here e_C counts the internal edges of block C and d_C the sum of the degrees of its vertices. Maximizing Q = finding the partition most “over-connected” relative to the null.

This is the objective function of Louvain (Blondel et al., 2008) — the industrial reference algorithm, O(m) per pass, deployed in networkx, Neo4j, Gephi.

Modularity vs SBM — the great debate

  1. Resolution limit (Fortunato–Barthélemy, 2007): two communities joined by a single edge are merged by modularity maximization as soon as each has fewer than about \sqrt{m/2} internal edges, even when both are cliques. Read the other way, a community found with fewer than about \sqrt{2m} internal edges may hide smaller ones.

    Example from the paper: a ring of 30 five-node cliques joined by single edges has m = 330; each clique has 10 internal edges, fewer than \sqrt{165} \approx 12.8, and pairing the cliques raises Q from 0.876 to 0.888. Statistical inference of a flat SBM has a resolution limit of its own, milder; the nested SBM of Peixoto (2014) is designed to avoid it.

  2. Sparse overfitting: in the O(1) degree regime, maximizing Q finds communities even in pure Erdős–Rényi — the bias of modularity on sparse graphs is well documented (Peixoto; Newman); Bayesian inference of the SBM penalizes complexity and resists.

  3. Two philosophies: modularity is descriptive (an objective heuristic, no model); the SBM is generative (a model, hence tests, thresholds, intervals — ch. 6). In practice: Louvain/Leiden for scale, SBM for statistics.

  4. The lesson of Leskovec et al. (2009): in large real graphs, “natural” communities are small (up to ~100 vertices) and drowned in a dense core without structure — it is core-periphery, not partitioning, that dominates at large scale.

7.2 — Applications: virality, alignment, privacy

  • Virality. Predicting the final size of a cascade (retweets, shares): early features (depth, width, timing of the first shares — Cheng et al., WWW 2016) and Hawkes models; the connection to the course: a massive cascade \Leftrightarrow percolated giant component (3.4), and the block structure modulates \sigma(S) (4.3-4.4) — spreading between two communities costs the b edges, spreading inside benefits from the a edges.
  • Graph alignment and privacy. Re-identifying users across two platforms (anonymized): the correlated graph alignment problem — partial recovery guarantees by Hall and Massoulié (ch. 9). GDPR reading: “anonymizing” a social graph is mathematically fragile; pseudonymization is not anonymization.
  • Echo chambers. The homophilous SBM (a > b) is the minimal model of polarization; recommendation policies (Graph ML module, ch. 6 — link prediction) that optimize engagement decrease b and deepen polarization — a quantitative bridge between algorithm and society.
  • Anomaly detection. Blocks = normal behaviors; misclassified vertices (low likelihood under a fitted SBM) are the bot/spam candidates — the individual log-likelihood of Example 6.6 serves as a score.

# Lab 2 -- discrete-time SIR: does the threshold depend on the degree law?
def sir(G, p, patient0):
    """Discrete SIR: infection p per edge per step, recovery in 1 step.
    Returns the final size of the epidemic."""
    inf, seen = {patient0}, {patient0}
    while inf:
        new = set()
        for u in inf:
            new |= {v for v in G[u] if v not in seen and rng.random() < p}
        seen |= new
        inf = new
    return len(seen)

def outbreak(G, p, trials=200):
    n = G.number_of_nodes()
    sizes = [sir(G, p, int(rng.integers(n))) for _ in range(trials)]
    return np.mean(sizes) / n            # final fraction of the population

for tau in (0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.2, 2.0):   # tau = beta/delta; recovery in 1 step,
    p = 1 - np.exp(-tau)                 # infection per edge per step
    print(f"tau={tau:4.1f}  ER {outbreak(G_er, p):.3f}   BA {outbreak(G_ba, p):.3f}")

# Lab 3 -- SBM: spectral detection (adjacency in dense, non-backtracking in sparse)

9. Course research notes (Massoulié, Inria)

The module is taught by Laurent Massoulié, Inria senior researcher (Dragon team, Inria Paris center; he headed the joint Microsoft Research–Inria center and teaches at École Polytechnique). His career covers exactly the syllabus: epidemics and networks, rumors, stochastic block model, and distributed systems (caching, load balancing, decentralized optimization). Five axes, with the founding papers to read as a project (exercise 12).

9.1 — Epidemics and topology (Ganesh, Massoulié, Towsley, INFOCOM 2005) The Effect of Network Topology on the Spread of Epidemics (> 1,000 citations) founded epidemic control through structure: Theorem 3.3 of the module is its core — fast extinction as soon as \beta/\delta < 1/\rho(A), proved via the spectral supermartingale we sketched, and long survival in the supercritical regime. Legacy: the entire literature on spectral epidemic thresholds (NIMFA, targeted vaccination), and malware/worm slowdown policies. Position in the course: it is the quantitative answer to “does the shape of the network change the epidemic?” — yes, spectrally.

9.2 — The SBM detection threshold (Massoulié, STOC 2014) Community Detection Thresholds and the Weak Ramanujan Property. Result: above the Kesten–Stigum threshold, random graphs “resemble” Ramanujan graphs (their nontrivial eigenvalues lie in the disk of radius \sqrt d) — the weak Ramanujan property — and a spectral algorithm exploits it to detect communities down to the information threshold. This is the first proof of the conjecture of Decelle et al. on the feasibility side (independently of Mossel–Neeman–Sly), and the answer to question 6.3 of the module. Legacy: the founding slab of detection below the threshold, extended by 9.3.

9.3 — The non-backtracking spectrum (Bordenave, Lelarge, Massoulié, FOCS 2015 / Ann. Probab. 2018) Non-backtracking Spectrum of Random Graphs: Community Detection and Non-regular Ramanujan Graphs. Theorem 6.5 of the module: the spectrum of the non-backtracking matrix of an SBM is confined in a disk of radius \sqrt d — bulk — except for the informative eigenvalues, which escape it exactly at the Kesten–Stigum threshold.

Double payoff: (i) the “eigenvectors of B + k-means” algorithm detects down to the threshold (in practice: it is the one of Lab 3, ch. 8); (ii) random graphs (even irregular ones) are asymptotically Ramanujan — a pure graph theory result that closes an open question.

It also justifies non-backtracking operators in learning on graphs: Graph ML, 9.2 uses them for SBM detection.

9.4 — Distributed systems: caching, balancing, bandwidth Three works connected to ch. 5:

  • Bandwidth sharing — Massoulié, Roberts (INFOCOM 1999; IEEE/ACM ToN 2002): convergence of decentralized protocols (TCP-AIMD) to proportional fairness (5.3), the foundation of network stability — ~1,000 citations.
  • Distributed caching over heterogeneous mobile networks — Ioannidis, Massoulié, Chaintreau (SIGMETRICS 2010; Queueing Systems 2012): users cache contents and exchange them when they meet; which decentralized policy maximizes throughput? The model is that of the epidemics of ch. 3 — a usable rumor to save bandwidth.
  • Bipartite graph structures for efficient balancing of heterogeneous loads — Leconte, Lelarge, Massoulié (ACM, 2012): balancing heterogeneous loads as a matching on a random bipartite graph — the bridge between power-of-two-choices (5.1) and graph structures (ch. 2).

9.5 — Today: graph alignment and distributed learning

  • Graph alignment — Hall, Massoulié, Partial Recovery in the Graph Alignment Problem (Operations Research 71(1), 2023): two correlated graphs (two platforms), can we recover the vertex correspondence? Partial recovery guarantees depending on sparsity and correlation — the theory of de-anonymization (ch. 7), with the privacy limit that goes with it.
  • Asynchronous SGD on graphs — Even, Koloskova, Massoulié, Asynchronous SGD on Graphs (AISTATS 2024): a unified framework for decentralized and federated optimization via gossip — chapter 5 of the module projected onto today’s federated learning.
  • Collective exploration — Cosson, Massoulié, Collective Tree Exploration via Potential Function Method (ITCS 2024): cooperating robots explore an unknown tree — distributed algorithms viewed through online analysis.

Guiding thread (for the exam) A single pattern runs through the whole body of work: take a system (network, platform, swarm), model it by a random process on a graph, identify a spectral or branching threshold, and derive from it a decentralized algorithm with guarantees. This is exactly the backbone of chapters 1 to 7 of this module — teaching one’s own research field.

Q1 (5 pts) — A giant component with prescribed degrees. A configuration graph (Definition 2.1) has half of its vertices of degree 1 and half of degree 3.

  1. Check the Molloy–Reed criterion of Theorem 2.2. (b) Give the excess-degree law and the mean of the exploration branching. (c) Using Definition 0.2, compute the extinction probability u of a branch, then the fraction of vertices in the giant component.

Proof. Marking points

  • \mathbb{E}[D] = 2 and \mathbb{E}[D^2] = 5 > 2\,\mathbb{E}[D] = 4, equivalently \sum_k k(k - 2)\, p_k = -0.5 + 1.5 = 1 > 0: a giant component exists (1 pt).
  • Excess law \tilde p_0 = 1 \cdot p_1 / 2 = 0.25 and \tilde p_2 = 3 \cdot p_3 / 2 = 0.75, with mean 2 \times 0.75 = 1.5 = \mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]/\mathbb{E}[D] (1.5 pts).
  • u is the smallest root in [0, 1] of u = 0.25 + 0.75\,u^2, that is of 3u^2 - 4u + 1 = 0, so u = 1/3 (1.5 pts). Common error: taking the root u = 1, which is the extinction probability only below the threshold.
  • The root explores with the degree law itself, so the giant fraction is (1 pt) 1 - \big(\tfrac12 u + \tfrac12 u^3\big) = 1 - \tfrac{10}{54} = \tfrac{22}{27} \approx 0.81 .

Q2 (5 pts) — An epidemic and a vaccination threshold. SIR runs on G(n, 4/n) with \beta = \delta = 1.

  1. Is the epidemic massive (Theorem 3.2)? (b) Give the equation of its final size (Theorem 3.4) and solve it to two decimals. (c) Which fraction of randomly chosen vertices must be vaccinated beforehand to stop massive outbreaks? (d) Why does the same strategy fail on a scale-free graph with 2 < \gamma \leq 3, and what works instead?

Proof. Marking points

  • Transmissibility T = \beta/(\beta + \delta) = 1/2 and cT = 2 > 1: yes, with positive probability (1 pt).
  • Percolated graph G(n, 2/n): the final fraction solves \rho = 1 - e^{-2\rho}, \rho \approx 0.80, by a few fixed-point iterations or by bisection (1.5 pts).
  • Removing a fraction v of the vertices at random multiplies the branching mean by 1 - v; outbreaks stop when 2(1 - v) \leq 1, so v \geq 1/2 (1.5 pts). Common error: applying 1/R_0 to the infection rate instead of the vertices.
  • With 2 < \gamma \leq 3, \mathbb{E}[D^2] diverges, the threshold of Theorem 3.5 tends to 0, and thinning at random leaves it at 0; targeting hubs, by degree or by acquaintance, restores a positive threshold (1 pt).

Q3 (4 pts) — Rumour spreading at scale. A rumour starts at one node of the complete graph on n = 10^6 nodes.

  1. Using Theorem 4.2, estimate the number of rounds for push and for push-pull. (b) Explain where the term \ln n of push comes from, and why pull removes it.

Proof. Marking points

  • Push: \log_2 n + \ln n \approx 19.9 + 13.8 \approx 34 rounds; push-pull: \log_3 n \approx 12.6, plus a term of order \log \log n (2 pts).
  • Once half the nodes know, an ignorant node stays ignorant in a push round with probability about e^{-1}, so ignorance decays geometrically and takes about \ln n rounds to vanish, as in a coupon collector (1 pt).
  • With pull, the ignorant node also calls and succeeds with probability close to 1, so the ignorant fraction is roughly squared at each round, and the last phase lasts O(\log \log n) rounds (1 pt). Common error: attributing the gain to the start, where pull alone cannot leave a single informed node.

Q4 (6 pts) — Detecting two communities. Two symmetric blocks have intra- and inter-block edge probabilities a/n and b/n (Definition 6.1).

  1. Is detection possible for (a, b) = (5, 1), and for (a, b) = (4, 2) (Theorem 6.3)? (b) Both graphs have mean degree 3. The informative eigenvalues of the non-backtracking matrix are (a + b)/2 and (a - b)/2; compare them with the bulk radius of Theorem 6.5 and conclude. (c) Compute the modularity (Definition 7.1) of the planted partition in expectation, for large n, and say why a positive modularity proves nothing on a sparse graph.

Proof. Marking points

  • (5, 1): (a - b)^2 = 16 > 2(a + b) = 12, detectable; (4, 2): 4 \leq 12, impossible (1.5 pts).
  • The bulk radius is \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73. For (5, 1) the second eigenvalue, 2, escapes it; for (4, 2) it equals 1 and stays hidden (1.5 pts). This is the same criterion as in (a): \frac{a - b}{2} > \sqrt{\frac{a + b}{2}} \iff (a - b)^2 > 2(a + b).
  • Each block has about na/8 internal edges, m \approx n(a + b)/4, and each block carries half of the total degree (2 pts), so Q \approx 2\Big(\frac{a}{2(a + b)} - \frac14\Big) = \frac{a - b}{2(a + b)}, \qquad \text{that is } 1/3 \text{ for } (5, 1).
  • Maximizing Q also finds partitions of positive modularity in a pure Erdős–Rényi graph of the same degree (sparse overfitting, ch. 7), so only a test against a null model or an SBM fit can conclude (1 pt). Common error: reading Q > 0.3 as proof of structure.
