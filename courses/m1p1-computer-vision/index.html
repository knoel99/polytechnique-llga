0. Review: linear algebra, convolution, probability

L3 review material used

  • Linear algebra: eigenvalues, eigenvectors, orthogonal matrices (R^\top R = I, they preserve norms), trace (\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)), and singular value decomposition (SVD);
  • Differential calculus: partial derivatives, gradient, Taylor expansions to order 1 and 2, chain rule;
  • Convolution and Fourier transform (see the Signal Processing module, chapters 2 and 5): 1D convolution, convolution theorem, Shannon theorem; we extend them to two dimensions;
  • Probability: expectation, covariance, Gaussians, independence; least squares method and normal equations (see the Machine Learning module, chapter 2).

Theorem 0.1 — SVD and best rank-k approximation Every real matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n} can be written A = U \Sigma V^\top with U, V orthogonal and \Sigma diagonal with entries \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq 0. Moreover, the best approximation of A in the Frobenius sense by a matrix of rank \leq k is \sum_{i\leq k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top, with error \big(\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2\big)^{1/2} (Eckart-Young theorem).

Proof. Sketch. The singular vectors are the principal axes of AA^\top and A^\top A: the \sigma_i^2 are their eigenvalues. The approximation follows from the fact that matrices of rank \leq k form a closed convex set and that the error decomposes orthogonally: \|A - X\|_F^2 = \sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2 + \|X_k - \sum_{i \leq k}\sigma_i u_iv_i^\top\|_F^2. The SVD will be our permanent tool: least squares (DLT of ch. 5), decomposition of homographies, 3D orientation (Kabsch in ch. 10), surface normals.

Proposition 0.2 — Sums of independent Gaussians If X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2) and Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2) are independent, then X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X+\mu_Y, \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2). One-line proof via characteristic functions: e^{it\mu_X - t^2\sigma_X^2/2}\cdot e^{it\mu_Y - t^2\sigma_Y^2/2} = e^{it(\mu_X+\mu_Y) - t^2(\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2)/2}. ∎ Immediate application: convolving an image with a Gaussian kernel amounts to modeling an additive Gaussian noise — hence the central role of the Gaussian filter in ch. 2-4.

1. Image formation: camera and sampling

Concrete scene: a 12 MP smartphone photo, you downsample ×4 with no anti-aliasing filter — moiré on grids, aliasing on text. This is not “the image looks ugly”: it is the formation model (projection + sampling) setting hard limits. This chapter links pixels to geometry before Canny, SIFT and CNNs.

What breaks Ignoring downsampling (aliasing); calibrating a camera on poorly measured points; treating a pixel as a metric measurement without a camera model. All three sabotage a stereo pipeline as badly as a poorly preprocessed CNN detector.

A digital image is the discretization of a projection of the 3D world onto a plane. Understanding the formation model is indispensable: it is what connects the pixels to geometry (ch. 5-6), and what imposes the limits of sampling (aliasing).

Definition 1.1 — Pinhole model and homogeneous coordinates The pinhole projects a 3D point X = (X_1, X_2, X_3)^\top onto the image plane Z = f along a straight line through the optical center: x = f\,X_1/X_3, y = f\,X_2/X_3. In homogeneous coordinates \tilde{x} = (x, y, 1)^\top \sim (f X_1, f X_2, X_3)^\top, the projection becomes linear: \lambda\,\tilde{u} = K\,[\,R \mid t\,]\,\tilde{X},\qquad K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, with R \in SO(3) the orientation, t the position, K the intrinsic parameters (focal length in pixels f_x = f/dx, principal point (c_x, c_y), skew s \approx 0). The 3\times4 matrix P = K[R\mid t] is the projection matrix; the equality is up to a factor \lambda — all of projective geometry follows from this scale indeterminacy.

Theorem 1.2 — Projective projection preserves alignment The image by a pinhole camera of a line in space is a line (never a curve), and incidence is preserved.

Proof. A 3D line is \{X_0 + \lambda v\}; its images are P\tilde{X_0} + \lambda P\tilde{v}, that is, the vector plane of \mathbb{R}^3 spanned by P\tilde{X_0} and P\tilde{v} (provided they are independent); now the projective points belonging to a common 2-dimensional vector plane form precisely a projective line of the image plane \mathbb{P}^2. ∎

Practical consequence: rectangles do not remain rectangles (except in the frontal case), circles become ellipses, length ratios are not preserved — but alignment is. This is what makes the epipolar geometry of ch. 6 possible.

Example 1.3 — Numbers: field of view and focusing Sensor 4000×3000 pixels of 3.45 µm (physical width w = 4000 \times 3.45\,\text{µm} = 13.8 mm), lens of focal length f = 50 mm: \mathrm{FOV} = 2\arctan\frac{w/2}{f} = 2\arctan\frac{6.9}{50} = 2 \times 7.86^\circ \approx 15.7^\circ. The same optics on a “full frame” 36 mm sensor would give 2\arctan(18/50) \approx 39.6^\circ: at equal focal length, the field depends on the sensor size — hence the use of 35 mm-equivalent focal lengths. In pixels, f_x = 0.05/3.45\times10^{-3} \approx 14\,490 px.

Definition 1.4 — Sampling, folding, aliasing Discretizing an image = sampling a 2D signal: the Shannon theorem (see the Signal Processing module, ch. 4) requires a step \leq 1/(2\nu_{max}) in each direction. A real sensor cannot satisfy Shannon perfectly: frequencies beyond the Nyquist frequency (half the sampling frequency) fold over (aliasing) — moiré on fabrics, wheels turning backwards. Sensors therefore combine an optical low-pass filter (the famed “OLPF”) and the binning of photosites; software antialiasing does the same job in image synthesis.

Definition 1.5 — Color: Bayer mosaic and spaces Most sensors place behind each photosite a red, green, or blue filter according to the Bayer mosaic (2 greens for 1 red and 1 blue, human sensitivity to green dominating); demosaicing interpolates the two missing components per pixel. Color is then handled in adapted spaces: RGB for sensors, YUV/YCbCr for compression (full-resolution luminance, chrominance subsampled 4:2:0), HSV/Lab for segmentation (ch. 8: separating hue from luminance stabilizes the k-means).

Note — Distortions of real lenses The pinhole is an idealization; real optics add a radial distortion r \mapsto r(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4) (barrel or pincushion) and a tangential distortion. Calibration (checkerboard, Zhang 2000) estimates K and the coefficients k_i, and the image is rectified before any geometric measurement: otherwise the homographies of ch. 5 lose several pixels at the edge of the field.

2. Filtering and convolution

Filtering an image means replacing each pixel with a combination of its neighborhood: smoothing noise, sharpening details, detecting variations. The universal tool is convolution, already met in 1D in the Signal Processing module (ch. 2): everything that follows is its 2D translation.

Definition 2.1 — 2D convolution and correlation For an image I and a kernel K with finite support: (I \* K)(x, y) = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} I(x-i,\,y-j)\,K(i, j). Convolution flips the kernel; correlation \sum I(x+i, y+j) K(i,j) does not — for symmetric kernels (Gaussian, box), the two coincide. A convolutional filter is linear and translation-invariant: these are exactly the LTI systems of signal processing, and the convolution theorem applies: I \* K \longleftrightarrow \hat I \cdot \hat K (2D Fourier transform).

Proposition 2.2 — Separability of the Gaussian The 2D Gaussian kernel factors: G_\sigma(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2\sigma^2} = g_\sigma(x)\,g_\sigma(y) with g_\sigma(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}. Convolving by G_\sigma therefore amounts to convolving first along rows then along columns.

Proof. The factorization is an algebraic identity: e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2\sigma^2} = e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\cdot e^{-y^2/2\sigma^2} and \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}. The rest follows by associativity of convolution: I \* G_\sigma = I \* (g_\sigma^{col} \* g_\sigma^{row}) = (I \* g_\sigma^{col}) \* g_\sigma^{row}. ∎

Gain: a k\times k kernel costs k^2 multiplications per pixel in 2D, 2k in two passes; for k = 31: 961 → 62, i.e. 15.5× fewer. In practice one samples g_\sigma over [-3\sigma, 3\sigma] (99.7% of the mass) and normalizes the sum to 1.

Definition 2.3 — Derivatives and finite differences The discrete derivative is approximated by centered differences \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}(x, y) \approx \frac{I(x+1,y) - I(x-1,y)}{2}, that is, a convolution by \frac12[-1, 0, 1]. On a noisy image, differentiating amplifies the high-frequency noise; one smooths first: \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(G_\sigma \* I) = \Big(\frac{\partial G_\sigma}{\partial x}\Big) \* I, the equality coming from swapping derivative and convolution (Fubini; see the ch. 0 review and the Signal Processing module). Conclusion: differentiating a smoothed image = convolving by the derivative of a Gaussian — a single kernel, optimal in the sense of the chosen scale. This is the starting point of ch. 3.

Proposition 2.5 — Pyramids: subsampling without folding Halving the resolution without precaution folds the frequencies beyond the new Nyquist frequency (Def. 1.4). The Gaussian pyramid therefore applies G_{\sigma_0} (with \sigma_0 \geq \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} pixel) before keeping one pixel out of two; each level I^{(k+1)} = \downarrow_2 (G \* I^{(k)}) represents the image at scale 2^k. These pyramids speed up multi-scale search (ch. 4: SIFT, ch. 7: pyramidal Lucas-Kanade) and progressive rendering.

3. Edges: gradient and Canny

An edge is a rapid variation of intensity: the first order (gradient) locates it by its maxima, the second order (Laplacian) by its zero crossings. The Canny detector (1986) combines these ideas into a pipeline still present in industrial chains.

Definition 3.1 — Image gradient \nabla I = (I_x, I_y)^\top; magnitude \|\nabla I\| = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2} (rate of variation), direction \theta = \mathrm{atan2}(I_y, I_x) — edges are perpendicular to \nabla I. In the discrete setting, I_x, I_y are estimated by Sobel (Example 2.4) after Gaussian smoothing.

Example 3.2 — Gradient by hand Intensities of a neighborhood \begin{pmatrix} 52 & 54 & 55\\ 50 & 120 & 122 \\ 48 & 118 & 120\end{pmatrix} at the central point: with Sobel, I_x = \frac{(55 + 2\cdot122 + 120) - (52 + 2\cdot50 + 48)}{4} = \frac{419 - 200}{4} \approx 55, I_y = \frac{(48 + 2\cdot118 + 120) - (52 + 2\cdot54 + 55)}{4} = \frac{404 - 215}{4} \approx 47. Magnitude \sqrt{55^2+47^2} \approx 72 levels/pixel, direction \theta \approx \mathrm{atan2}(47, 55) \approx 40.5^\circ — a strong, diagonal edge. A pixel from a homogeneous area would give \pm 5 under the noise: magnitude thresholding separates signal from noise.

Algorithm 3.3 — The Canny detector

  1. Gaussian smoothing G_\sigma (\sigma sets the scale of the detected edges);
  2. gradient (Sobel) → magnitude and direction;
  3. non-maximum suppression: quantize \theta into 4 directions (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) and keep a pixel only if its magnitude exceeds that of its two neighbors in the direction of the gradient — an edge one pixel wide;
  4. hysteresis thresholding at two levels (s_{low} < s_{high}): a pixel ≥ s_{high} is a starting point; the edge is propagated to neighbors ≥ s_{low} — which reconnects edges interrupted by noise without multiplying the false positives a single low threshold would produce.

Two settings (\sigma, thresholds) — and a remarkable property: good detection/localization trade-off, implemented in a handful of milliseconds (OpenCV: cv2.Canny(img, s_low, s_high)).

Note — Zero crossings of the Laplacian (LoG) The second order detects edges as the zeros of \Delta(G\*I) = (\Delta G)\*I — the “LoG” kernel, close to the “difference of Gaussians” of ch. 4. The zero crossing is finer than the gradient maximum but more sensitive to noise, and the sign on either side encodes the bright/dark transition. Marr and Hildreth made it a model of the first level of the visual cortex — the biological prehistory of the convolutional networks of ch. 9.

4. Interest points: Harris and SIFT

Matching two images amounts to finding points that are distinctive and stable there: corners, blobs, local structures. Two classics organize the chapter: Harris (1988) to detect, SIFT (2004) to describe — and the CNNs of ch. 9 are their learned heirs.

Definition 4.1 — The structure tensor and the Harris criterion Measure the change of appearance of a window W shifted by (u, v): E(u, v) = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} w(x,y)\,\big[I(x+u, y+v) - I(x, y)\big]^2. A first-order Taylor expansion (I(x+u, y+v) \approx I + u\,I_x + v\,I_y) gives the quadratic form E(u, v) \approx \begin{pmatrix}u & v\end{pmatrix} M \begin{pmatrix}u\\ v\end{pmatrix},\qquad M = \sum_W w \begin{pmatrix} I_x^2 & I_xI_y \\ I_xI_y & I_y^2\end{pmatrix}, the structure tensor. If \lambda_1, \lambda_2 are its eigenvalues: both small = flat area; one large and one small = edge; both large = corner (varies in all directions). The Harris criterion avoids the explicit extraction of the eigenvalues: R = \det M - k\,(\mathrm{tr}\,M)^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2 - k(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2, with k \approx 0.04 — positive = corner, negative = edge.

Example 4.2 — Harris with numbers A window yields \sum I_x^2 = 126, \sum I_xI_y = 43, \sum I_y^2 = 18: M = \begin{pmatrix}126 & 43\\ 43 & 18\end{pmatrix}, \det M = 2268 - 1849 = 419, \mathrm{tr}M = 144, hence R = 419 - 0.04\times 144^2 = 419 - 829 = -410 < 0 → edge. The eigenvalues \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{144 \pm \sqrt{144^2 - 4\cdot419}}{2} \approx 141 and 3 confirm: one direction varies 47× more than the other. A corner would typically have M \sim \begin{pmatrix}400 & 10\\ 10 & 380\end{pmatrix}, R = 151\,900 - 0.04\times 625^2 \approx 137\,000 > 0.

Proposition 4.3 — Invariances of Harris, and its flaw The tensor M is an average of matrices \nabla I\,\nabla I^\top: under a rotation R of the image, \nabla I \mapsto R\,\nabla I, hence M \mapsto RMR^\top and the eigenvalues — hence the corner/edge decision and the response R — are rotation-invariant; an affine change of illumination I \mapsto aI + b multiplies M by a^2 without changing the signs. Zooming, however, changes the number of pixels participating in the corner: Harris is not scale-invariant — precisely the problem SIFT solves.

Algorithm 4.4 — SIFT: detection + description

  1. scale space: convolve with Gaussians L(x, \sigma) = G_\sigma \* I for \sigma increasing by octaves; the difference of Gaussians \mathrm{DoG}(x,\sigma) = L(x, k\sigma) - L(x, \sigma) approximates the normalized Laplacian \sigma^2\Delta I, required for scale invariance;
  2. space-scale extrema: keypoints are the local extrema of DoG in the volume (x, y, \sigma) — comparison with the 26 neighbors (8 in space, 9×2 in scale);
  3. localization: refinement by 2D Taylor; rejection of low-contrast points and of edges via the Harris ratio applied to the 2×2 Hessian matrix (\mathrm{tr}^2/\det < r rejected);
  4. orientation: histogram (36 bins) of the gradients of a neighborhood, weighted by a Gaussian; the dominant orientation provides the canonical frame of the descriptor → rotation invariance;
  5. descriptor: around the point, 4×4 cells, each an orientation histogram with 8 bins → 4\times4\times8 = 128 values, normalized (thresholding at 0.2 for robustness to saturations) → affine illumination invariance.

Matching is done by nearest neighbor with Lowe’s ratio test: accept if d_1/d_2 < 0.8 (distance to the first neighbor over distance to the second): rejecting ambiguities costs less than tolerating false matches.

Proposition 4.5 — Cost of matching Two images with n descriptors each: the exhaustive nearest-neighbor search costs O(n^2) in 128D distance — for n = 10^4, 10^8 distances ≈ a few seconds. The kd-tree (or its randomized counterpart) answers in O(\log n) in low dimension, but the curse of dimensionality makes approximation (kd-tree forests, FLANN, locally sensitive hashing) indispensable in practice — the same problematic as the vector search of the DataBase Management System module (ch. 9), 128 dimensions apart.

Example 4.6 — Harris and SIFT in Python (numpy + OpenCV)

import cv2, numpy as np

img  = cv2.imread("scene.jpg", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
imgf = img.astype(np.float32)

# --- 1. manual Harris: structure tensor, response, thresholding
Ix = cv2.Sobel(imgf, cv2.CV_32F, 1, 0, ksize=3)
Iy = cv2.Sobel(imgf, cv2.CV_32F, 0, 1, ksize=3)
A  = cv2.GaussianBlur(Ix*Ix, (5, 5), 1.5)     # weighted local sum
B  = cv2.GaussianBlur(Ix*Iy, (5, 5), 1.5)
C  = cv2.GaussianBlur(Iy*Iy, (5, 5), 1.5)
R  = (A*C - B*B) - 0.04*(A + C)**2
corners = np.argwhere(R > 0.01 * R.max())        # relative thresholding

# --- 2. OpenCV versions
 Rc = cv2.cornerHarris(imgf, 2, 3, 0.04)
 sift = cv2.SIFT_create(nfeatures=2000)
 kps, desc = sift.detectAndCompute(img, None)   # 128-D per keypoint
 img2 = cv2.drawKeypoints(img, kps, None,
                          flags=cv2.DRAW_RICH_KEYPOINTS)

# --- 3. matching two views with Lowe's ratio test
kps2, desc2 = sift.detectAndCompute(cv2.imread("scene2.jpg", 0), None)
bf  = cv2.BFMatcher()
mch = [m for m, n in bf.knnMatch(desc, desc2, k=2) if m.distance < 0.8*n.distance]
print(len(mch), "matches kept out of", len(desc))

5. Projective geometry and homographies

Two views of the same plane (facade, document, whiteboard), or one camera that only rotates about itself: in both cases, the relation between pixels is a homography. It is the basic tool of registration, panoramas, and augmented reality.

Definition 5.1 — Homography A homography is a projective map of the plane \mathbb{P}^2 defined by a matrix H \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3} of rank 3, up to a scale factor: \tilde{x}' \sim H\tilde{x}. It therefore has 9 - 1 = 8 degrees of freedom: 4 pairs of points (2 independent equations each) suffice to determine it. In \mathbb{P}^2, points and lines are in duality: the line through x_1, x_2 is l = x_1 \times x_2, the intersection point of l_1, l_2 is l_1 \times l_2, and a homography carries points and lines (l' \sim H^{-\top} l).

Theorem 5.2 — When a homography applies The relation between two views is a homography if (i) the scene is planar, or (ii) the camera undergoes only a pure rotation about its optical center.

Proof. Case (i): plane \Pi = \{\tilde{X} : \tilde{n}^\top \tilde{X} = d\} in the frame of camera 1, relative motion (R, t). For X \in \Pi: X_2 = R X_1 + t = (R + t\,\tilde{n}^\top/d)\,X_1 — the depth is eliminated in favor of a 3×3 linear combination; with u_1 \sim K_1 X_1 it follows that u_2 \sim K_2 (R + t\tilde n^\top/d) R_1^\top K_1^{-1} u_1 = H u_1. Case (ii): if t = 0, P = K R and \tilde{u}_2 \sim K_2 R_2 R_1^\top K_1^{-1}\,\tilde{u}_1 — a 3\times3 product, and we are done. ∎

Proposition 5.3 — Direct linear estimation (DLT) Each correspondence \tilde{x} \leftrightarrow \tilde{x}' provides \tilde{x}' \times (H\tilde{x}) = 0, i.e. 2 linear equations in the 9 coefficients of H (up to scale, one fixes \|h\| = 1). With n \geq 4 correspondences, the system Ah = 0 (2n \times 9) is solved by SVD: h is the right singular vector associated with the smallest singular value — the “smallest residual” version of homogeneous least squares. Golden rule: normalize the points before DLT (translate the centroid to the origin, scale so that the mean distance is \sqrt2; Hartley) — otherwise the conditioning degrades the solution by several orders of magnitude. With noisy correspondences: nonlinear refinement (Levenberg-Marquardt) of the reprojection error \sum_i \|\tilde{x}'_i \times H\tilde{x}_i\|^2.

Proposition 5.4 — RANSAC: estimating amid outliers SIFT correspondences contain false positives. The RANSAC paradigm (RANdom SAmple Consensus) draws at random a minimal set of points (here 4), estimates H, counts the inliers (residual < \varepsilon), repeats, and refines the model on the best consensus. With a proportion w of inliers and 4 points drawn: probability that a draw is clean = w^4; for a success rate p after N draws: N \;\geq\; \frac{\log(1-p)}{\log(1-w^4)}. Worked example: 50% inliers, p = 0.99 → N = \frac{-4.605}{\ln(1 - 0.0625)} = \frac{-4.605}{-0.0645} \approx 72 draws. At 90% inliers: w^4 = 0.656, N = 3. The cost is trivial compared to least squares on contaminated data — and the model extends to any pair (minimal estimator, number of inliers).

Note — Panoramas and warping Assembling a panorama: SIFT correspondences between pairs of neighboring images → homographies by RANSAC → a global “bundle adjustment” to avoid drift → final projection (plane, cylindrical, or spherical depending on the angular amplitude). Warping is written in inverse coordinates: for each output pixel, x = H^{-1}x', then bilinear interpolation — interpolating in the forward direction would leave holes.

6. Stereo vision and epipolar geometry

Two views of a 3D scene, taken from different points: the epipolar constraint says that the correspondent of a pixel lies on a known line in the second image. All of stereo vision — from reconstruction to robotics — follows from this geometric observation.

Definition 6.1 — Epipolar geometry, F and E Consider a pair of cameras P_1 = K_1[I \mid 0], P_2 = K_2[R \| t]. The 3D point X projects to x_1 and x_2; the epipolar plane passes through X, x_1, x_2, and the two optical centers; its intersection with image plane 2 is the epipolar line of x_1 — every match must live on it. The constraint reads \tilde{x}_2^\top F\, \tilde{x}_1 = 0,\qquad F = K_2^{-\top} [t]_\times R\, K_1^{-1}, F is the fundamental matrix (rank 2, 7 dof); its version normalized by the intrinsic matrices, E = K_2^\top F\,K_1 = [t]_\times R, is the essential matrix (5 dof): it depends only on the relative motion.

Proposition 6.2 — The 8-point algorithm Each match \tilde{x}_1 \leftrightarrow \tilde{x}_2 gives one linear equation in the 9 coefficients of F; 8 matches suffice (hence the name). Solve by SVD of the homogeneous system, then project onto the set of rank-2 matrices (replace the smallest singular value by 0: F = U\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, 0)\,V^\top). The normalization of the points (Prop. 5.3) is again crucial here. Next: decomposition of E (SVD) into 4 candidate (R, t) pairs, ambiguity resolved by the chirality test (the reconstructed points must lie in front of the cameras). This is “calibrated stereo”; the uncalibrated version stops at F.

Theorem 6.3 — Rectified stereo: disparity and depth After rectification (resampling of both images so that the epipolar lines are the horizontal lines — a pair of homographies, ch. 5), a point at depth Z is seen at abscissae x_1, x_2 with d = x_1 - x_2 = \frac{f\,B}{Z}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; Z = \frac{fB}{d}, f: focal length in pixels, B: baseline (distance between cameras), d: disparity.

Proof. Elementary triangulation: the 3D point (X, Y, Z) projects to x_1 = fX/Z in the left camera; the right camera, translated by B along the common optical axis, sees x_2 = f(X - B)/Z. The difference x_1 - x_2 = fB/Z no longer depends on X: disparity encodes depth. ∎

Example 6.4 — Orders of magnitude (KITTI style) f = 721 px, B = 0.54 m. A car at Z = 39 m has a disparity d = fB/Z = 721\times0.54/39 \approx 10 px; at 10 m, d \approx 39 px. Error: since Z = fB/d is non-linear, \Delta Z/Z = \Delta d/d: an uncertainty of ±0.2 px on d = 10 px gives ±2% (±0.8 m); on d = 3 px (130 m), ±7% — stereo sees far away poorly: precision degrades with the square of the distance, hence the value of large baselines or LiDAR (ch. 10).

Note — Modern dense stereo The classic pipeline: per-pixel local cost (SSD/SAD over windows, or the illumination-robust census transform), aggregation and regularization by per-row dynamic programming, or semi-global matching (Hirschmüller, 2008): minimize a global energy by summing costs over 8 directions — the industrial standard for depth maps, up to learned stereo networks (PSM-Net etc.) that process the image pair end to end. Triangulation of an isolated match is solved, for its part, as a linear system: \tilde{x} \times (P\tilde{X}) = 0 provides 2 equations per camera, 4 for the pair — SVD, again.

Definition 6.5 — Structure from motion (SfM) and MultiView Stereo The image pair of the chapter generalizes to n unordered views:

  • Structure from motion (SfM): recovering both the camera poses and the 3D structure. Incremental pipeline (COLMAP): SIFT matches (ch. 4) filtered by RANSAC on F then E (Prop. 6.2) → well-conditioned initial pair, decomposition of E into (R, t), triangulation → views added one by one by resection (DLT, ch. 5), additional 3D points by triangulation; the whole is refined by bundle adjustment: global minimization of the reprojection error \sum_{i,j} \big\| x_{ij} - \pi(P_j X_i) \big\|^2 over poses and points (Gauss-Newton / Levenberg-Marquardt on the sparse system).
  • MultiView Stereo (MVS): poses known, one densifies — dense correlation (the Note above) applied to several pairs to produce depth maps, then fusion into a global point cloud by visibility and geometric consistency tests; this is modern “photogrammetry”, which meets the LiDAR clouds of ch. 10.

SfM and MVS close the loop of the course: features (ch. 4), geometry (ch. 5, 6), and dense correspondences manufacture, from images alone, the 3D scene and the poses — exactly the “3D shape recovery” that the course sheet gives as its objective.

7. Optical flow: Lucas-Kanade

The apparent motion of luminances between two images — optical flow — is the ingredient of tracking, video compression, and navigation. Lucas-Kanade (1981) remains the reference formulation, and its mathematics is a condensed version of the course.

Definition 7.1 — Gradient constraint and the aperture problem Luminance conservation assumption: I(x + u, y + v, t + \Delta t) = I(x, y, t). First-order Taylor expansion: I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0 \qquad (\text{with } u = dx/dt,\; v = dy/dt,\; I_t = \partial I/\partial t), a single equation for two unknowns (u, v): the line of possible flows. Locally, an aperture (small window) does not suffice to determine the motion of an edge — this is the aperture problem, or the illusion of the wheel turning behind a slot.

Theorem 7.2 — Lucas-Kanade: least squares over a window Assuming the flow is constant in a window W, each pixel provides a gradient constraint; the optimal flow minimizes J(u, v) = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} w(x,y)\,\big(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\big)^2. Setting the gradient to zero (\partial J/\partial u = \partial J/\partial v = 0) yields the normal equations \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sum w\,I_x^2 & \sum w\,I_xI_y\\ \sum w\,I_xI_y & \sum w\,I_y^2\end{pmatrix}}_{=\,M,\ \text{the structure tensor!}} \begin{pmatrix}u\\ v\end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix}\sum w\,I_xI_t\\ \sum w\,I_yI_t\end{pmatrix}.

Proof. Derivative with respect to u: 2\sum w\,I_x(I_xu + I_yv + I_t) = 0 → first row; symmetrically for v → second row. The matrix M is exactly Harris’s structure tensor (Def. 4.1): Lucas-Kanade is solvable precisely where Harris detects a corner — same large \lambda_1, \lambda_2. ∎

Domain of validity: small motions (Taylor), locally constant flow — hence the two remedies: (i) iterative resolution by successive warping; (ii) coarse-to-fine approach via a pyramid (Prop. 2.5): estimate large at the coarse scale, refine while going down.

Example 7.3 — A 2×2 system by hand Window of 4 pixels, gradients and time derivative (I_x, I_y\,;\,I_t): (3, 1; 8), (5, 2; 10), (4, 2; 9), (6, 3; 12). Sums: M = \begin{pmatrix}9+25+16+36 & 3+10+8+18\\ \cdot & 1+4+4+9\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}86 & 39\\ 39 & 18\end{pmatrix}, right-hand side -\big(\sum I_xI_t, \sum I_yI_t\big) = -(182, 82). Let us solve: \det M = 86\times18 - 39^2 = 1548 - 1521 = 27 — nearly singular (eigenvalues ≈ 103.7 and 0.26: nearly aligned gradients). Cramer’s formulas: u = \frac{-182\times18 + 82\times39}{27} = \frac{-78}{27} \approx -2.89, v = \frac{86\times(-82) + 39\times182}{27} = \frac{46}{27} \approx 1.70. The flow is computable but unreliable: \lambda_2 \ll \lambda_1 signals an edge, not a corner — Example 4.2 gave the opposite diagnosis on another tensor. In practice: require \lambda_2 > \tau before trusting the flow (and weight by the distance to the centroid to smooth).

Note — Horn-Schunck, the global version Instead of a window, Horn-Schunck (1981) regularizes over the whole image: minimize \int (I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2 + \lambda\,\big(\|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2\big): data attachment (gradient constraint) + a smooth flow field. The Euler-Lagrange equations are solved iteratively; \lambda tunes the data/regularity trade-off — the same energy structure as the graph cuts of ch. 8, and as many inverse problems of the Signal Processing module. Modern variant: learned networks supervised by these very energies.

8. Segmentation: k-means and graph cuts

Segmenting means partitioning the image into coherent regions: objects of interest, surfaces, backgrounds. Two families: the clustering approach (pixels are points in a feature space) and the energy-based approach (look for a partition that minimizes a global cost).

Definition 8.1 — k-means on pixels Each pixel becomes a vector z_i — RGB, or RGB + (\alpha x, \alpha y) to account for spatial proximity, or Lab — and one partitions \{z_i\} into k groups by minimizing J = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i \in C_j} \|z_i - \mu_j\|^2. Lloyd’s algorithm alternates assignment to the nearest centroid then recentering — each step decreases J, hence convergence in a finite number of steps (to a local minimum; k-means++ initialization or several restarts mitigate its variance — see the Machine Learning module, ch. 6).

Example 8.2 — k-means in grayscale, by hand Intensities \{10, 12, 11, 40, 42, 90\}, k = 2, initial centroids 10 and 40. Assignment: 10, 12, 11 → C_1 (distances 0, 2, 1 versus 30, 28, 29); 40, 42, 90 → C_2. Recentering: \mu_1 = 11, \mu_2 = 57.3. Second pass: no reassignment (12 remains at distance 1 versus 45.3…): convergence. J = (1+1+0) + (17.3^2 + 15.3^2 + 32.7^2) \approx 1606. The pixel 90, at 33 from its centroid, drives the whole cost: a k=3 would isolate it — the choice of k (elbow, silhouette, domain prior) is the real problem, not convergence.

Definition 8.3 — Segmentation by graph cuts (binary) Graph: vertices = pixels (+ source s and sink t); neighborhood edges weighted by similarity B_{pq} \propto e^{-\|z_p - z_q\|^2/2\sigma^2} and terminal edges by the fit to the seeds provided by the user (Boykov-Jolly mode): R_p(s), R_p(t). The minimum cut separates s and t and defines the segmentation; min-cut = max-flow (Ford-Fulkerson), polynomial. The energy formulation: E(L) = \sum_p R_p\big(L_p\big) + \lambda \sum_{(p,q)\ \text{neighbors}} B_{pq}\,\big[L_p \neq L_q\big], data attachment + boundary regularization (Potts model); it is exactly minimizable by a cut if the interaction terms are “submodular” (here: B_{pq} \geq 0). For k > 2 labels, the problem becomes NP-hard: alpha-expansion (Boykov) gives a 2-approximation that remains practically unbeatable.

9. CNNs for vision: detection and segmentation

Convolutional networks, introduced in ch. 4 of the Deep Learning module, have absorbed a large part of the classical pipeline: learned filters replace Sobel and SIFT. This chapter focuses on vision tasks: classifying, detecting (where are the objects?), segmenting (which pixels belong to them?).

Proposition 9.1 — Why convolution suits images Three inductive biases together: (i) shared weights: the same filter slides everywhere — translation invariance and a parameter count independent of image size; (ii) local connectivity: each neuron sees only a neighborhood — the receptive field grows with depth; (iii) hierarchy: edges → motifs → parts → objects, learned rather than programmed. The residual connections of ResNet (y = \mathcal{F}(x) + x, He et al. 2015) allow depths of hundreds of layers by short-circuiting vanishing gradients (see the Deep Learning module, ch. 2-3); transfer learning — reusing a ResNet trained on ImageNet as a feature extractor — remains the first step of any project with scarce data (ibid., ch. 8).

Definition 9.2 — Detection: two families Detection returns boxes (x, y, w, h) + class + score. Two-stage: R-CNN (2014) proposes regions, a CNN classifies them; Fast R-CNN shares the computations; Faster R-CNN (2015) has the proposals generated by an anchor-based (boxes of predefined sizes/ratios) Region Proposal Network — top accuracy at the time. One-stage: YOLO (2015) cuts the image into a grid and directly regresses boxes + classes per cell, in a single pass — the precision/speed trade-off that opened embedded vision. NMS (non-maximum suppression) eliminates redundant boxes: sort by decreasing score, keep the first, remove all those whose overlap (IoU) exceeds a threshold (~0.5), iterate. Anchor-free detectors and transformer-based models (DETR: learnable queries + attention, set prediction with Hungarian matching — see the Deep Learning module, ch. 6) close the loop: end of manual NMS, set detection.

Definition 9.3 — Semantic segmentation and U-Net Decision per pixel (each receives a class); instance segmentation additionally distinguishes objects of the same class, panoptic unifies the two. The U-Net architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015): a contracting encoder (global context) then an expansive decoder with skip connections that reinject fine resolutions — because the per-pixel decision requires both semantics (deep) and localization (fine); the decoder learns to “re-blend” the two. Trainable with few images (aggressive geometric augmentation), U-Net has become the universal tool: medical imaging, inspection, and — in ch. 11 — LiDAR as depth images.

Definition 9.4 — Metrics: IoU and mAP The intersection-over-union between predicted boxes (or masks) P and ground truth G: \mathrm{IoU} = \|P \cap G\|\,/\,\|P \cup G\| — a prediction is a true positive if \mathrm{IoU} > \tau (typically 0.5) and if the class is correct. Average precision (AP): sort the detections by decreasing score, plot the (recall, precision) points, integrate (area under the curve); mAP averages the APs over classes — and sometimes over IoU thresholds (COCO: mAP@[0.5:0.95]).

Example 9.5 — AP by hand (1 class, IoU threshold 0.5) Detections sorted by score: D_1 (IoU 0.8), D_2 (IoU 0.3), D_3 (IoU 0.6), D_4 (IoU 0.55 with an object already covered) — 2 objects in total. Cumulative counts:

Detection TP/FP Cumulative TP Cumulative FP Precision Recall
D1 (0.8) TP 1 0 1.00 0.5
D2 (0.3) FP 1 1 0.50 0.5
D3 (0.6) TP 2 1 0.67 1.0

AP = area under the precision-recall curve (decreasing envelope): with “all-point” interpolation, \mathrm{AP} \approx 1.0\times0.5 + 0.67\times0.5 = 0.83. The benchmark datasets (ImageNet for classification, COCO for detection/segmentation, Cityscapes for streets) dictate architectures as much as the literature does.

Note — Beyond massive supervision Pre-training on image-text pairs (CLIP: contrastive over 400 million pairs — see the Multimodal GenAI module, ch. 5) yields features that zero-shot classify by simple text-image similarity; “promptable” segmentation and conditional diffusion models (ibid., ch. 4-6) push the same idea into generation. Classical vision is not dead for all that: calibration, epipolar geometry, and ICP remain the foundations of metric systems — the subject of the next chapter.

10. 3D point clouds: ICP and urban analysis

Mobile or airborne LiDAR, photogrammetry, RGB-D depth: the world also arrives as point clouds — millions of 3D points, possibly colored, with no topology. The questions become geometric: register two clouds, estimate normals, segment, classify.

Definition 10.1 — LiDAR: time-of-flight measurement A laser rangefinder emits a pulse and measures the echo: d = c\,\Delta t/2 with c \approx 3\times10^8 m/s. A precision of 2 cm requires timing to 2\times0.02/(3\times10^8) \approx 0.13 ns — and the echo is integrated over surfaces at varied incidence, hence the “comb” noise at edges. A mobile LiDAR (on a vehicle, IGN’s Stéréopolis for example) combines rangefinders, cameras, and an inertial/GNSS unit: fused odometry (Kalman filter) gives the trajectory, each position adds a “cap” of points to the global cloud; an urban network easily produces 10^9 points.

Proposition 10.2 — Neighborhoods, normals, complexities On a cloud of n points: the nearest neighbor is obtained with a kd-tree (or octree in 3D) in O(\log n) on average (construction O(n\log n)) versus O(n) by brute force — with the same dimensional caveat as in ch. 4. The local normal at point p is estimated by PCA of the neighborhood: the eigenvector associated with the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix \sum_{q \in \mathcal{N}(p)} (q - \bar q)(q - \bar q)^\top (the residual variance to the tangent plane is minimal — a direct consequence of the Eckart-Young theorem of ch. 0). Cost per point: O(k) once the neighborhood is found.

Theorem 10.3 — ICP: optimal rigid transform (Kabsch) Given two paired sets \{p_i\}, \{q_i\}, i = 1..n, seeking R \in SO(3) and t minimizing \sum_i \|R\,p_i + t - q_i\|^2 has an exact solution: t = \bar q - R\bar p (centroids) and, setting H = \sum_i (p_i - \bar p)(q_i - \bar q)^\top = U\Lambda V^\top (SVD), R = V\,U^\top (and R = V\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, \det(VU^\top))\,U^\top if \det(VU^\top) = -1, the “reflection” case).

Proof. The term in t is quadratic: setting the derivative to zero gives t = \bar q - R\bar p. Substituting, the sum becomes \sum_i \|R\,\tilde p_i - \tilde q_i\|^2 = \sum \|\tilde p_i\|^2 + \sum\|\tilde q_i\|^2 - 2\sum \tilde q_i^\top R\tilde p_i, where the \tilde{} denote centered points: minimizing amounts to maximizing \sum_i \tilde q_i^\top R \tilde p_i = \mathrm{tr}(RH). Now \mathrm{tr}(RH) = \mathrm{tr}(R\,U\Lambda V^\top) = \mathrm{tr}(\underbrace{V^\top R U}_{=\,Z}\,\Lambda) = \sum_i Z_{ii}\, \lambda_i with Z orthogonal, so Z_{ii} \leq 1 and \lambda_i \geq 0 give \mathrm{tr}(RH) \leq \sum_i \lambda_i, attained for Z = I, i.e. R = VU^\top. The negative-determinant correction maximizes under the constraint \det R = +1 by zeroing Z_{33} (smallest singular value). ∎

Proposition 10.4 — ICP: the algorithm and its local convergence ICP (Iterative Closest Point, Besl-McKay 1992) alternates: (1) matching: each point of P is associated with its nearest neighbor in Q (kd-tree, O(n\log n)); (2) alignment: optimal rigid transform of the pairs (Theorem 10.3); (3) application — until convergence.

Proof. The objective E(R, t) = \sum_i \|R p_i + t - \mathrm{nn}_Q(Rp_i + t)\|^2 decreases at every iteration: step (1) can only improve (or leave unchanged) each term by choosing the current nearest neighbor, and step (2) exactly minimizes the sum over these fixed matches. Combination of a decreasing sequence bounded below (by 0): convergence — to a local minimum, depending on the initialization. ∎

Remedies: coarse initialization (GPS, global descriptors, RANSAC over quadruplets), rejected matches (distance > quantile, incompatible normals), the point-to-plane variant (minimize distances to tangent planes — once linearized, it gives a more precise 6×6 least squares), or robust formulations: trimmed ICP, robust optimal transport (see below).

Example 10.5 — ICP and matching in Python

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def best_rigid_transform(P, Q):            # theorem 10.3 (Kabsch)
    cp, cq = P.mean(0), Q.mean(0)
    H = (P - cp).T @ (Q - cq)              # 3x3
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(H)
    d = np.sign(np.linalg.det(Vt.T @ U.T))
    R = Vt.T @ np.diag([1, 1, d]) @ U.T
    return R, cq - R @ cp

def icp(P, Q, iters=50, tol=1e-7):
    tree = cKDTree(Q); R = np.eye(3); t = np.zeros(3); prev = np.inf
    for _ in range(iters):
        d, idx = tree.query(P @ R.T + t)          # (1) matching
        Rk, tk = best_rigid_transform(P, Q[idx])  # (2) Kabsch
        R, t = Rk @ R, Rk @ t + tk                # (3) accumulation
        err = np.mean(d**2)
        if prev - err < tol: break
        prev = err
    return R, t, np.sqrt(err)

# synthetic test: 25-degree rotation + 1 cm noise
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
src = rng.normal(size=(3000, 3)) * [10, 10, 1]    # noisy "ground"
a = np.deg2rad(25)
Rt = np.array([[np.cos(a), -np.sin(a), 0],
               [np.sin(a),  np.cos(a), 0],
               [0, 0, 1]])
dst = src @ Rt.T + [2, -1, 0] + rng.normal(scale=.01, size=src.shape)
R, t, rms = icp(src, dst)
print("angle error (deg):", np.rad2deg(np.arccos((np.trace(R.T @ Rt)-1)/2)))

Note — ICP and optimal transport Replacing “nearest neighbor” with a global matching that minimizes a transport cost means reformulating registration as an optimal transport problem (the M1P2 module of the same master’s program, ch. 1-2): the Wasserstein distance compares clouds as measures, not as labeled points — insensitive to differing densities, robust to noise with concave costs. This is the path explored by the recent literature on robust OT registration (RobOT, NeurIPS 2021; partial transport for incomplete clouds). The same formalism reappears to compare descriptor histograms or depth maps: a common language for ch. 6-10.

Proposition 10.6 — Representing a cloud for learning Three families of network inputs: (i) volumes (voxelize into a 3D grid — 3D convolutions possible but cubic cost; sparse trees/octrees for the worst case); (ii) raw points (PointNet: MLP per point + global max = symmetric function, permutation invariance; its successors add a local neighborhood); (iii) range images (range images: project the cloud onto a spherical image whose every pixel encodes the distance — 2D CNNs then apply as-is, see LU-Net in ch. 11). The choice is a memory/precision/speed trade-off — over 10^9 points, family (iii) is unbeatable and feeds the real-time systems of autonomous vehicles.

  3. Lucas-Kanade (solved). Window of 4 pixels, measurements (I_x, I_y\,;\,I_t): (2, 1; 6), (4, 2; 10), (3, 1; 7), (5, 2; 12). Solve for the flow; compute the eigenvalues of the tensor; is the flow reliable? Propose two remedies. Solution. M = \begin{pmatrix}54 & 23\\ 23 & 10\end{pmatrix}, right-hand side -(133, 57)^\top. \det M = 11 — nearly singular: \lambda_1 \approx 63.8, \lambda_2 \approx 0.17. Cramer: u = v = -19/11 \approx -1.73. Reliability: no — \lambda_2 \ll \lambda_1 signals an edge (aperture problem, Thm 7.2); the uncertainty ellipse is very elongated: the flow component along the edge is poorly observed. Remedies: pyramid (large → small motion) and Gaussian weighting of the window; failing that, reject the pixel (\lambda_2 < \tau).
  4. Stereo (solved). f = 721 px, B = 0.54 m. (a) Disparity of an object at 25 m, at 5 m? (b) Depth if d = 25 px? (c) Depth uncertainty at 25 m if the disparity is known to ±0.25 px? (d) Why double the baseline? Solution. (a) d = fB/Z: 15.6 px at 25 m; 77.9 px at 5 m. (b) Z = fB/d = 721\times0.54/25 \approx 15.6 m. (c) \Delta Z/Z = \Delta d/d = 0.25/15.6 \approx 1.6\,\% → ±0.4 m (Thm 6.3). (d) Z = fB/d: doubling B doubles d at equal depth, hence halves the relative error — at the price of a wider separation and increased occlusion areas between views.
