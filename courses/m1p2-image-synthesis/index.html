0. Background: probability, integrals, linear ODEs

L3 background mobilized

  • Expectation and variance: linearity of expectation, \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2, variance of a mean of N i.i.d. variables divided by N — all the noise of Monte Carlo rendering (chapter 7) and its denoising (chapter 8) are in these three lines;
  • Law of large numbers: \bar{X}_N \to \mathbb{E}X almost surely — the convergence of path tracing; central limit theorem: the typical error decreases as \sigma/\sqrt{N};
  • Multiple integrals, spherical coordinates, solid angle d\omega = \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi, area of the unit hemisphere 2\pi — chapter 1 lives on the sphere;
  • Change of variables for a density: if X \sim p_X and Y = g(X), then p_Y(y) = p_X(g^{-1}(y))\, |\det Dg^{-1}(y)| — the sampling of light (chapter 7);
  • First-order linear ODE: y' + a(t)\,y = b(t) is solved with the integrating factor y(t) = e^{-\int a}\big(y_0 + \int b e^{\int a}\big) — the transmittance of chapter 10;
  • Algebra: dot and cross products, orthonormal bases, change-of-basis matrices — the camera (chapter 2).

Proposition 0.1 — The formula to remember from chapter 7 To estimate I = \int_D f(x)\,dx, sample X_1, \dots, X_N i.i.d. with density p on D (with p > 0 where f \neq 0) and set \hat{I}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{f(X_k)}{p(X_k)}. Then \mathbb{E}[\hat{I}_N] = I (unbiased estimator) and \mathrm{Var}(\hat{I}_N) = \mathrm{Var}(f(X)/p(X))/N. Monte Carlo rendering = choosing D (the light paths), f (the radiative contribution) and p (the sampling strategy) — and all the art lies in p.

1. Radiometry: radiance

A render that looks pretty but is not physical breaks as soon as the lighting changes. This chapter fixes the physical quantities of light, and singles out the one that a renderer must compute: radiance.

What breaks Three mistakes recur: confusing irradiance (power per unit area) with radiance (power per unit area and per unit solid angle), selling a biased estimator as unbiased, and using material models that violate reciprocity.

Definition 1.1 — Flux, irradiance, exitance, radiance

  • Flux (power): \Phi, in watts — the light energy per second;
  • Irradiance E(x) in W/m²: flux received per unit area at x; exitance M: flux emitted per unit area;
  • Solid angle: \omega = A/r^2 (surface cut out on the unit sphere), in steradians; the hemisphere: 2\pi sr, the sphere: 4\pi sr;
  • Radiance L(x, \omega) in W/(m²·sr): the flux per unit area perpendicular to the direction and per unit solid angle, d^2\Phi = L(x, \omega)\,\cos\theta\, dA\, d\omega, where \theta is the angle between \omega and the normal at x. This is the quantity of the module: sensors (pixels, the eye) respond to radiance.

Theorem 1.2 — Invariance of radiance along a ray In a vacuum (no absorption, no emission), radiance is constant along a ray: for two points x_1, x_2 that “see” each other, L(x_1 \to x_2) = L(x_2 \to x_1).

Proof. Consider the beam delimited by two small surfaces dA_1 (around x_1) and dA_2 (around x_2), at distance r. The flux crossing this beam can be written on both sides: d^2\Phi = L_1 \cos\theta_1\, dA_1\, d\omega_1 = L_2 \cos\theta_2\, dA_2\, d\omega_2, and the solid angle under which dA_2 is seen from x_1 equals d\omega_1 = \cos\theta_2\, dA_2 / r^2 (and symmetrically d\omega_2 = \cos\theta_1\, dA_1 / r^2). Substituting: L_1 \cos\theta_1 dA_1 \frac{\cos\theta_2 dA_2}{r^2} = L_2 \cos\theta_2 dA_2 \frac{\cos\theta_1 dA_1}{r^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; L_1 = L_2. Conservation of flux in the beam forces the equality — all the geometric factors cancel out exactly. ∎

Example 1.3 — A light bulb, round numbers An isotropic “bulb” of flux \Phi = 800 lm (≈ 1.2 W radiometric at 555 nm) at 2 m from a wall. Flux in a cone of aperture d\omega = 0.01 sr directed at the wall: d\Phi = \Phi\, d\omega/(4\pi) \approx 0.93 mW. Illuminated patch: dA = r^2\, d\omega/\cos\theta = 0.04 m² (at normal incidence), hence irradiance E = d\Phi/dA \approx 23 mW/m². At 4 m: same computation, dA multiplied by 4, E divided by 4 (\approx 5.8 mW/m²) — the famous 1/r^2 law is not a law of radiance (constant!) but of beam geometry: the pixels see the same radiance, the projected surface of the source decays as 1/r^2.

Why so many units? Photometry (lumens, candelas, lux) repeats radiometry (watts, W/sr, W/m²) weighted by the eye’s sensitivity (the function V(\lambda), peak at 555 nm where 1 W = 683 lm). Physical rendering computes in spectral radiance W/(m²·sr·nm) then converts to RGB by integration against the response functions of the three sensors — the “white” of an image is only a convention of these integrals (chapter 8: tone mapping).

2. Cameras: the pinhole model

An image is a set of radiance measurements, one per pixel. This chapter models the device that makes these measurements — the camera — and gives the two maps a renderer needs: from a 3D point to a pixel (projection), and from a pixel to a ray (ray generation).

Definition 2.1 — The pinhole An ideal camera is a point O (optical center) and an image plane at distance f (focal length). Every ray through O contributes: the pixel corresponding to direction \omega receives the radiance L(\omega). In camera coordinates (z forward), the projection reads \pi(x, y, z) = \Big(\, f\,\frac{x}{z},\ \ f\,\frac{y}{z} \,\Big), \qquad K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, K gathers focal lengths in pixels f_x = f/\delta_x (pixel size \delta) and optical center (c_x, c_y) (principal point); the camera pose (extrinsics) is the world \to camera change-of-basis matrix [R \mid t]. Ray generation: \mathbf{d} = \mathrm{norm}\big(R^\top K^{-1} (u, v, 1)^\top\big), origin O — the inverse loop of the projection.

Theorem 2.2 — A central projection maps lines to lines The image under a pinhole of any 3D line not passing through O is a line (or a point if the line passes through O).

Proof. Let \ell be a 3D line. The plane \Pi = \mathrm{span}(O, \ell) (plane containing O and \ell) exists as soon as \ell does not pass through O. Every ray from a point of \ell is included in \Pi; the image of \ell — the set of intersections of these rays with the image plane — is therefore included in \Pi \cap \text{image plane}, the intersection of two distinct planes: a line. ∎

Example 2.3 — Projecting then pixelizing 640×480 sensor, square pixels of 50 µm ⟹ f_x = f_y = f/\delta; for f = 50 mm: f_x = 1000 px, c = (320, 240). Camera point (x, y, z) = (1, 2, 5) m: projection (f x/z,\ f y/z) = (10,\,20) mm, then pixels, in image convention (y downward): u = f_x\, x/z + c_x = 200 + 320 = 520, v = c_y - f_y\, y/z = 240 - 400 = -160 — the point is out of the vertical field: the vertical field of view equals 2\arctan(H/(2 f_x)) = 2 \arctan(480/2000) \approx 27° (half-field 13.5°), whereas the direction of the point rises \arctan(2/5) \approx 22° above the axis — well beyond the top edge. Remember: short focal = wide field = strong perspective; long focal = narrow field = compressed “telephoto” perspectives.

Definition 2.4 — Beyond the pinhole

  • Thin lens: aperture of radius r and focus at distance D; out-of-focus points form on the sensor a disk (circle of confusion) of size \propto r\,|D - z|/z — the bokeh; in rendering: sample the aperture (lens) and the focus, then average;
  • Distortions: the pinhole + lens model is linear in homogeneous coordinates; real lenses add radial distortion (r \mapsto r + k_1 r^3 + \dots) — the “fish-eye” model of the vision module;
  • Exposure: the sensor integrates radiance for a duration \Delta t at a given aperture; the (aperture, time) pair does not change the scene’s radiance, only the measured signal — hence rendering in high dynamic range values (HDR, chapter 8).

3. Rasterization versus ray tracing

Once the camera is fixed, a renderer must decide which surface each pixel sees. This chapter compares the two ways of doing it — looping over the objects (rasterization) or over the pixels (ray tracing) — and the arithmetic that makes the first one so fast.

Definition 3.1 — The two orders of traversal

  • Rasterization (object order): for each triangle, determine the covered pixels and write the framebuffer; complexity O(\sum_i A_i) (area of the triangles), independent of lighting complexity; native GPU hardware;
  • Ray tracing (image order): for each pixel, generate a ray (chapter 2) and find the first intersection (chapter 6); complexity O(N_{pixels} \cdot \log N_{triangles}) with an acceleration structure; secondary rays (shadows, reflections) come naturally — the decisive argument for global illumination.

Theorem 3.2 — Edge functions and barycentric coordinates In the image plane, define the edge function from a to b at p: E_{ab}(p) = (b_x - a_x)(p_y - a_y) - (b_y - a_y) (p_x - a_x) (2D cross product). Then p is inside triangle (a, b, c) iff E_{ab}, E_{bc}, E_{ca} have the same sign at p, and the barycentric coordinates are the area ratios \lambda_a = \frac{E_{bc}(p)}{E_{bc}(a)},\qquad \lambda_b = \frac{E_{ca}(p)}{E_{ca}(b)},\qquad \lambda_c = \frac{E_{ab}(p)}{E_{ab}(c)},\qquad \lambda_a + \lambda_b + \lambda_c = 1. Moreover E_{ab} is affine in p ⟹ incremental update: E_{ab}(p + (1, 0)) = E_{ab}(p) + (b_y - a_y) — two additions per pixel.

Proof. E_{ab}(p) is (up to sign) the area of the parallelogram spanned by (b - a) and (p - a), hence the oriented area of triangle (a, b, p). The point p is on the same side of (ab) as c iff E_{ab}(p) and E_{ab}(c) have the same sign; the three conditions together characterize the interior of the triangle (convexity). For the barycentrics: p \mapsto E_{bc}(p)/E_{bc}(a) is affine, equals 1 at a and 0 at b and c (bases of (a,b,c)); it is therefore \lambda_a, and the sum equals 1 because it equals 1 at the three vertices (uniqueness of barycentric interpolation). Affinity gives the increment by differentiation: the gradient of E_{ab} is (-(b_y - a_y),\ b_x - a_x). ∎

Definition 3.3 — Z-buffer and visibility Rasterization resolves visibility with the z-buffer: a depth buffer initialized to +\infty; for each fragment of depth z_p (interpolated), write the color only if z_p < z_{buffer}. Memory cost: one float per pixel; independent of the drawing order (unlike painter’s sorting) — but ambiguous at equal depths (z-fighting: add a polygonal bias). In ray tracing, visibility is resolved by the nearest intersection — the z-buffer is useless there: the two families also differ on where visibility is computed (per fragment versus per ray).

Example 3.4 — A triangle over pixels, by hand Projected vertices a = (1, 1), b = (5, 1), c = (1, 4) (pixel coordinates). Edge functions: E_{ab}(p) = (b - a) \times (p - a) = 4(p_y - 1); E_{bc}(p) = (c - b) \times (p - b) = -4(p_y - 1) - 3(p_x - 5); E_{ca}(p) = 3(1 - p_x). Test of the pixel with center (1, 2): E_{ab} = 4, E_{bc} = 8, E_{ca} = 0 — on edge [a, c], inside by the “top-left” rule. Barycentrics: \lambda_a = E_{bc}(p)/E_{bc}(a) = 8/12 = \tfrac23, \lambda_b = E_{ca}(p)/E_{ca}(b) = 0, \lambda_c = E_{ab}(p)/E_{ab}(c) = 4/12 = \tfrac13 — consistent: (1, 2) is one third of the way along edge [a, c]. The pixel (2, 2): E_{ab} = 4, E_{bc} = 1, E_{ca} = -3 — mixed signs, outside. The same computation, repeated and made incremental (the gradient of E_{ab} is constant), renders tens of millions of triangles per second: rasterization is the arithmetic of finite differences.

Whitted and the synthesis of the two worlds Whitted’s (1980) “ray tracing” follows mirror and refraction rays and then stops: perfect reflections, hard shadows — already out of reach for pure rasterization. Current engines are hybrid: rasterization for primary visibility (fast, memory-coherent), rays on demand for soft shadows, reflections, approximate global illumination (chapter 9); path tracing (chapter 7) remains the reference for architectural rendering and animated film — when render time is counted in hours per frame.

4. The rendering equation

Radiance is the quantity to compute; this chapter writes the equation it satisfies at every surface point. The equation is recursive — the light leaving a point depends on the light leaving all the others — and its solution is a series of bounces.

Definition 4.1 — The light transport equation (LTE) At a surface point x with normal \mathbf{n}, the outgoing radiance L_o(x, \omega_o) in a direction \omega_o of the hemisphere equals (Kajiya, 1986): L_o(x, \omega_o) = L_e(x, \omega_o) + \int_{\Omega^+} L_i(x, \omega_i)\, f_r(x, \omega_i \to \omega_o)\, \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i, with L_e the self-emission, L_i the incident radiance, f_r the BRDF (chapter 5) and \cos\theta_i = \omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n} the projection factor. Writing L_i(x, \omega) = L_o(r(x, -\omega), \omega) (the ray traced backwards), the equation becomes an integral over surfaces: it is a Fredholm equation of the second kind, L = L_e + T\,L, \qquad T\ \text{the transport operator (one bounce)}.

Theorem 4.2 — Bounce series (Neumann) and convergence If the materials have bounded albedo \rho = \sup \int_{\Omega^+} f_r \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i < 1, then T is a contraction in the sup norm and the unique solution of the LTE is the series L = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} T^k L_e = \underbrace{L_e}_{\text{emission}} + \underbrace{T L_e}_{1^{\text{st}}\ \text{bounce}} + \underbrace{T^2 L_e}_{2^{\text{nd}}\ \text{bounce}} + \dots with truncation error \|T^{k+1} L\|_\infty \leq \rho^{k+1} \|L\|_\infty — geometric decay of the weight of the bounces.

Proof. For every bounded function f on rays, \|T f\|_\infty \leq \sup_x \int_{\Omega^+} f_r \cos\theta_i d\omega_i \cdot \|f\|_\infty \leq \rho \|f\|_\infty. By induction \|T^k L_e\|_\infty \leq \rho^k \|L_e\|_\infty: the series converges in norm (geometric series of ratio \rho < 1), and L = \sum T^k L_e satisfies L = L_e + T L by summing. Uniqueness follows from contractivity: if L, L' are solutions, \|L - L'\|_\infty = \|T(L - L')\|_\infty \leq \rho \|L - L'\|_\infty forces L = L'. ∎

Example 4.3 — Two surfaces face to face: the series in action Two large parallel diffuse planes of albedo \rho = 0.5 face each other; the first one also emits L_e = 1. Each plane sees only the other, so a perfectly diffuse plane of albedo \rho receiving a uniform radiance L sends back the radiance \rho L (this is what albedo means; ch. 5 derives the corresponding BRDF \rho/\pi). The radiance leaving the receiver is \rho times what the emitter sends, i.e. L_r = \rho\,(L_e + \rho L_r), whence L_r = \frac{\rho L_e}{1 - \rho^2}. Expanded, this is the bounce series: L_r = \rho + \rho^3 + \rho^5 + \cdots, i.e. 0.5 (first bounce) + 0.125 (one more round trip) + 0.031 + \cdots \approx 0.667. With \rho = 0.9 (white paint) the same formula gives L_r = 0.9/0.19 \approx 4.7: more than five times the direct contribution 0.9, and the tenth round trip still carries 0.81^{10} \approx 12\% of the first one. Interreflections dominate direct lighting in a white room (a white alcove looks brighter than a single wall — and the colour of one surface tints its neighbours: the “colour bleeding” of global illumination).

Alternative forms Depending on the context the LTE is rewritten: as a surface integral (integrate L_i over all visible surfaces, with the visibility factor V(x \leftrightarrow y) and the geometry G = \cos\theta_x \cos\theta_y / \|x - y\|^2) — useful for sampling emissive surfaces (next event estimation, chapter 7); or as a flight equation (volume rendering, chapter 10: the emission-scattering term \sigma_s \int p\, L replaces the BRDF). Changing the formulation = changing the sampling strategy.

5. BRDFs and material models

The rendering equation contains one function that describes the material: the BRDF. This chapter defines it, states the two physical constraints it must satisfy, and presents the models used in practice, from the matte Lambertian surface to microfacets; it ends with textures, which make the parameters of these models vary over the surface.

Definition 5.1 — BRDF The bidirectional reflectance distribution function f_r(x,\ \omega_i \to \omega_o) relates incident and outgoing radiance: L_o(x, \omega_o) = \int_{\Omega^+} L_i(\omega_i)\, f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i \quad (\text{units } \mathrm{sr}^{-1}). Two physical properties: (i) Helmholtz reciprocity: f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o) = f_r(\omega_o \to \omega_i); (ii) energy conservation: $ f_r(_i _o) _o, d_o $ for every \omega_i (the albedo of chapter 4), and f_r \geq 0.

Theorem 5.2 — The Lambertian model is f_r = \rho/\pi A perfectly diffuse surface (identical outgoing radiance in all directions) with albedo \rho \in [0, 1] has the constant BRDF f_r = \frac{\rho}{\pi}.

Proof. By assumption L_o is constant; the exitance equals M = \int_{\Omega^+} L_o \cos\theta_o\, d\omega_o = L_o \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi = L_o \pi. The received irradiance is E = \int L_i \cos\theta_i d\omega_i, and L_o = f_r E. The definition of albedo (M = \rho E) gives \pi f_r E = \rho E, hence f_r = \rho/\pi. The factor \pi disappears: integrating a constant against \cos\theta over the hemisphere produces \pi — it will return in the cosine sampling of chapter 7. ∎

Definition 5.3 — Phong, Blinn-Phong, microfacets

  • Phong (1975): f_r = \tfrac{\rho_d}{\pi} + k_s\, \dfrac{n+2}{2\pi}\, \cos^n\alpha with \alpha the angle to the mirror direction — the empirical specular. The factor \frac{n+2}{2\pi} is a normalization: at normal incidence the mirror direction is the normal, \alpha = \theta_o, and \int_{\Omega^+} \cos^n\theta\,\cos\theta\,d\omega = 2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^{n+1}\theta\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi}{n+2}, so the lobe reflects exactly the fraction k_s of the incident energy whatever its sharpness n; Blinn-Phong replaces \alpha by the angle to the half vector \mathbf{h} = \mathrm{norm}(\omega_i + \omega_o);
  • Microfacets (Cook–Torrance, 1982): the surface is a field of micro-mirrors; f_r = \frac{D(\mathbf{h})\, G(\omega_i, \omega_o)\, F(\omega_i)} {4\, \cos\theta_i\, \cos\theta_o}, with D the distribution of normals (GGX/Trowbridge-Reitz: heavy tails = realistic “blurry” reflections), G a masking-shadowing factor (Smith) and F the Fresnel coefficient. This is the family of standard PBR materials (film and games); layered models (Disney/Burley 2012) add a “sheen” component and a retroreflection lobe to it.

Example 5.4 — Schlick’s Fresnel, in numbers Schlick’s approximation: F(\theta) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \cos\theta)^5, with F_0 the reflectance at normal incidence. Typical dielectric (F_0 = 0.04): F(0°) = 0.04, F(45°) \approx 0.05, F(60°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.5^5 \approx 0.07, F(85°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times (1 - 0.087)^5 \approx 0.65. Physical reading: all surfaces become mirrors at grazing incidence — hence the shiny edges of water or varnish. For gold (F_0 \approx (1.00,\ 0.71,\ 0.29)), even at normal incidence the reflected light is already tinted: the metal “colors” its reflection, a dielectric does not.

Textures: mapping, perspective-correct interpolation, filtering

A BRDF with constant parameters gives plastic-looking objects. Real surfaces vary from point to point — the albedo of wood grain, the roughness of a scratched metal, the small-scale relief of a brick wall — and this variation is stored in textures: images whose texels are read during shading to set the parameters of the material model (albedo, roughness, metalness, normal perturbation).

Definition 5.5 — Texture mapping Each vertex of a mesh carries texture coordinates (u, v) \in [0, 1]^2 (a UV parameterization of the surface, made by an artist or computed by unwrapping). At a shading point inside a triangle, (u, v) is interpolated from the three vertices with the barycentric coordinates of theorem 3.2 (in ray tracing they come from the intersection test, definition 6.1), and the texture is read at (u, v): by nearest texel, or by bilinear interpolation of the four surrounding texels, T(u, v) \approx (1-a)(1-b)\,T_{00} + a(1-b)\,T_{10} + (1-a)b\,T_{01} + ab\,T_{11}, where (a, b) \in [0,1)^2 is the fractional position between texel centres. Normal maps store a perturbation of the shading normal, displacement maps an actual offset of the geometry.

Proposition 5.6 — Perspective-correct interpolation (proved) On a triangle seen in perspective, an attribute u that is affine on the 3D triangle is not affine in screen coordinates; but 1/Z and u/Z are, where Z is the camera-space depth. A rasterizer therefore interpolates 1/Z and u/Z linearly with the screen-space barycentrics and recovers u = (u/Z)/(1/Z) at each pixel.

Proof. Take the camera at the origin and the pinhole projection x_s = X/Z, y_s = Y/Z (ch. 2). The triangle lies in a plane aX + bY + cZ = d with d \neq 0 (the plane does not contain the camera). Dividing by Z: a x_s + b y_s + c = d/Z, so 1/Z = (a x_s + b y_s + c)/d is affine in (x_s, y_s). An affine attribute u = \alpha X + \beta Y + \gamma Z + \delta can be rewritten on the plane, using 1 = (aX + bY + cZ)/d, as u = \alpha' X + \beta' Y + \gamma' Z with \alpha' = \alpha + \delta a/d, \beta' = \beta + \delta b/d, \gamma' = \gamma + \delta c/d; dividing by Z gives u/Z = \alpha' x_s + \beta' y_s + \gamma', affine in (x_s, y_s). ∎

Example 5.7 — The texture that swims, and the mipmap level Interpolation. A segment goes from depth Z = 1 (where u = 0) to Z = 3 (where u = 1). At its midpoint on screen, 1/Z interpolates to \tfrac12(1 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac23, i.e. Z = 1.5, and u/Z to \tfrac12(0 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac16, so u = \tfrac16 / \tfrac23 = \tfrac14; naive screen-space interpolation would give u = \tfrac12, and the texture would slide over the surface as the camera moves. Filtering. When a 1\,024 \times 1\,024 texture covers a quad only 128 pixels wide, each pixel spans \rho = 8 texels and a single bilinear lookup aliases (moiré, shimmering). Mipmapping (Williams 1983) precomputes the pyramid of textures downsampled by 2, 4, 8, \dots — an extra memory of \sum_{k \geq 1}4^{-k} = \tfrac13 — and reads level \lambda = \log_2\rho = 3, the 128 \times 128 version, where one texel matches one pixel; trilinear filtering blends the two nearest levels when \lambda is not an integer, and anisotropic filtering takes several samples along the elongated footprint of oblique surfaces. Reading a texture is again the estimation of a pixel integral (ch. 8): mipmaps are a prefiltered answer to it.

6. Spatial structures and shadows: intersection, BVHs, visibility queries

A ray tracer (ch. 3) — and the path tracer of ch. 7 even more — spends most of its time asking which triangle a ray hits first, or whether anything blocks the segment towards a light. This chapter makes these queries cheap: a fast ray-triangle test, then a hierarchy of boxes that discards most of the scene without testing it; it ends with shadows, the visibility queries between a point and a light, in ray tracing and in rasterization.

Definition 6.1 — Ray-triangle intersection (Möller–Trumbore) For a ray o + t\mathbf{d} and a triangle (v_0, v_1, v_2), one solves o + t\mathbf{d} = v_0 + u\,(v_1 - v_0) + v\,(v_2 - v_0) in (t, u, v): a 3 \times 3 linear system solved by Cramer’s rule (2 cross products and a few divisions), the triangle being hit iff t > 0, u, v \geq 0, u + v \leq 1 — and the test returns the barycentrics of Theorem 3.2 for free (interpolation of normals and UVs). Cost: a few tens of floating-point operations, without branching until the final tests; together with the fact that any surface can be approximated by triangles, this explains the universal conversion of scenes into triangle meshes.

Definition 6.2 — Bounding volume hierarchies (BVHs) A BVH is a binary tree whose every node carries the bounding box (AABB) of its triangles; a ray traversal descends a node iff its box is hit (slab test: intersection of 3 intervals, ~12 comparisons); the leaves test their triangles. Average cost: O(\log n) boxes for n triangles (well-distributed scenes), versus O(n) brute force; construction O(n \log^2 n) (median), in practice almost O(n \log n). The alternatives: kd-trees (splitting planes, optimal for static scenes, expensive construction) and uniform grids (uniform scenes, “teapot in a stadium” destroys them). The BVH wins in animation: refitting the tree when the boxes move is nearly free.

Theorem 6.3 — SAH heuristic: area governs probability For a ray of uniform random direction, the probability of hitting an AABB is proportional to its surface area; P[\text{hit } B] = \frac{A(B)}{A(B_{parent})}. From this follows the surface area heuristic (SAH) for choosing a split: \mathrm{cost}(x) = C_{trav} + P_L(x)\, C_L(x) + P_R(x)\, C_R(x), \qquad P_{L,R} \propto A(B_{L,R}), and one minimizes \mathrm{cost} over the candidate positions — the optimal kd/BVH partition to first order.

Proof. (Argument, for random lines.) Model the rays as random lines, isotropic in direction and uniform in position, conditioned on meeting B_{parent}. For a fixed direction \omega, the lines of direction \omega that meet a convex body K are those crossing the orthogonal projection of K on a plane perpendicular to \omega: their measure is the projected area of K. By Cauchy’s formula, the projected area of a convex body averaged over all directions equals a quarter of its surface area, A(K)/4. The measure of the lines meeting K is therefore proportional to A(K), and since every line meeting B \subset B_{parent} also meets B_{parent}, P = A(B)/A(B_{parent}). The cost formula then adds the expectations (linearity): traversal paid once, plus the intersection cost of the children weighted by their probabilities. ∎

Example 6.4 — Choosing a split with SAH, round numbers The parent box is a cube of edge 2 (area A = 24) containing 8 triangles: 5 clustered in the slab x \in [-1, -0.5] and 3 in the slab x \in [0.5, 1], nothing in between; each cluster spans the full 2 \times 2 extent in y and z, and c_i is the cost of one ray-triangle test. Median split by triangle count (4 and 4, sorted along x): the left child holds 4 triangles of the first cluster, in a box 0.5 \times 2 \times 2 of area 2(1 + 1 + 4) = 12; the right child holds the fifth triangle of that cluster and the 3 others, so its box is the whole cube, of area 24. Hence \mathrm{cost} = C_{trav} + \tfrac{12}{24}\cdot 4\,c_i + \tfrac{24}{24}\cdot 4\,c_i = C_{trav} + 6\,c_i. SAH split between the clusters: two boxes of area 12 holding 5 and 3 triangles, \mathrm{cost} = C_{trav} + \tfrac{12}{24}\cdot 5\,c_i + \tfrac{12}{24}\cdot 3\,c_i = C_{trav} + 4\,c_i. The median balances the triangle counts; SAH weighs each child by the probability that a ray enters it, and here leaves the empty slab out of both children — it “cuts through the void” and saves a third of the intersection cost. On real scenes, with triangles concentrated on surfaces and large empty volumes, this is the typical situation.

In practice: coherence and SIMD The primary rays of an image are coherent (neighboring, close directions): traversing them in packets of 4–64 (SIMD) maximizes the rejection of entire branches of the tree. Diffuse rays (chapter 7) are incoherent: they are processed one by one, or by dynamic regrouping. These implementation details make a 10× in performance — this is where the “real time” of chapter 9 is played out.

Shadows: visibility between a point and a light

A shadow is a visibility question: is the light source visible from the shading point? In the rendering equation it appears as the visibility factor V(x \leftrightarrow y) \in \{0, 1\} of the surface form (ch. 4). Ray tracing answers it with one more ray; rasterization needs a second rendering pass from the light. Both are queries against the scene, answered by the same structures as primary visibility: a shadow ray traverses the BVH of definition 6.2 like any other ray.

Definition 6.5 — Shadow rays, hard and soft shadows A shadow ray from the shading point x to a point y of a light returns V(x \leftrightarrow y); it is an any-hit query — the traversal can stop at the first intersection found before y, which makes it cheaper than the closest-hit query of primary rays. A point light gives hard shadows (V is 0 or 1). An area light gives soft shadows: the irradiance from the light involves \int_{\text{light}} V(x \leftrightarrow y)\,G(x, y)\,L_e\,dA_y, the penumbra being the region where only a fraction of the light is visible; it is estimated by shooting several shadow rays to random points of the light — next event estimation (ch. 7, definition 7.4).

Example 6.6 — Penumbra width, by similar triangles A light of width w_L = 0.5 m, an occluder edge at distance d_o = 2 m from the light and a receiving floor at distance d_r = 3 m from the light. Lines from the two ends of the light through the occluder edge reach the floor at points w_L(d_r - d_o)/d_o = 0.5 \times 1/2 = 0.25 m apart: the penumbra is 25 cm wide. It shrinks to 0 when the occluder touches the receiver (d_r = d_o) — contact shadows are sharp — and grows with the size of the light, which is why a point light, and hard shadows, are an approximation.

Shadow mapping (Williams 1978), the rasterization answer: (1) render the scene from the light and store, per texel, the depth of the closest surface — the shadow map; (2) while shading from the camera, transform each point into the light’s view and compare its depth z with the stored z_{\text{map}}: the point is in shadow if z > z_{\text{map}} + b. The bias b prevents shadow acne (a surface shadowing itself through rounding and the finite resolution of the map), but too large a bias detaches shadows from their casters (peter-panning). The finite resolution produces jagged edges, reduced by percentage-closer filtering (PCF: average the binary test over neighbouring texels, which also fakes a penumbra) and by cascaded shadow maps (several maps covering increasing distances from the camera); ch. 9 places these techniques in the real-time budget.

7. Monte Carlo path tracing

The bounce series of ch. 4 is an integral over light paths of unbounded dimension, out of reach of deterministic quadrature. This chapter estimates it by random sampling, proves that the estimator is unbiased, and shows how the choice of the sampling density controls the noise.

Definition 7.1 — The rendering estimator Path tracing estimates the LTE (chapter 4) by sampling paths \mathbf{x} = (x_0, \dots, x_k) from the eye toward the sources, with density p(\mathbf{x}), via \hat{L} = \frac{L_e\, f_r\, \cos\theta\, f_r \cos\theta \cdots}{p(\mathbf{x})} \quad \text{(product of transport factors / probability of the path)}. A path is built bounce after bounce: at each bounce, draw an outgoing direction according to a density p(\omega) (hemispherical uniform, or proportional to \cos\theta/\pi — cosine sampling), accumulate the contributions, terminate with Russian roulette (Definition 7.4). It is an unbiased estimator of the complete bounce series — in contrast with deterministic methods (radiosity, photon mapping: biased but less noisy).

Theorem 7.2 — Unbiased, and variance in \sigma/\sqrt{N} (i) \mathbb{E}\big[f(X)/p(X)\big] = \int_D f as soon as p > 0 on the support of f. (ii) For N i.i.d. samples, the mean estimator \hat{I}_N satisfies \mathrm{Var}(\hat{I}_N) = \mathrm{Var}(f(X)/p(X))/N and \hat{I}_N \to I almost surely. To divide the typical error by 2, you need 4 times more samples — the curse of Monte Carlo (and its salvation: the -1/2 exponent is independent of the dimension, hence the triumph of MC in rendering, with its infinite dimension of paths).

Proof. (i) \mathbb{E}[f/p] = \int (f/p)\, p = \int f. (ii) the variance of an independent sum adding up, \mathrm{Var}(\hat I_N) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum \mathrm{Var} = \mathrm{Var}/N — and the strong law of large numbers gives the convergence. For the typical error, the CLT gives \hat I_N - I \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathrm{Var}/N): standard deviation in \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/N}. ∎

Theorem 7.3 — Importance sampling: the zero-variance optimum Among the densities p, the variance of f(X)/p(X) is minimal for p^\ast \propto |f|, and it is zero when f \geq 0; in practice one chooses p close to f where f is large. The module’s perfect example: for I = \int_{\Omega^+} \cos\theta\, d\omega = \pi (point-to-point form factor), uniform sampling (p = 1/2\pi) gives the estimator 2\pi\cos\theta, of variance \pi^2/3; cosine sampling (p = \cos\theta/\pi) gives the constant estimator \pi — exactly zero variance.

Proof. For f \geq 0, p^\ast = f/\int f gives f/p^\ast = \int f for every draw: a constant, hence zero variance. (For a signed f, p^\ast = |f|/\int|f| gives f/p^\ast = \pm\int|f|, which is the minimal variance but not zero.) For the uniform sampling computation: \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int (2\pi\cos\theta)^2 \frac{d\omega}{2\pi} = 2\pi \int_{\Omega^+}\cos^2\theta\, d\omega = 2\pi \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{3}, and \mathbb{E}[X]^2 = \pi^2, hence \mathrm{Var} = \frac{4\pi^2}{3} - \pi^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} (using \int_{\Omega^+}\cos^2\theta\,d\omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi = \frac{2\pi}{3}). For the cosine: f/p = \cos\theta / (\cos\theta/\pi) = \pi for every draw. ∎

Definition 7.4 — The three ingredients of a real path tracer

  • Next Event Estimation (NEE): at each bounce, connect the point directly to the emissive sources (shadow ray) instead of waiting for a path to hit them by chance — multiple importance sampling (MIS, Veach): combine the “BRDF” strategy and the “light” strategy with balance weights;
  • Russian roulette: at each bounce, continue the path with probability q (for instance the albedo of the point) and stop it otherwise, dividing the contribution of the surviving paths by q. The estimator stays unbiased, since q \cdot \frac{X}{q} + (1 - q)\cdot 0 = X in expectation, and the number of bounces is geometric with mean \frac{1}{1-q} — the geometric series of Theorem 4.2, sampled. With q = 0.8, paths have 5 bounces on average, and only 0.8^{20} \approx 1\% of them exceed 20 bounces;
  • Sampling of extended lights: a 1\,\mathrm{m}^2 panel seen from 3 m subtends a solid angle of at most about 1/9 sr, about 2\% of the hemisphere, and less at oblique incidence — a direction drawn at random over the hemisphere rarely hits it. Drawing points on the source (weighted by its area, the cosines and the squared distance) instead makes every shadow ray count; this often makes the difference between a render still noisy after minutes and one that is clean in seconds.

Common mistakes in a first path tracer (i) Forgetting a factor of the estimator: the BRDF, the cosine or the density p — with cosine sampling and a Lambertian BRDF, \frac{f_r \cos\theta}{p} = \frac{(\rho/\pi)\cos\theta}{\cos\theta/\pi} = \rho, a useful check. (ii) Self-intersection (“shadow acne”): a secondary ray starting exactly on the surface hits it again because of rounding; offset its origin by a small \varepsilon along the normal. (iii) Counting the light twice: with next event estimation, the emission met by a bounce ray must not be added again for the same light, unless the two strategies are combined with MIS weights. (iv) Averaging in the display space after tone mapping instead of in linear radiance (ch. 8).

Connections with the rest of the curriculum Estimating the LTE is a high-dimensional integration problem — exactly the setting of Wasserstein distances and their sampling estimators (cf. Optimal Transport module: importance sampling, concentration, curse of dimensionality); MIS is a special case of combining estimators (variance minimization under an unbiasedness constraint). The same proofs serve rendering and learning; only the integrand changes.

8. Noise, denoising, tone mapping

A Monte Carlo render is noisy, and its values are physical radiances that no screen can display directly. This chapter covers the two last steps of the pipeline: removing the noise at a controlled bias, and mapping high-dynamic-range radiance to displayable pixel values.

Definition 8.1 — The noise/time budget An MC render at N samples per pixel (spp) has noise \propto 1/\sqrt{N}; the mean squared error decomposes as bias² + variance; pure path tracing is unbiased (variance only), truncated-bounce or photon mapping methods trade a little bias for a lot of variance. Typical numbers: interior with sky through the window — 256 spp: visible noise in the corners; 4096 spp: “clean” for cinema; 16–64 spp: the target of real-time engines — then denoising.

Definition 8.2 — Three families of denoising

  • Geometric kernel filters: weighted average by distances (position, normal, depth, albedo) — the bilateral filter and its multi-scale “à-trous” cascade (5 levels, increasing bias, decreasing variance); still the standard of architectural rendering;
  • Rendered signal decomposition: separate albedo × lighting before filtering (the noise lives mostly in the lighting); filter then recompose — free texture edges gained;
  • Learning: temporal recurrent network (Chaitanya et al., 2017) accumulating the frames of an interactive render; OIDN / OptiX denoiser: CNNs trained on (noisy, reference) pairs with auxiliary buffers — from a few samples per pixel, learned denoisers produce images visually close to high-sample references, and they are the essential link of real time (chapter 9).

Example 8.3 — Measuring the gain Uniform patch of mean radiance \bar{L} = 0.5, noise standard deviation \sigma = 0.15 at 16 spp (SNR ≈ 10.5 dB): at 64 spp, \sigma = 0.075 (SNR ≈ 16.5 dB, +6 dB at each quadrupling); a bilateral filter over 9² neighbors of the same normal and albedo divides the variance by ~81 (81 effective samples): SNR ≈ 29 dB at 16 spp — the equivalent of 1300 spp. This is the weapon of real time: the filter “knows” the patch is flat, MC does not. The difficulty is at edges: different normals ⟹ fewer valid neighbors ⟹ residual noise; hence the multi-scale cascades.

Definition 8.4 — Tone mapping and gamma Radiance is HDR (ratio 10⁴–10⁸ between shadow and sun); the screen is LDR (8 bits, gamma 2.2); the eye is relative (approximately logarithmic response). Tone mapping compresses the scale: Reinhard operator L_d = L/(1 + L) (global), local extensions (block-wise dodge and burn), or artistic curves (filmic ACES). Then gamma correction: V_{enc} = V_{lin}^{1/2.2}. Classic mistake: filtering (antialiasing, mipmaps, denoising) in sRGB space — nonlinear: the averages are wrong; the whole pipeline of this module works in linear space and converts only at the output.

Aliasing is noise too Antialiasing by stochastic supersampling (per-pixel jitter, code chapter) is an MC estimator of the pixel integral — same theory as chapter 7; deterministic aliasing (sample at the center) is a quadrature bias that turns into periodic moiré patterns. Rule: as soon as an integral is difficult, jitter it — Gaussian noise is visually preferred over structural moiré.

9. Real time: GPU, hybrid, upscaling

Film rendering spends minutes per image; a game has about 16 ms. This chapter shows how the same equations are solved under that budget: a hardware rasterization pipeline programmed with shaders, a few rays where they matter most, learned reconstruction to fill in the rest, and the classical approximations of global illumination.

Definition 9.1 — The hardware graphics pipeline Hardware rasterization (OpenGL/Vulkan/DirectX) chains: vertex shading (transformations, chapter 2) ⟹ assembly/clipping ⟹ rasterization (Theorem 3.2 in silicon) ⟹ pixel/fragment shading (materials, chapter 5) ⟹ z-buffer ⟹ framebuffer. Coherence exploited: triangles cover blocks of contiguous pixels — massive parallelism (thousands of SIMT cores), hierarchies of early depth rejection. Budget: 16.6 ms/frame (60 Hz) for everything — geometry, materials, shadows, post-effects. Pre-MC soft shadow techniques remain essential: filtered shadow maps (PCF, variance shadow maps), screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO), precomputed lightmaps — a hierarchy of bias/variance trade-offs.

Definition 9.2 — RTX hybrid and upscaling

  • Hardware ray tracing (RTX, 2018): units dedicated to BVH traversal (chapter 6) — rays on demand: reflections, soft shadows, GI at 1–2 sampled bounces… then denoised (chapter 8) and recombined over rasterization: the hybrid;
  • Temporal upscaling (DLSS, FSR): render at 1080p, display at 4K; the network (or filter) receives the low-resolution frame + the re-projected temporal history (motion) + the geometric buffers; the formulation is a supervised inverse problem — video super-resolution with the constraint set of chapter 8;
  • Game path tracing (Cyberpunk 2077, Alan Wake 2): full MC at 1–4 spp + recurrent denoising + temporal reconstruction: the theory of chapters 7 and 8, in 16 ms.

What “real time” means for an engineer Real time is not a different theory: it is the same set of equations (chapters 1–7) under a budget constraint — and hence a spending strategy: where to accept bias (shadows, approximate GI), where to keep variance (specular reflections, the eye is sensitive there), where to learn (denoising, upscaling). The design of a modern engine is an exercise in managing bias and variance per millisecond of budget — the same trade-offs as in ML, transposed to rendering.

Programming the GPU: shaders

The programmable stages of the pipeline of definition 9.1 are small programs, shaders, written in a C-like language (GLSL for OpenGL, the API of the official course; HLSL, WGSL or SPIR-V elsewhere). The application uploads the geometry (vertex buffers), the textures and the uniform parameters (matrices, light positions), then issues draw calls; the GPU runs the vertex shader once per vertex and the fragment shader once per covered pixel sample, on thousands of threads in parallel. The pair below implements the transformation chain of ch. 2, the perspective-correct interpolation of proposition 5.6 (done by the hardware for every out/in variable), a texture lookup and the Blinn-Phong model of definition 5.3.

// vertex shader
#version 330 core
layout(location = 0) in vec3 position;
layout(location = 1) in vec3 normal;
layout(location = 2) in vec2 uv;
uniform mat4 model, view, projection;
out vec3 fragPos; out vec3 fragNormal; out vec2 fragUV;
void main() {
    vec4 world = model * vec4(position, 1.0);
    fragPos = world.xyz;
    fragNormal = mat3(transpose(inverse(model))) * normal;   // normals transform by the inverse transpose
    fragUV = uv;
    gl_Position = projection * view * world;                 // clip space; the divide by w is done by the GPU
}

// fragment shader
#version 330 core
in vec3 fragPos; in vec3 fragNormal; in vec2 fragUV;
uniform sampler2D albedoMap;                                 // mipmapped texture (example 5.7)
uniform vec3 lightPos, lightColor, camPos;
uniform float ks, shininess;
out vec4 color;
void main() {
    vec3 n = normalize(fragNormal);
    vec3 l = normalize(lightPos - fragPos);
    vec3 v = normalize(camPos - fragPos);
    vec3 h = normalize(l + v);                               // half vector (Blinn-Phong)
    vec3 albedo = texture(albedoMap, fragUV).rgb;            // filtered texture lookup
    float d2 = dot(lightPos - fragPos, lightPos - fragPos);
    vec3 E = lightColor * max(dot(n, l), 0.0) / d2;          // irradiance from a point light
    vec3 fr = albedo / 3.14159265 + ks * (shininess + 8.0) / (8.0 * 3.14159265) * pow(max(dot(n, h), 0.0), shininess);
    color = vec4(fr * E, 1.0);                               // linear radiance; tone map and gamma at the end (ch. 8)
}

The factor (n + 8)/(8\pi) is the usual approximate energy normalization of the Blinn-Phong lobe, the analogue of the (n + 2)/(2\pi) of Phong (definition 5.3). Shadows are added by sampling a shadow map in the fragment shader (ch. 6); compute shaders run general parallel kernels outside the graphics pipeline — denoisers, BVH builds, the image-space passes of the next section.

Global illumination approximations

Path tracing (ch. 7) solves the rendering equation without bias but needs many samples. Long before real-time path tracing, and still today inside most engines, global illumination is approximated: indirect light is smooth over diffuse surfaces, so it can be computed sparsely, precomputed, or replaced by a cheaper quantity — trading bias for speed, as definition 8.1 anticipated.

Definition 9.3 — Ambient occlusion The ambient occlusion at a point x of normal \mathbf{n} is the cosine-weighted fraction of the hemisphere that is not blocked by nearby geometry, \mathrm{AO}(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega^+} V_r(x, \omega)\cos\theta\,d\omega \in [0, 1], where V_r(x, \omega) = 1 if the ray from x in direction \omega meets no surface within a distance r. It is the exact irradiance (divided by \pi) under a uniform white sky when only geometry closer than r casts shadows; engines multiply the ambient term by it to darken creases and contacts. Screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) estimates it from the depth buffer alone, at the price of missing what the camera does not see.

Example 9.4 — Ambient occlusion at the foot of a wall, exact and sampled A point on the floor, right at the foot of an infinite vertical wall, with r = \infty. The vertical plane of the wall cuts the hemisphere into two halves that are mirror images of each other, and \cos\theta takes the same values on both; the wall blocks one half exactly, so \mathrm{AO} = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2} = \tfrac12. With cosine sampling, p(\omega) = \cos\theta/\pi, the estimator of \mathrm{AO} is V_r(x, \omega) itself (the integrand divided by p, theorem 7.3), so the estimate is the fraction of unoccluded rays: a Bernoulli mean of variance \mathrm{AO}(1 - \mathrm{AO})/N. With N = 16 rays, the standard deviation is \sqrt{0.25/16} = 0.125 — visible noise, which real-time engines blur spatially and temporally (ch. 8).

The other classical approximations, from the most precomputed to the most dynamic:

  • Radiosity (Goral et al. 1984): for purely diffuse scenes, discretize the surfaces into patches of uniform radiosity B_i and solve the linear system B_i = E_i + \rho_i\sum_j F_{ij}B_j, where the form factor F_{ij} is the fraction of the energy leaving patch i that reaches patch j — the rendering equation of ch. 4 projected onto piecewise constant functions; its solution is view-independent and can be baked into light maps.
  • Irradiance caching (Ward et al. 1988): compute the indirect diffuse irradiance accurately at sparse points, chosen where it varies quickly (near geometry), and interpolate elsewhere; light probes and irradiance volumes store precomputed incident light, often as a few spherical-harmonic coefficients, on a grid through the scene.
  • Photon mapping (Jensen 1996): first trace photons from the lights and store their hits; then estimate reflected radiance by density estimation over the k nearest photons, L_r(x, \omega_o) \approx \sum_p f_r(x, \omega_p \to \omega_o)\,\Delta\Phi_p/(\pi r^2), with r the radius containing them. The estimate is biased (blurred over the radius r) but consistent as the number of photons grows, and it captures caustics that path tracing from the eye finds very hard.
  • Instant radiosity / virtual point lights (Keller 1997): turn the photon hits into many small point lights and render with shadow maps; voxel cone tracing and screen-space reflections are their real-time descendants, now competing with the hybrid ray tracing of definition 9.2.

10. Neural rendering: NeRF and 3D Gaussians

The previous chapters render a scene that is given; this chapter recovers a scene from photographs, by making the renderer differentiable and fitting the scene to the images. Both NeRF and 3D Gaussians rest on the same volume rendering equation, whose discretization is alpha compositing.

Definition 10.1 — The volume rendering equation In a participating medium of density \sigma(x) (extinction, m⁻¹) and colored emission c(x, \omega), the radiance along a ray r(t) = o + t\mathbf{d}, t \in [s, \infty), equals L(r) = \int_s^{\infty} T(t)\, \sigma(r(t))\, c(r(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\Big(\!-\int_s^{t} \sigma(r(u))\, du\Big), where T is the transmittance — the probability that the ray “survives” (without interaction) until t.

Theorem 10.2 — Transmittance solves a linear ODE T is the unique solution of \frac{dT}{dt}(t) = -\,\sigma(r(t))\,T(t), \qquad T(s) = 1, and for \sigma piecewise constant on intervals [t_i, t_{i+1}) of lengths \delta_i, volume rendering discretizes into alpha compositing L \approx \sum_i \Big(\prod_{j<i} (1 - \alpha_j)\Big)\, \alpha_i\, c_i, \qquad \alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}.

Proof. The ODE is first-order linear (chapter 0 recall): setting \Sigma(t) = \int_s^t \sigma(r(u))\,du, d(e^{\Sigma} T)/dt = e^{\Sigma}(\Sigma' T + T') = e^{\Sigma}(\sigma T - \sigma T) = 0, so e^{\Sigma(t)}T(t) is constant equal to T(s) = 1 (since \Sigma(s) = 0): T(t) = e^{-\Sigma(t)}. On an interval of constant \sigma_i, T(t_{i+1}) = T(t_i)\, e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}, hence the interaction probability \alpha_i = 1 - T(t_{i+1})/T(t_i) = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}; summing “interaction at i AND survival before” gives the discrete series (the telescoping products \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j) = T(t_i)). ∎

Definition 10.3 — NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) represents the scene with a neural network F_\theta: F_\theta\big(\gamma(x), \gamma(\mathbf{d})\big) = \big(\sigma(x),\ c(x, \mathbf{d})\big), where \gamma is the positional encoding \gamma(u) = (u, \sin 2^k \pi u, \cos 2^k \pi u)_k — it gives the MLP the high-frequency content it struggles to learn from raw coordinates (the Fourier-feature analysis of Tancik et al., 2020). Training minimizes the photometric error on rays from calibrated photos (known pose, SfM); evaluation renders 100–300 samples per ray and alpha-composites (Theorem 10.2). Result: photorealism, novel views — at the price of hours of training and one second per image; the successors (Instant-NGP and its hash grids encoding space) bring training down to minutes.

Definition 10.4 — 3D Gaussians (splatting) The 3D Gaussian representation (Kerbl et al., 2023) replaces the network with 10⁵–10⁷ anisotropic Gaussians (position, 3D covariance, opacity, spherical-harmonic color); a ray accumulates them by alpha compositing ordered by depth, each Gaussian being projected (rasterized) into a 2D ellipse. The gradients of the covariance make the optimization differentiable and fast (the number of Gaussians adapts: cloning/splitting): training in minutes, rendering in real time — today’s standard for scene capture. The kinship with chapter 3: it is a rasterization (object order!) of volumetric primitives — the two paradigms of chapter 3 are reconciled.

Example 10.5 — Checking alpha compositing by hand Ray crossing 4 equal intervals \delta = 0.5, densities \sigma = (2, 0, 4, 2). Alphas: \alpha_1 = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632, \alpha_2 = 0, \alpha_3 = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865, \alpha_4 = 0.632. Colors c_i = (1, 0.5, 1, 0.25): L \approx 0.632 \cdot 1 + 0.368 \cdot 0 \cdot 0.5 + 0.368 \cdot 0.865 \cdot 1 + 0.368 \cdot 0.135 \cdot 0.632 \cdot 0.25 \approx 0.957, (transmittances: T_1 = 1, T_2 = 0.368, T_3 = 0.368, T_4 = 0.0498) For densities and colours that are constant on each interval, as here, this sum is the exact integral L = \int T\sigma c\, dt (theorem 10.2): there is no quadrature error. When \sigma and c vary inside an interval, evaluating them at a single point per interval, as NeRF does, introduces a systematic quadrature error; drawing the evaluation points at random inside the intervals (stratified sampling) turns most of this error into noise that averages out over training.

Differentiable rendering, the common paradigm NeRF, 3D Gaussians, ML denoising (chapter 8), upscaling (chapter 9): everywhere, a differentiable renderer connects parameters and image, and learning does the rest — the rendering equation becomes the final layer of a network (cf. Machine Learning module, backpropagation chapter; Multimodal GenAI module, chapter on 3D generation). The theory of chapters 1–7 is not replaced: it provides the structure (physics, estimators, bias/variance) that the networks can only exploit.

A complete path tracer in Python

The program below (~70 lines, NumPy only) implements chapters 1 (radiance), 2 (camera), 4–5 (the bounces of the rendering equation, Lambertian materials, shadows), 6 (cosine sampling, next event estimation, a fixed depth of 4 bounces instead of Russian roulette) and 8 (stochastic antialiasing, gamma); its bounces are the terms of the series of Theorem 4.2. Reduce W, H, SPP to test; at full resolution, expect ~1 minute (interpreter): each pixel is an independent MC estimator — parallelization is trivial.

import numpy as np

W, H, SPP = 240, 180, 8
def norm(v): return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

# scene: spheres (center, radius, albedo, albedo rho)
S = [(( 0.0, -100.0, -1.0), 100.0, (0.85, 0.80, 0.75), 0.6),   # ground
     (( 0.0,    1.0, -5.0),   1.0, (0.75, 0.25, 0.20), 0.7),
     ((-2.3,    0.7, -4.2),   0.7, (0.25, 0.70, 0.30), 0.7),
     (( 2.3,    0.7, -4.2),   0.7, (0.25, 0.35, 0.90), 0.7)]
Lp  = np.array([6.0, 9.0, 1.0])      # point light
I   = np.array([180.0, 170.0, 150.0])
SKY = np.array([0.45, 0.60, 0.85])   # sky radiance

def intersect(o, d):
    tbest, ibest = 1e9, -1           # first intersection (naive ch. 6)
    for i, (c, r, _, _) in enumerate(S):
        oc = o - c; b = oc @ d; disc = b*b - (oc @ oc - r*r)
        if disc > 0:
            t = -b - np.sqrt(disc)
            if 1e-3 < t < tbest: tbest, ibest = t, i
    return tbest, ibest

def trace(o, d):
    col, att = np.zeros(3), np.ones(3)          # L = sum of T^k Le (th. 4.2)
    for depth in range(4):                      # ... + implicit roulette
        t, i = intersect(o, d)
        if i < 0:
            return col + att * SKY * max(0.1, d[1])
        c, r, albedo, rho = S[i]
        p = o + t*d; n = norm(p - c)
        # next event estimation: point light + shadow ray (ch. 7)
        Lv = Lp - p; dist = np.linalg.norm(Lv); L = Lv / dist
        if intersect(p + 1e-3*n, L)[0] > dist:
            col += att * rho * albedo / np.pi * I / dist**2 * max(0.0, n @ L)
        # diffuse bounce: cosine sampling (p = cos/pi, th. 7.3)
        g = np.random.normal(size=3)            # uniform direction on the sphere
        d = norm(n + g / np.linalg.norm(g))
        o, att = p + 1e-3*n, att * rho * albedo
    return col

img = np.zeros((H, W, 3))
for y in range(H):
    for x in range(W):
        acc = np.zeros(3)
        for _ in range(SPP):                    # stochastic AA (ch. 8)
            u = (x + np.random.rand()) / W  - 0.5
            v = 0.5 - (y + np.random.rand()) / H
            acc += trace(np.array([0.0, 1.2, 0.0]), norm([u, v, -1.0]))
        img[y, x] = acc / SPP

img = np.clip(img, 0, None) ** (1.0 / 2.2)      # gamma tone map (ch. 8)
img = img / img.max()                           # exposure normalization
with open("render.ppm", "wb") as f:
    f.write(f"P6\n{W} {H}\n255\n".encode())
    f.write((img * 255).astype(np.uint8).tobytes())
