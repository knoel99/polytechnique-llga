0. Reminders: spectrum, surfaces, vector calculus

Every later chapter rests on three pieces of mathematics: the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices, the notion of a surface, and integration by parts on it. This chapter recalls them, with proofs, and sets up the Fourier analogy that the module follows.

L3/M1 prerequisites mobilized

  • Differential calculus: gradient, divergence, Laplacian in \mathbb{R}^2 (\Delta f = \partial_{xx} f + \partial_{yy} f); Green’s formula;
  • Symmetric diagonalization: L = U\Lambda U^\top, orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions — already seen for the graph Laplacian (Graph ML module, ch. 0);
  • Convolution & Fourier (Signal Processing, if you took it — the essentials are recalled at the end of this chapter): the Laplacian is the frequency operator; heat diffusion \partial_t u = \Delta u;
  • GNN (Graph ML module): a mesh is a graph — but a geometric one (3D positions, metric).

Theorem 0.1 — Spectral (reminder with proof) If A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real and there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors: A = U \Lambda U^\top . Moreover the Rayleigh quotient satisfies R(x) = \frac{x^\top A\, x}{x^\top x}, \qquad \lambda_{\min} = \min_{x \ne 0} R(x), \quad \lambda_{\max} = \max_{x \ne 0} R(x).

Proof. Standard sketch. R is continuous on the (compact) unit sphere, so it attains its maximum at some u_1, \|u_1\| = 1.

On the sphere, the critical points of R satisfy Au = R(u)\,u (set the gradient of x^\top A x to zero under the constraint x^\top x = 1, Lagrange multipliers): u_1 is an eigenvector.

Iterate on the orthogonal complement of u_1, which is stable under A by symmetry: \langle Au_1, v\rangle = \lambda_1\langle u_1, v\rangle = 0 . This yields the basis by recursion.

The characterization of \lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max} follows from R(x) = \sum_k \lambda_k c_k^2 with \sum_k c_k^2 = 1 (barycenter of the \lambda_k). ∎

Generalized problem — the one Laplacian object of the page Geometric operators give a pair (L_c, M): a symmetric positive semi-definite stiffness matrix L_c (the cotangent matrix of ch. 1–2) and a positive definite diagonal mass matrix M (vertex areas). One solves L_c\,\phi = \lambda M\phi, \qquad 0 = \lambda_1 < \lambda_2 \le \dots \quad (\text{1-based, as for graphs; connected mesh}). Setting \tilde L = M^{-1/2}L_cM^{-1/2} (symmetric!), thm. 0.1 applies and \phi = M^{-1/2}\tilde\phi; the \phi_k are M-orthonormal.

The smooth Laplacian \Delta_{\mathcal M} = \mathrm{div}\,\nabla is negative semi-definite, and its discrete version is -M^{-1}L_c.

In the labs, libigl returns the negative semi-definite matrix, so negate it once and pass L_c to scipy: L_c = -igl.cotmatrix(v, f), then eigsh(L_c, k, M=M, sigma=-1e-8).

Definition 0.2 — Surface, charts, atlas A surface (2-dimensional manifold) is a set covered by charts: bijections x : U \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \to V \subset \mathcal M whose chart transitions x_2^{-1} \circ x_1 are smooth. The collection of charts is an atlas.

Example: the sphere. Two stereographic charts (from the North pole and the South pole) suffice; on their intersection, the chart transition is the inversion u \mapsto u/\|u\|^2 — smooth.

A function f : \mathcal M \to \mathbb{R} is smooth if f \circ x is in every chart; intrinsic quantities (lengths, areas, Laplacian) do not depend on the chosen chart.

Triangle meshes in one box (for students who did not take Shapes)

  • Arrays: a mesh is stored as V, an n \times 3 float array of vertex positions, and F, an m \times 3 integer array of vertex indices, one row per triangle. A consistent order of the three indices orients each face, and the normal follows the right-hand rule.
  • Half-edge structure: each edge is split into two oriented half-edges. Each half-edge stores its twin, the next half-edge of its face, its origin vertex and its face, so walking around a vertex or a face costs O(1) per step. This is how Shapes ch. 1 and libraries such as OpenMesh or CGAL traverse one-rings; libigl keeps only V and F and builds adjacency arrays on demand.
  • Manifold mesh: every edge belongs to one face (boundary) or two faces (interior), and the faces around each vertex form one fan, a disk or a half-disk. The cotangent Laplacian of ch. 2 assumes this; a non-manifold edge shared by three faces breaks it.
  • Euler characteristic: \chi = |V| - |E| + |F|. For a closed orientable surface of genus g, \chi = 2 - 2g (sphere 2, torus 0), and a closed triangle mesh has 3|F| = 2|E|. Ch. 1 shows that \chi fixes the total curvature.

Proposition 0.3 — Vector calculus: Green’s formula and self-adjointness Gradient (direction of steepest slope), divergence (outgoing flux per unit area), \Delta f = \mathrm{div}\,\nabla f.

Green’s formula (geometric integration by parts), \int_{\Omega} \langle \nabla f, \nabla g\rangle\, dA = -\int_\Omega f\,\Delta g\, dA + \oint_{\partial\Omega} f\,\partial_n g\, ds, is the reason why the Laplacian is symmetric. On a closed surface (without boundary), \int f \Delta g = \int g \Delta f — and this symmetry carries over to the discrete setting (ch. 2).

Convolution and Fourier (reminder). Convolution (f * g)(x) = \int f(x-y)g(y)dy is the unique commutative, bilinear, continuous, translation-invariant product. The Fourier transform diagonalizes it: \widehat{f*g} = \hat f \cdot \hat g, \qquad \widehat{\Delta f} = -\|\omega\|^2\hat f . This whole machinery disappears on a surface (no translation group); the Laplacian — and it alone — plays its role: this is the program of chapters 2–4.

Heat is a low-pass filter (reminder). On \mathbb{R}^n, the heat equation \partial_t u = \Delta u is solved by convolution with a Gaussian, and in Fourier each frequency is damped: u(t) = G_t * u_0, \qquad G_t(x) = (4\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-\|x\|^2/4t}, \qquad \hat u(t,\omega) = e^{-t\|\omega\|^2}\,\hat u_0(\omega). High frequencies die first, so diffusing for a time t keeps the details larger than about \sqrt t. On a surface, \|\omega\|^2 becomes \lambda_k and the damping becomes e^{-t\lambda_k} (Theorem 2.3): the HKS of ch. 3 and DiffusionNet of ch. 6 are built on this filter.

OT in one page (self-study, read before session 5)

Optimal transport (OT) compares two distributions by the cheapest way of moving one onto the other. It appears in ch. 4 (functional maps as transport), Ex. 12 and the first group project; the full treatment is Optimal Transport, ch. 1–5.

  • Kantorovich problem: for discrete measures with weights a \in \mathbb{R}^n, b \in \mathbb{R}^m (each summing to 1) and a cost matrix c_{ij}, \mathrm{OT}(a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a,b)} \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\,c_{ij}, \qquad U(a,b) = \{P \ge 0 : P\mathbf{1} = a,\ P^\top\mathbf{1} = b\}. The coupling P is a soft correspondence; it is a linear program.
  • Wasserstein distance: with c_{ij} = d(x_i, y_j) the optimum is W_1(a, b), a true distance between distributions of points (for example, distributions of HKS descriptors on two shapes). Adding an entropy term -\varepsilon H(P) gives the Sinkhorn algorithm, a few matrix–vector products per iteration.
  • Gromov–Wasserstein: when the two shapes live in different spaces (two poses, no common coordinates), compare their internal distance matrices D^X, D^Y (geodesic or diffusion): \mathrm{GW}(a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a,b)} \sum_{i,j,k,l} \big(D^X_{ik} - D^Y_{jl}\big)^2 P_{ij}P_{kl}. It is a non-convex quadratic problem, solved by iterated linearisation.
  • In code (POT library): ot.emd2(a, b, cost), ot.sinkhorn2(a, b, cost, reg) and ot.gromov.gromov_wasserstein2(DX, DY, a, b).

1. Discrete differential geometry

A computer stores a surface as a mesh of triangles, while curvature, gradients and the Laplacian are defined on smooth surfaces. This chapter transposes these notions to meshes. A concrete scene shows why they matter: to align two scans of the same object, raw XYZ coordinates work when the pose is fixed but break as soon as the object bends; we need intrinsic quantities, which do not change under such deformations.

What breaks Three classic failures: extrinsic features (coordinates, normals) that change when the shape bends without stretching; a badly discretized Laplacian on a non-manifold mesh; and too few eigenmodes to resolve the details one needs.

Definition 1.1 — Triangulated surface A mesh \mathcal M = (V, E, F): vertices v_i \in \mathbb{R}^3, edges, triangular faces. It is both a graph and an approximation of a smooth surface; the mixed Voronoi area A_i associates a “zone” with each vertex.

Construction 1.2 — Barycentric Voronoi areas (computation) Each vertex i receives, within each incident triangle, the portion of the Voronoi cell contained in that triangle. Summed up, the dual area is written per edge: A_i = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j \sim i}\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big)\,\|v_i - v_j\|^2, where \alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij} are the angles opposite to edge (i,j).

Check on a regular triangular lattice of side a: each vertex has 6 neighbors, each edge has two opposite angles of 60°, hence \cot\alpha + \cot\beta = 2/\sqrt3 per edge and A_i = \frac{1}{8}\cdot 6\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\,a^2 = \frac{\sqrt3\,a^2}{2} = 6 \times \frac{A_T}{3}, \qquad A_T = \frac{\sqrt3\,a^2}{4}, that is, exactly one third of the area of each of the six incident triangles.

For obtuse triangles, the per-edge formula can become negative: one switches locally to the barycenter (“mixed”, Meyer et al. 2003).

Discrete curvature: the Gauss angle deficit K_i = \frac{2\pi - \sum_{f \ni i}\theta_{i,f}}{A_i} (flat ⇒ K = 0; cone ⇒ positive; saddle ⇒ negative). This is a total measure of Gaussian curvature over the zone A_i.

Proof. Why 2\pi minus the sum of the angles? Transport a tangent vector parallel to itself around vertex i, staying on the faces. Unfold the incident faces one after the other into the plane, so that inside each face and across each edge the vector does not rotate (the faces are flat).

The unfolded faces cover a total angle \sum_f \theta_{i,f} instead of 2\pi. So when the loop closes, the final vector makes with the initial one an angle equal to the deficit: 2\pi - \sum_f \theta_{i,f} .

Now on a smooth surface, parallel transport around a loop encircling a total Gaussian curvature \int K\,dA rotates by exactly this angle (local Gauss–Bonnet theorem, “transport” equivalent of the theorema egregium: curvature is intrinsic). The deficit is therefore the discrete analogue of \int_{A_i} K\,dA, hence the density K_i. ∎

Theorem 1.3 — Discrete Gauss–Bonnet (statement + numerical example) \sum_{i \in V} K_i\, A_i \;=\; \sum_i \Big(2\pi - \sum_{f\ni i}\theta_{i,f}\Big) \;=\; 2\pi\,\chi(\mathcal M), \qquad \chi = |V| - |E| + |F|. The total curvature depends only on the topology.

Example: the cube. Eight vertices, three squares (faces split into triangles: angles of \pi/2) meet at each vertex. The deficit per vertex and the total are 2\pi - 3\times\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 8 \times \frac{\pi}{2} = 4\pi = 2\pi \times 2 — the characteristic of the sphere.

A useful counterexample: the torus (donut) has \chi = 0: however it is meshed, the positive and negative deficits cancel out exactly.

Principal curvatures, mean curvature: via the Laplacian of positions At each point, the normal sections of the surface have extreme curvatures k_1 \le k_2 (principal); Gaussian curvature K = k_1k_2 (the deficit approximates it); mean curvature H = \frac{k_1+k_2}{2}.

Vector version, computable directly on the mesh, with \mathbf{n} the outward unit normal (so that H = 1/r > 0 on a sphere of radius r): \Delta_{\mathcal M}\, \mathbf{x} = -2H\,\mathbf{n} \qquad\text{(positions }\mathbf{x}(p) \text{ viewed as 3 functions on } \mathcal{M}\text{)}. Check on the unit sphere: the coordinate functions are eigenfunctions, \Delta x = -2x, and \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{x}, so H = 1.

Justification (sketch). The vector -\Delta\mathbf{x} is the gradient of area: varying the surface by \varepsilon\,f\,\mathbf{n} changes the area by \varepsilon \int 2Hf\,dA (first variation; minimal surfaces — soap films — are those with H = 0). Computing the same derivative by integration by parts (prop. 0.3) brings out -\Delta\mathbf{x}; comparing the two yields the formula.

In the discrete setting, with the stiffness L_c and the mass M of ch. 0 (so that \Delta \approx -M^{-1}L_c): H_i\,\mathbf{n}_i = \tfrac12\big(M^{-1}L_c\,\mathbf{x}\big)_i . One line of libigl. On a 642-vertex icosphere of radius 1, with the Voronoi mass of the code in ch. 2, it gives H_i = 1 at every vertex, pointing outward to within half a degree; with the barycentric mass, H_i = 1.00 \pm 0.02.

  • Normal of a face: n_f \propto (v_j - v_i) \times (v_k - v_i); vertex normal: area-weighted (or angle-weighted) average;
  • Barycentric coordinates: in a triangle (i,j,k), every point can be written \alpha v_i + \beta v_j + \gamma v_k, \alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1, with \alpha = normalized area of the opposite sub-triangle; they serve to interpolate any function from the vertices to the interior of the faces (a mesh carries values only at vertices);
  • Finite differences vs finite elements: the “FD” route approximates \Delta f by combinations of neighbors (like the graph Laplacian). The “FEM” route projects onto the basis of hat functions \psi_i (1 at v_i, 0 at the other vertices, affine on the faces) and assembles the stiffness matrix L_c and the mass matrix M: (L_c)_{ij} = \int_{\mathcal M} \langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle\, dA, \qquad M_{ij} = \int_{\mathcal M} \psi_i\psi_j\, dA . In practice M is lumped to the diagonal of vertex areas A_i, and the discrete Laplacian is \Delta \approx -M^{-1}L_c. The explicit computation of (L_c)_{ij} on a triangle — an object of ch. 2 — recovers exactly the cotangents: FEM and discrete geometry converge.

Useful jargon The pair (L_c, M) of ch. 0 is called “stiffness/mass” here (the Gaussian curvature keeps the letter K); the convergence of discrete operators toward the smooth Laplace-Beltrami (as the mesh is refined) is subtle — the cotangent one converges in the weak sense (Wardetzky 2007); this is the reason why DiffusionNet (ch. 6, ch. 9) prefers to require only heat operators from the discrete setting.

2. The Laplace-Beltrami operator

On a surface there is no Fourier transform, because there are no translations; the operator that takes its place is the Laplacian of the surface. This chapter builds it on meshes (the cotangent matrix), derives it from an energy, and proves the two properties that make it the central tool of shape analysis: its eigenfunctions act as frequencies, and it does not change when the surface bends without stretching.

Definition 2.1 — Laplace-Beltrami The Laplacian on the surface: \Delta_{\mathcal M} f = \mathrm{div}_{\mathcal M}(\nabla_{\mathcal M} f). In a local chart with metric g = (g_{ab}) (the g_{ab} measure how the chart distorts lengths): \Delta_{\mathcal M} f = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{a,b}\partial_a\!\Big(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{ab}\,\partial_b f\Big), with |g| = \det(g_{ab}) and (g^{ab}) the inverse matrix, which recovers \partial_{xx} + \partial_{yy} when g = I.

In the discrete setting, the cotangent stiffness matrix L_c (symmetric, positive semi-definite): (L_c)_{ij} = \begin{cases} \sum_{l \sim i} w_{il} & i = j\\[2pt] -w_{ij} & j \sim i\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad w_{ij} = \tfrac12\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big), where \alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij} are the angles opposite to edge (i,j). With the lumped mass M = \mathrm{diag}(A_i), the discrete Laplace-Beltrami operator is \Delta \approx -M^{-1}L_c, \qquad (\Delta f)_i \approx \frac{1}{2A_i}\sum_{j \sim i}(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij})\,(f_j - f_i). This area-normalised matrix already contains M^{-1}: the eigenproblem uses L_c and M separately (ch. 0), otherwise the mass is divided twice.

Like the graph Laplacian L = D - A, the matrix L_c is symmetric positive semi-definite. Its Fourier basis \{\phi_k\} solves L_c\phi_k = \lambda_k M\phi_k, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \Delta \phi_k = -\lambda_k \phi_k, with increasing \lambda_k (= the “frequencies” of the surface, 1-based: \lambda_1 = 0).

Derivation 2.2 — The cotangent operator via the Dirichlet energy The starting point is the Dirichlet energy (the “stiffness” of f): E_D(f) = \tfrac12 \int_{\mathcal M} \|\nabla f\|^2\, dA = \tfrac12\sum_{T}\int_T \|\nabla f\|^2\, dA. By approximating f with its barycentric interpolant (affine on each triangle, values f_i at vertices), one gets an exact quadratic form: E_D(f) = \tfrac14 \sum_{i}\sum_{j \sim i} w_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2, \qquad w_{ij} = \tfrac12\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big) (each edge is counted twice, once from each endpoint).

The stiffness L_c is the matrix of this quadratic form, E_D(f) = \tfrac12 f^\top L_c f: \frac{\partial E_D}{\partial f_i} = \sum_{j\sim i} w_{ij}(f_i - f_j) = (L_cf)_i \approx -A_i\,(\Delta f)_i . In words, L_c f is the gradient of the Dirichlet energy, and L_c f = 0 at the interior vertices characterizes the functions of minimal energy (discrete harmonic functions).

Proof. Step 1 — gradient of an affine function on a triangle. On triangle T = (i,j,k), the interpolant is f = f_i\psi_i + f_j\psi_j + f_k\psi_k . Each \nabla\psi is constant on T, orthogonal to the opposite side, with norm 1/h (associated height). In particular \nabla\psi_i + \nabla\psi_j + \nabla\psi_k = 0 .

Step 2 — inner product of two gradients. Let h_i, h_j denote the heights from i and j, and \theta_k the angle at vertex k (opposite to edge (i,j)).

The gradient \nabla\psi_i is orthogonal to side (j,k), points toward i, and \|\nabla\psi_i\| = 1/h_i; likewise \|\nabla\psi_j\| = 1/h_j, orthogonal to side (i,k).

The two sides (j,k) and (i,k) making an angle \theta_k with each other, their normals make an angle \pi - \theta_k, and the pointing directions flip the sign: \langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle = -\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_i h_j} \qquad \text{(constant on } T\text{)}. Then, with |T| the area and |e| the side lengths: \int_T \langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle\, dA = |T|\cdot\Big(-\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_i h_j}\Big) = -\frac{|T|\,|e_{jk}|\,|e_{ik}|}{(2|T|)(2|T|)}\cos\theta_k = -\frac{\cos\theta_k}{2\sin\theta_k} = -\frac12\cot\theta_k, where we used h_i = 2|T|/|e_{jk}|, h_j = 2|T|/|e_{ik}| and then |T| = \tfrac12|e_{jk}|\,|e_{ik}|\sin\theta_k.

Step 3 — assembly. Each edge (i,j) belongs to one or two triangles. Sum over these triangles the terms \tfrac12 f_i f_j \int_T\langle\nabla\psi_i,\nabla\psi_j\rangle and reorganize the quadratic form as \tfrac14\sum_{ij} w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2 (the diagonal terms absorb the off-diagonal ones — exercise 6). The cotangent weights appear, with the contribution -\tfrac12\cot\theta_k of each opposite triangle. ∎

Key result — the cotangent weights w_{ij} = \tfrac12\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big), \qquad (L_cf)_i = \sum_{j\sim i} w_{ij}(f_i - f_j), \qquad \Delta \approx -M^{-1}L_c

Properties (and why they hold)

  • Symmetry: W = (w_{ij}) is symmetric (each edge carries its weight, constructed symmetrically), so L_c = L_c^\top; this is the discrete version of the self-adjointness of ch. 0;
  • Constants annihilated: L_c\mathbf{1} = 0 because the quadratic form only sees the differences f_i - f_j;
  • Linear precision: on a flat mesh, every affine function (in particular each coordinate x, y) satisfies (L_c f)_i = 0 at the interior vertices — a generic f does not;
  • Positivity: E_D(f) \ge 0, zero iff f is constant (on each connected component); hence \lambda_1 = 0 has multiplicity 1 and \lambda_2 > 0 measures the “connectivity” (the Fiedler value of surfaces);
  • Convergence: as the mesh is refined, -M^{-1}L_c converges to \Delta_{\mathcal M} in the weak sense — subject to triangle quality (no too-obtuse angles; Wardetzky 2007);
  • Nodal sets: the zeros of \phi_k cut the surface into increasingly fine regions as k grows — the “wavefronts” of the surface; \lambda_k \sim c\,k (Weyl’s law in dimension 2).

Theorem 2.3 — Heat kernel: spectral construction The solution of the heat equation \partial_t u = \Delta u, u(0) = u_0, reads (recall \Delta\phi_k = -\lambda_k\phi_k with \lambda_k \ge 0) u(t) = e^{t\Delta}u_0 = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\,\langle \phi_k, u_0\rangle\,\phi_k, \qquad h_t(x, y) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\,\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y). Each mode \phi_k is damped by e^{-\lambda_k t}: diffusion = spectral low-pass filtering.

Proof. Direct substitution, term by term (using \Delta\phi_k = -\lambda_k\phi_k): \partial_t\big[e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k\big] = -\lambda_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k = \Delta\big[e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k\big]. At t = 0, the sum reduces to \sum_k\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k = u_0 (orthonormal basis — for the M-weighted inner product of the lab sessions).

The kernel h_t is just the same writing in “integral” form: u(x,t) = \int h_t(x,y)u_0(y)dy (exercise 4). ∎

Proposition 2.4 — Diffusion distances: geometry of the heat kernel D_t^2(x, y) = \|h_t(x, \cdot) - h_t(y, \cdot)\|^2_{L^2} = \sum_k e^{-2\lambda_k t}\,\big(\phi_k(x) - \phi_k(y)\big)^2. Two points are close if their “thermal fingerprints” are close; the exponential weighting short-circuits high frequencies — robust to noise, unlike the geodesic distance. This is the building block of the diffusion geometry of ch. 3.

Theorem 2.5 — Isometric invariance (proved) If T : \mathcal M \to \mathcal N is an isometry (preserves the lengths of curves), then for every f on \mathcal N and every x \in \mathcal M: \Delta_{\mathcal M} (f \circ T)(x) = (\Delta_{\mathcal N} f)(T(x)). Consequence: identical spectra, transported eigenfunctions — the foundation of all non-rigid shape comparison.

Proof. An isometry preserves lengths and areas, hence inner products of gradients and integration: for all u, v on \mathcal N, \int_{\mathcal N} \langle\nabla u, \nabla v\rangle\, dA_{\mathcal N} = \int_{\mathcal M} \langle\nabla(u\circ T), \nabla(v\circ T)\rangle\, dA_{\mathcal M} (change of variable T, Jacobian = 1).

By Green’s formula (prop. 0.3 — self-adjointness), for every v, \int v\,\Delta_{\mathcal N} u = \int (v\circ T)\,\Delta_{\mathcal M}(u\circ T). Since this holds for every test function v\circ T (surjective), the two Laplacians agree pointwise: \Delta_{\mathcal M}(u\circ T) = (\Delta_{\mathcal N} u)\circ T . ∎

Why this is the central tool

  • Diffusion: heat u(t) = e^{t\Delta}u_0 — “Can one hear the shape of a drum?”: the spectrum encodes the geometry (with honest limits, ch. 3);

3. Shape descriptors and distances

To compare or match two shapes we need, at each point, a signature that is computable and unchanged when the shape bends. This chapter builds such descriptors from the spectrum of chapter 2 (HKS, WKS, ShapeDNA), together with intrinsic distances, and states the limit of the whole spectral approach.

Definition 3.1 — Heat Kernel Signature (HKS) The amount of residual heat at x after time t: \mathrm{HKS}(x, t) = h_t(x, x) = \sum_k e^{-t\lambda_k}\, \phi_k(x)^2 — an intrinsic descriptor: stable, isometrically invariant; multi-scale (small t = local detail, large = global). These signatures play the role of “features” for correspondence.

Proof. Stability (proof sketch). Perturb the metric by a relative amount \varepsilon (scan noise, different mesh). Two perturbation bounds are used, the second one in the sup norm and for an isolated eigenvalue: |\delta\lambda_k| \le C\varepsilon\lambda_k \ \ \text{(min–max)}, \qquad \|\delta\phi_k\|_\infty \le \frac{C'\varepsilon\lambda_k B_k}{\mathrm{gap}_k}, \qquad B_k = \|\phi_k\|_\infty, where \mathrm{gap}_k is the distance from \lambda_k to the rest of the spectrum.

To first order in \varepsilon, each term of the HKS varies as \delta\mathrm{HKS}(x,t) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\big(-t\,\delta\lambda_k\,\phi_k(x)^2 + 2\,\phi_k(x)\,\delta\phi_k(x)\big). Bounding each factor pointwise, over the K eigenpairs actually computed: |\delta\mathrm{HKS}(x,t)| \le \varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{-\lambda_k t}\,\lambda_k B_k^2\Big(C\,t + \frac{2C'}{\mathrm{gap}_k}\Big) + O(\varepsilon^2).

  • For a fixed t > 0 this is a finite sum: the HKS is Lipschitz in the metric — hence its empirical robustness. The constant grows as t \to 0 (more modes count) and as gaps close.
  • The gaps are an artefact of the eigenfunction route. The HKS only involves the sum of \phi_k(x)^2 over each eigenspace, which does not change when the basis of that eigenspace rotates; a perturbation argument on spectral projectors replaces \mathrm{gap}_k by the distance between well-separated clusters of eigenvalues.

Note that this computation also justifies the limitation: at large t, only the small \lambda_k contribute (a global, not very discriminative descriptor); at small t, the series is sensitive to high frequencies (noise); hence the choice of a logarithmic grid of t. ∎

  • Scale-normalized HKS: dilating the shape by s gives \lambda_k \to s^{-2}\lambda_k and \phi_k \to s^{-1}\phi_k (unit norm on an area multiplied by s^2), hence \mathrm{HKS}'(x, t) = s^{-2}\,\mathrm{HKS}(x,\, t/s^2). Dividing by \max_x \mathrm{HKS}(x,t) removes the amplitude s^{-2} but not the rescaled time t/s^2. Two fixes:

    • measure time in units of the shape, t = \tau\cdot\mathrm{Area} or t = \tau/\lambda_2, and then normalise the amplitude;
    • the scale-invariant HKS of Bronstein & Kokkinos (2010): sample t = \alpha^\tau, take \log\mathrm{HKS}, differentiate in \tau (removes the amplitude), and keep the modulus of its Fourier transform in \tau (removes the shift in \tau);

  • Wave Kernel Signature (Aubry et al., 2011): a quantum particle of energy e distributed log-uniformly: \mathrm{WKS}(x, e) = \sum_k \phi_k(x)^2 e^{-(\log e - \log\lambda_k)^2/2\sigma^2} — a Gaussian window on a logarithmic scale: each energy probes one precise frequency band (better localization than the HKS, whose every t mixes low frequencies);

  • ShapeDNA (Reuter et al.): the vector (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k) — normalized by area — as a global “fingerprint”; L^2 distance between fingerprints;

  • Intrinsic distances:

    • geodesic (shortest path on the surface): Dijkstra’s algorithm on the graph of the mesh (edge lengths) gives an upper bound — edge paths are only some of the paths on the surface, and their zigzag error does not vanish when a regular mesh is refined;
    • continuous refinement (Fast Marching, Kimmel–Sethian) lets paths cross faces and solves the eikonal equation \|\nabla d\| = 1 by a growing wavefront, which lowers the Dijkstra estimate toward the true distance;
    • the modern “heat method” variant (Crane et al.) first solves heat and then takes its renormalized gradient — O(linear solver);
    • diffusion distance (prop. 2.4): truncated spectral approximations of both;

  • Can one hear the shape of a drum? No — Gordon, Webb, and Wolpert (1992) constructed pairs of planar domains (polygonal drums) that are isospectral but not isometric. The spectrum does not determine the shape: this is a fundamental (and mathematically sound) limit of the whole spectral pipeline — ShapeDNA, spectral constraints of functional maps, HKS.

    In practice: the spectrum discriminates almost everything, and one always complements it with operator and descriptor constraints (ch. 4);

  • Flattening (embedding): the first non-constant eigenfunctions give an embedding x \mapsto (\phi_2(x), \dots, \phi_{k+1}(x)) that “unfolds” the shape (\phi_1 is constant and dropped) — the exact analogue of Laplacian eigenmaps and spectral clustering for graphs (Graph ML, ch. 4; spectral positional encodings in Advanced GNN ch. 4 if you take it): same algebra, different object.

4. Functional maps

Finding which point of one shape corresponds to which point of another is a huge combinatorial problem. This chapter replaces it with a small linear one: instead of matching points, we transport functions, and in the spectral bases of chapter 2 the map becomes a k \times k matrix.

Theorem/Concept 4.1 — Functional Maps (Ovsjanikov et al., 2012) Instead of looking for the point-to-point correspondence (a combinatorial discrete map, costly), transport functions defined on the surfaces: T_F : \mathcal{F}(\mathcal M) \to \mathcal{F}(\mathcal N), \qquad T_F(g) = g \circ T^{-1} Restricted to the spectral bases \Phi_{\mathcal M}, \Phi_{\mathcal N} (the first k eigenfunctions, M-orthonormal), the map becomes a small matrix C \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}: b_{\mathcal N} \approx C\, b_{\mathcal M}, \qquad b = \Phi^\top M f, \qquad C_{ij} = \big\langle \phi_i^{\mathcal N},\, T_F\,\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle, where b holds the spectral coefficients of a function f and M is the mass matrix of the shape it lives on. A correspondence of 10^5 points is replaced by k^2 numbers (k \sim 30): this is the paradigm shift of the field.

Key result — a map between shapes is a small matrix b_{\mathcal N} \approx C\, b_{\mathcal M}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}, \quad b = \Phi^\top M f

Constraints 4.2 — what C must satisfy

  • Descriptor preservation: if D_{\mathcal M} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times q} are the coefficients of q descriptors (HKS, WKS…) on \mathcal M and D_{\mathcal N} on \mathcal N (in the same order): C\,D_{\mathcal M} \approx D_{\mathcal N} \qquad (k \times q \text{ equations});
  • Commutation with operators: C\,\Lambda_{\mathcal M} \approx \Lambda_{\mathcal N}\,C (spectral regularization — the subject of the proof below);
  • Orthogonality: if one believes in an isometry, C^\top C \approx I. An isometry preserves areas, so T_F preserves the L^2 inner products of functions, \langle T_F f, T_F g\rangle_{\mathcal N} = \langle f, g\rangle_{\mathcal M}; in orthonormal bases the matrix of such a map is orthogonal (exactly for complete bases, approximately after truncation to k functions);
  • Correspondence coupling: if a few pairs of points are known, encode them in a 0/1 matrix \Pi with one row per vertex of the second shape and one column per vertex of the first, and \Pi_{yx} = 1 when T(x) = y. Then T_F acts as \Pi on vertex values, and C \approx \Phi_{\mathcal N}^\top M_{\mathcal N}\, \Pi\, \Phi_{\mathcal M} (hard constraint vs least squares).

Proof. Commutation: proved. Assume T : \mathcal M \to \mathcal N is an isometry. Theorem 2.5 says that, for every f on \mathcal N, \Delta_{\mathcal M}(f \circ T) = (\Delta_{\mathcal N} f)\circ T . Since T_F^{-1}f = f \circ T, this is an identity between operators — T_F^{-1} intertwines the two Laplacians — that is T_F\, \Delta_{\mathcal M} = \Delta_{\mathcal N}\, T_F .

Apply it to an eigenfunction f = \phi_j^{\mathcal M} of \mathcal M, with eigenvalue \lambda_j^{\mathcal M}: \Delta_{\mathcal N}\,T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M} = T_F\,\Delta_{\mathcal M}\phi_j^{\mathcal M} = -\lambda_j^{\mathcal M}\,T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M} . The transport of an eigenfunction is an eigenfunction for the same eigenvalue.

In coefficients, apply the self-adjointness of \Delta_{\mathcal N} (prop. 0.3) to the entry C_{ij} defined in 4.1: -\lambda_i^{\mathcal N}C_{ij} = \langle \Delta_{\mathcal N}\phi_i^{\mathcal N}, T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\rangle = \langle \phi_i^{\mathcal N}, \Delta_{\mathcal N}T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\rangle = -\lambda_j^{\mathcal M}C_{ij}. So C_{ij}\,\big(\lambda_j^{\mathcal M} - \lambda_i^{\mathcal N}\big) = 0, which is entry (i,j) of C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} = \Lambda_{\mathcal N} C: C_{ij} \ne 0 only if \lambda_i^{\mathcal N} = \lambda_j^{\mathcal M}.

If the spectrum is simple, T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M} = \pm\phi_j^{\mathcal N} and C is diagonal with entries \pm 1. Real surfaces are only approximately isometric and multiple eigenvalues create blocks, hence a weak constraint, in the least-squares sense. ∎

Resolution 4.3 — least squares and normal equations C^* = \arg\min_C\ \big\|C D_{\mathcal M} - D_{\mathcal N}\big\|_F^2 + \alpha\big\|C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} - \Lambda_{\mathcal N} C\big\|_F^2 + \beta\big\|C^\top C - I\big\|_F^2 + \mu\|C\|_F^2. Without the orthogonality term, the problem separates over the rows of C. The commutativity penalty is a sum over entries, \big\|C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} - \Lambda_{\mathcal N} C\big\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} C_{ij}^2\,\big(\lambda_j^{\mathcal M} - \lambda_i^{\mathcal N}\big)^2, so row i (written as a column c_i) gets its own diagonal penalty and its own normal equations: c_i = \big(D_{\mathcal M} D_{\mathcal M}^\top + \alpha\,\Omega_i + \mu I\big)^{-1} D_{\mathcal M}\, (d_i^{\mathcal N})^\top, \qquad \Omega_i = \mathrm{diag}\big((\lambda_j^{\mathcal M} - \lambda_i^{\mathcal N})^2\big)_{j=1}^{k}, with d_i^{\mathcal N} the i-th row of D_{\mathcal N} — k linear systems of size k \times k, solved in a few milliseconds.

With orthogonality, one projects onto orthogonal matrices (SVD C = U\Sigma V^\top, then C \leftarrow UV^\top — Procrustes projection).

Conversion to point-to-point correspondence + refinement Soft correspondence: P = \Phi_{\mathcal N}\, C\, \Phi_{\mathcal M}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{|V_{\mathcal N}| \times |V_{\mathcal M}|}, \qquad \Pi(x) = \arg\max_y P_{yx} — the matrix P (barely dense, a product of three long matrices) encodes the “probability” that y corresponds to x.

Refinement (the ZoomOut spirit, ch. 9): iteratively,

  1. increase k by one notch;
  2. recompute C using the current hard correspondence as a coupling constraint;
  3. re-derive P.

The growing spectral resolution acts as annealing: large structures settle at small k (strong regularization), details adjust afterwards — algorithm:

C = least_squares(k=k0, descriptors)          # coarse start
for k in range(k0, kmax, step):
    Pi = argmax_per_column(Phi_N[k] @ C @ Phi_M[k].T)   # hard
    C = solve(constraints + coupling(Pi), dim=k)        # soft
return Pi                                          # final correspondence
  • Partial maps: when only a part of \mathcal M corresponds to \mathcal N, T_F becomes a restriction; the matrix C is then rectangular (or one embeds \mathcal M into \mathcal N via a prolongation operator); the constraints adapt (descriptors only on the visible part);
  • Learned extensions: the descriptors D can be produced by a network (FMNet of Litany et al. 2017, then GeomFMaps of Donati et al. 2020 — deep geometric functional maps): the network learns descriptors that are optimal for the matrix C, the resolution remaining a differentiable least squares; the complete computation graph (network → C → correspondence loss) is trained end to end (Architecture 6.3);
  • Supervised vs unsupervised: with annotated pairs (FAUST), one directly supervises C or the geodesic error of \Pi; otherwise, one penalizes the structure that C should have — bijectivity, orthogonality, Laplacian commutativity (Architecture 6.3): unsupervised works well for quasi-isometries (humans);
  • Applications: texture transfer: the texture (a color function!) on \mathcal M is transported by b_{\mathcal N} = C\,b_{\mathcal M} — a matrix multiplication; pose transfer (the correspondence coordinates), morphing;
  • LLGA connection: a functional map ≈ an optimal transport between reduced bases; shape alignment ≈ graph alignment by Gromov-Wasserstein (box OT in one page of ch. 0; Optimal Transport, ch. 5); the three languages — GNN, OT, functional maps — describe the same operation of structural correspondence.

5. Parameterization and processing

Many tasks — texturing, remeshing, editing, reconstruction from a scan — need a surface to be flattened, smoothed or rebuilt. This chapter shows that most of these operations reduce to minimizing an energy built on the Laplacian of chapter 2, hence to solving a sparse linear system.

Harmonic maps — equations proved Flatten a surface with boundary onto the disk \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2: look for u : \mathcal M \to \mathbb{R}^2 minimizing the Dirichlet energy E_D(u) = \tfrac12\int\|\nabla u\|^2 with the boundary fixed on \partial\Omega (unit circle, uniformly — Tutte).

Proof. First variation: for every perturbation \delta u that vanishes on the boundary, 0 = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E_D(u + \varepsilon\,\delta u)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0} = \int_{\mathcal M} \langle\nabla u, \nabla\delta u\rangle\, dA = -\int_{\mathcal M} \delta u\,\Delta u\, dA + \oint_{\partial \mathcal M}\delta u\,\partial_n u\, ds. The boundary term vanishes (\delta u = 0 on \partial \mathcal M). Since \delta u is arbitrary in the interior, the minimizer is harmonic (component by component): \Delta u \equiv 0 .

Injectivity, smooth case: the Radó–Kneser–Choquet theorem says that a harmonic map onto a convex domain, whose boundary map is a homeomorphism onto the convex boundary, is a diffeomorphism (no folding).

In the discrete setting: the discrete Dirichlet energy (derivation 2.2!) leads to the linear system (L_c u)_i = 0 at the interior vertices, with the boundary rows replaced by the constraints — a Poisson solver, a few tens of milliseconds. ∎

Discrete injectivity needs positive weights Tutte’s theorem (1963), extended by Floater (2003), guarantees a fold-free map when the boundary goes to a convex polygon and each interior vertex is a convex combination of its neighbours, i.e. all weights are positive: uniform (Tutte) or mean-value (Floater) weights.

Cotangent weights do not qualify: w_{ij} < 0 as soon as \alpha_{ij} + \beta_{ij} > \pi, which happens on meshes with obtuse triangles, and the discrete cotangent-harmonic map can then fold. Use mean-value weights when injectivity must be guaranteed, or an (intrinsic) Delaunay mesh, on which the cotangent weights of interior edges are non-negative, and positive in the generic case.

  • Conformal: a map is conformal if it preserves angles — the local Jacobian is a similarity du = s\,R (Cauchy–Riemann intuition: real and imaginary parts are conjugate harmonic); it preserves the shape of small details (textures without shear), but distorts areas;

  • LSCM — least-squares conformal maps (Lévy et al. 2002): for a map (u, v), conformality is the Cauchy–Riemann equation \nabla v = R_{90}\nabla u on each face. LSCM minimizes its squared residual, which splits into the Dirichlet energy minus the signed area of the image: E_{\mathrm{LSCM}}(u,v) = \tfrac12\sum_T |T|\,\big\|\nabla_T v - R_{90}\nabla_T u\big\|^2 = E_D(u) + E_D(v) - \mathcal A(u, v). The energy is quadratic in (u,v); its minimizer is only defined up to a similarity, so one pins two vertices and solves one sparse linear system. Unlike Tutte, the boundary is free;

  • ARAP — as-rigid-as-possible: minimize E_{\mathrm{ARAP}}(u) = \sum_{T}\min_{R_T \in SO(2)} \big\|J_T(u) - R_T\big\|_F^2, where J_T(u) is the Jacobian of the map on face T and R_T the “closest” rotation. Local/global solver:

    1. local: each face fixes R_T = UV^\top (SVD of J_T);
    2. global: with the rotations fixed, E becomes quadratic in u → linear system;

    alternate until convergence — a geometric EM; an angle/area compromise without excessive distortion;

  • Spectral smoothing/filtering: attenuate high frequencies (denoise), sharpening — signal processing (M1) transported to surfaces;

  • Deformation: laplacian editing: move handles while preserving \Delta\mathbf{x} of the positions (the details): minimize \sum_i\|(L_c\mathbf{x}')_i - (L_c\mathbf{x})_i\|^2 under constraints — the same machinery as the harmonic map; skinning, morphing — graphics/AR applications of the sister ViCAI module;

  • Poisson reconstruction: from a normal field \mathbf{v} (oriented, scan): look for the indicator function \chi of the solid whose gradient best matches \mathbf{v}. An exact solution would satisfy \nabla\chi = \mathbf{v} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta\chi = \mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}, but a measured \mathbf{v} is generally not a gradient, so the converse fails. The Poisson equation is the normal equation of \min_\chi \int\|\nabla\chi - \mathbf{v}\|^2: it computes the least-squares projection of \mathbf{v} onto gradients. This is the bridge scan → closed mesh;

  • Decimation — quadric error metric: associate to each vertex the quadric (sum of the products of the unit plane equations) Q_i = \sum_{p \in \text{incident planes}} A_p^\top A_p . The cost of contracting edge (i,j) to the point \bar v is Q_{ij}(\bar v) = Q_i(\bar v) + Q_j(\bar v) — a quadratic form, minimized in closed form; one contracts the lowest-cost edges with a binary heap;

  • Curvature-based remeshing: vertex density \propto local curvature (deficit of ch. 1) — allocate the triangle budget where the geometry demands it.

6. Deep learning on surfaces

The networks of image processing assume a regular grid, which point clouds and meshes do not have. This chapter presents the architectures that learn directly on 3D data — PointNet and its local extensions, then DiffusionNet — and the obstacle they all face: a surface has no canonical orientation for a filter.

Architecture 6.1 — PointNet (Qi et al., 2017) Point clouds (without a mesh): per point: shared MLP; global max aggregation (permutation-invariant — the same requirement as GNNs, Graph ML module ch. 2); cloud classification/segmentation: f(X) = \rho\Big(\max_{x_i \in X}\ \gamma(x_i)\Big), \qquad |X| = n,\ x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3.

Proof. Invariance. A permutation \pi reorders the rows (points) of X. The shared MLP \gamma is applied row by row, to each point separately, so it only reorders the rows of \gamma(X); the entrywise max over rows does not depend on their order: f(\pi X) = f(X).

Why max rather than another invariant aggregator?

  • The product \prod_i \gamma(x_i) is likewise permutation-invariant, but numerically disastrous (explodes toward 0 or \infty, unstable multiplicative gradients);
  • the mean is stable but dilutes local signals — a discriminative sub-region representing 1% of the cloud is drowned;
  • the max is selective: it captures “at least one point exhibits the characteristic”, which is exactly the building block of the universal approximation that follows.

Universality (sketch). On a compact set K, a continuous permutation-invariant function can be uniformly approximated by sums of “soft indicator functions” of balls: g(x) \approx \sum_j c_j\,\mathbb{1}_{B(c_j, r_j)}(x). Now, with h_\varepsilon a steep sigmoid, \mathbb{1}_{B(c,r)}(x) \approx h_\varepsilon(\|x - c\|), and \max_{x_i}\big[1 - h_\varepsilon(\|x_i - c\|)\big] equals ~1 as soon as a single point falls in the ball. The union of balls is expressed via the max: PointNet approximates the functions of “membership of at least one point in a ball”, the universal building block for compact sets (thm. 1 of the paper). ∎

PointNet++ and DGCNN — injecting the local PointNet only sees unions of regions (the global max ignores co-localization). Two answers:

  • PointNet++:

    1. farthest point sampling: iteratively choose the point farthest from the already chosen centers (uniform coverage, greedy algorithm in O(n\,k));
    2. ball query: group the neighbors in a ball of fixed radius;
    3. a local PointNet per group, then a hierarchy (progressive subsampling — exactly a spatial pooling);

Geodesic convolutions and the gauge problem A convolution filters a patch (geodesic neighborhood, anisotropic). But on a surface, there is no canonical frame in the tangent plane: where should “north” go? Every choice of gauge (local rotation of the frame) rotates the filter: an anisotropic filter is not well-defined. Three strategies:

  • measure the alignment with an extrinsic direction (the gradient, the normal) — fragile;
  • principal coordinates (eigenframes of the curvature tensor): stable but sign-ambiguous;
  • isotropic filtering (angularly invariant): one gives up anisotropy — this is the spectral answer (filters that are functions of \Delta, cf. ChebNet of the Graph ML module) and… the DiffusionNet answer below.

Architecture 6.2 — DiffusionNet (Sharp, Attaiki, Crane, Ovsjanikov — TOG 2022): the block equations Replace convolution with learned diffusion. Each block maps per-vertex features U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} (column u_c = channel c) to new features of the same width:

  1. Learned diffusion, one time per channel: each channel c has its own learned time t_c > 0, and h_c = e^{t_c\Delta}u_c = \Phi\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{-\lambda_k t_c}\big)\,\Phi^\top M\,u_c in a truncated spectral basis (about 128 modes). Small t_c keeps the channel local, large t_c makes it global; no grid of scales is fixed by hand.
  2. Spatial gradient features (gauge-invariant): at each vertex i, the tangent gradient of every diffused channel, written in an arbitrary local frame as a complex number, gives z_i \in \mathbb{C}^d. With a learned complex matrix A \in \mathbb{C}^{d \times d}, g_i = \tanh\Big(\mathrm{Re}\big(\overline{z_i} \odot A z_i\big)\Big) \in \mathbb{R}^d . Rotating the frame by an angle \theta multiplies every entry of z_i by e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}. The factor cancels between \overline{z_i} and Az_i, so g_i does not depend on the frame; the raw gradient does, which is why it is never used directly.
  3. Per-vertex MLP with a residual: u \leftarrow u + \mathrm{MLP}\big([u,\ h,\ g]\big) at each vertex — a pointwise operator (no gauge!).
  4. Stack a few blocks (4 blocks of width 128 by default), between an input and an output linear layer.

Properties: discretization-agnostic (only a Laplacian, a mass matrix and a tangent gradient are required — available on meshes and on point clouds, through the point-cloud Laplacian of Sharp & Crane 2020); isotropic diffusion (no gauge) + inner products of gradients for directional information; learned scales; state of the art on FAUST/SCAPE at the time, robust to re-meshings — the “discrete element” of the field.

DiffusionNet in practice: the training recipe

  • Precompute once per shape, and cache: center the shape and scale it to unit area; compute the mass M, about 128 eigenpairs of L_c\phi = \lambda M\phi, and the tangent-gradient operators. This is the only costly step (seconds per mesh); training then only multiplies small dense matrices.
  • Inputs: either raw XYZ, with a random rotation of each training shape at each step (augmentation, since XYZ is not rotation-invariant), or 16 HKS values on a log-spaced time grid, which are intrinsic and need no rotation augmentation.
  • Loss by task: classification — mass-weighted mean over vertices, then cross-entropy; segmentation — per-vertex cross-entropy; correspondence — a functional-map head (Architecture 6.3), supervised by the ground-truth map or with the unsupervised loss.
  • Optimizer and test: Adam with learning rate 10^{-3} and step decay. Always also evaluate on remeshed test shapes, never seen in training: that is the robustness claim to check.

Architecture 6.3 — Learned functional maps (level 3) Put the network before the functional map of ch. 4 and train through it.

  1. A feature extractor F_\theta (DiffusionNet) produces q descriptors per vertex on both shapes, G_{\mathcal M} = F_\theta(\mathcal M) and G_{\mathcal N} = F_\theta(\mathcal N), with shared weights.
  2. Project them on the spectral bases, as in Theorem 4.1: D_{\mathcal M} = \Phi_{\mathcal M}^\top M_{\mathcal M}\,G_{\mathcal M}, \qquad D_{\mathcal N} = \Phi_{\mathcal N}^\top M_{\mathcal N}\,G_{\mathcal N} \qquad (k \times q).
  3. Functional-map layer: solve Resolution 4.3 row by row in closed form (with the per-row penalty \Omega_i). A linear solve is differentiable, so gradients reach \theta: c_i = \big(D_{\mathcal M}D_{\mathcal M}^\top + \alpha\,\Omega_i\big)^{-1}D_{\mathcal M}\,(d_i^{\mathcal N})^\top .
  4. Supervised loss (GeomFMaps, Donati, Sharma & Ovsjanikov 2020): \|C - C_{\mathrm{gt}}\|_F^2, with C_{\mathrm{gt}} built from the ground-truth point map as in Constraints 4.2.
  5. Unsupervised loss (Roufosse, Sharma & Ovsjanikov 2019): compute C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N} and C_{\mathcal N\mathcal M} and penalize what a near-isometry implies: \mathcal L = w_{b}\big\|C_{\mathcal N\mathcal M}C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N} - I\big\|_F^2 + w_{o}\big\|C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N}^\top C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N} - I\big\|_F^2 + w_{l}\big\|C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N}\Lambda_{\mathcal M} - \Lambda_{\mathcal N}C_{\mathcal M\mathcal N}\big\|_F^2, plus the same terms with the roles of the shapes swapped: bijectivity, orthogonality, Laplacian commutativity.
  6. At test time, convert C into a point map and refine it with ZoomOut (ch. 4, ch. 9).

Why it works: the network no longer has to output a correspondence for 10^4 vertices; it only has to make the k \times k problem of ch. 4 well posed. The structure of C (ch. 4) becomes the training signal.

class LearnedDiffusion(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d):
        super().__init__()
        self.t = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d))       # softplus(t) > 0, one per channel
    def forward(self, H, evals, evecs, mass):             # H: (n, d); evecs: (n, k); mass: (n,)
        H_spec = evecs.T @ (mass[:, None] * H)            # (k, d) spectral coefficients
        t = torch.nn.functional.softplus(self.t)          # (d,)
        decay = torch.exp(-evals[:, None] * t[None, :])   # (k, d): e^{-lambda_k t_c}
        return evecs @ (decay * H_spec)                   # (n, d); parentheses matter

7. Euclidean equivariance

A rotated or translated molecule is the same molecule, and a network should not have to learn this from data. This chapter defines equivariance, recalls the EGNN layer proved equivariant in Graph ML, and asks what invariance means for point clouds and surfaces — where bending adds a symmetry that rotations alone do not cover.

Definition 7.1 — Equivariance and invariance A group G acts on inputs and outputs through representations \rho_{\mathrm{in}}(g) and \rho_{\mathrm{out}}(g). A map f is equivariant if f\big(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}(g)\,x\big) = \rho_{\mathrm{out}}(g)\,f(x) \qquad \forall g \in G, and invariant if \rho_{\mathrm{out}} is trivial: f(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}(g)x) = f(x). For clouds/meshes, the natural group is E(n): an element (Q, t) with Q \in O(n) (rotations and reflections) acts on each point as x \mapsto Qx + t. A rotated molecule remains the same molecule — the network should neither relearn every orientation, nor confuse them.

Recall — EGNN (Satorras, Hoogeboom, Welling, ICML 2021), proved in Graph ML

State: positions x_i \in \mathbb{R}^n, features h_i. Layer: m_{ij} = \phi_e\big(\|x_i - x_j\|^2,\ h_i, h_j\big), \qquad x_i' = x_i + \sum_{j \ne i} (x_i - x_j)\,\phi_x(m_{ij}), \qquad h_i' = \phi_h\big(h_i, \textstyle\sum_j m_{ij}\big).

  • Why it works: Q is orthogonal, so distances, hence messages and h_i', are invariant; the position update is a sum of differences x_i - x_j, each mapped to Q(x_i - x_j), so x' \mapsto Qx' + t.
  • Full proof: Graph ML, ch. 8, Proposition 8.3; Ex. 5 below asks you to redo it in the notation of this page.
  • Point clouds: PointNet on raw XYZ (ch. 6) is permutation-invariant but not rotation-invariant. Three cures, in increasing strength: random-rotation augmentation (approximate, learned from data), invariant inputs (distances, angles), or layers that are equivariant by construction (EGNN, SE(3)-transformers);
  • Surfaces: intrinsic objects — \Delta_{\mathcal M}, its spectrum, the HKS, DiffusionNet fed with HKS — are invariant to rigid motions and to isometric bending, a much larger symmetry than E(3). DiffusionNet fed with XYZ is not, hence the rotation augmentation of the recipe in ch. 6. Its gradient features (Architecture 6.2) are invariant to a local change of tangent frame — a gauge symmetry, the surface cousin of E(n) equivariance;
  • Equivariance vs invariance: for classification, invariance suffices (max pooling) — but to generate or localize, one needs equivariance (to output positions consistent with the inputs); the same debate as “final aggregation vs messages” in GNN theory;
  • SE(3)-transformers (sketch): for the continuous version, attention is built on equivariant fields (irreducible representations; invariant keys/queries, vector-valued values) — the machinery exceeds this course, but the principle stands: invariant similarities, transformed values;
  • E(2)-symmetric routing: in a routing problem on a 2D map (travelling salesman, vehicle routing), the optimal tour does not change when the customer coordinates are rotated, reflected or translated. Sym-NCO (Kim, Park & Park, NeurIPS 2022) trains a neural solver with a REINFORCE baseline shared across rotated copies of each instance, plus a regularizer that makes the encoder’s embedding rotation-invariant — augmentation turned into a training signal. The Year 2 dispatcher subject of the transverse project (Y2.4) builds on it;
  • Connection with the Advanced GNN module, ch. 8: EGNN is a GNN whose messages are constrained by geometry — “geometry first, network second”; the generation of conformers by EGNN (positions = equivariant output) meets diffusion models (the three-line DDPM recall is in ch. 8).

DDPM in three lines (full treatment: Deep Learning P1 9.4 or Deep Learning P2 7.3)

Noise the data x_0 in closed form, with a schedule \beta_t, \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t and \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}\alpha_s: x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I). Train a network to predict the noise, then sample by running the learned denoising steps from x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I) back to x_0: \mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,\varepsilon}\,\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big\|^2 . For 3D point sets, take \varepsilon_\theta to be an EGNN acting on centered positions: the generated distribution is then invariant to rotations. Worked bridges (self-study, ≈ 2 h)

Three worked examples connect the module to graphs and to language. Each one reuses a chapter almost verbatim, and each one is the seed of a Year 2 transverse-project subject.

Bridge A — the HKS on a graph Laplacian. A graph has no metric, but it has a Laplacian L = D - A (symmetric positive semi-definite, 1-based spectrum, Graph ML, ch. 4). It plays the role of L_c, with M = I, and Definition 3.1 carries over unchanged: \mathrm{HKS}(v,t) is the heat that stays at node v after time t.

Two things change. Graphs have no physical scale, so measure time in units of 1/\lambda_2 and normalise each time column — the scale normalisation of ch. 3. And graphs can be large: there, compute only the low end of the spectrum with a sparse shift-invert solver, and check what it returns. Lanczos can return fewer copies of a repeated eigenvalue than its multiplicity, and leaves hanging on the same node, frequent in real graphs, create exactly such repeats. On the 77-node example below, the dense solver is exact and instant.

import numpy as np, networkx as nx
from scipy.sparse.linalg import eigsh

G = nx.les_miserables_graph()                     # 77 characters, weighted co-appearance graph
L = nx.laplacian_matrix(G).astype(float)          # L = D - A: sparse, PSD (plays L_c, M = I)
lam, phi = np.linalg.eigh(L.toarray())            # all 77 eigenpairs, ascending
# large graph: lam, phi = eigsh(L, k=200, sigma=-1e-3), then check repeated eigenvalues
ts = np.geomspace(0.1, 10, 8) / lam[1]            # time grid in units of 1/lambda_2
hks = (phi**2) @ np.exp(-np.outer(lam, ts))       # (n, 8): HKS(v, t)
hks /= hks.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)             # amplitude normalisation per time
nodes = list(G.nodes())
print([nodes[i] for i in np.argsort(-hks[:, 0])[:3]])   # most heat kept at small t

At small t the heat stays longest at peripheral characters of degree 1 — low degree means slow leakage. Jondrette and MlleVaubois come first; then six characters seen only with Myriel tie exactly (they are twins, see Bridge B), so which of them is printed third depends on rounding.

These leaves make \lambda = 1 an eigenvalue of multiplicity 9. A 30-mode eigsh run often misses some of its copies, and the ranking then changes from run to run: the warning above, observed. Used as node features, these columns are a structural encoding of the kind studied in Advanced GNN, ch. 4.

On a knowledge graph (Y2.1) or a transaction graph (Y2.5), the stability bound of ch. 3 suggests that a few injected edges move the HKS little. Treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a theorem: the bound is for small perturbations, and one edge can be a large perturbation of a sparse graph.

Bridge B — graph alignment by functional maps. Two graphs on the same entities, with an unknown node correspondence (two snapshots of a network, two knowledge graphs over shared entities), are two “shapes”. Ch. 4 applies verbatim: eigenbases of both Laplacians, HKS columns as the functions to preserve, C by Resolution 4.3, then a one-to-one node map. This is the pipeline of GRASP (Hermanns et al., 2021), without its base-alignment step.

The protocol needs gold correspondences: take G_2 as a randomly permuted copy of G_1, optionally with a fraction of its edges removed, and score the fraction of nodes mapped to their true partner.

from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
def basis(G, k):                                  # dense eigh: fine below ~5,000 nodes
    Ld = nx.laplacian_matrix(G, nodelist=range(len(G))).toarray().astype(float)
    lam, phi = np.linalg.eigh(Ld); return lam[:k], phi[:, :k]
def hks_cols(lam, phi, q=16):
    return (phi**2) @ np.exp(-np.outer(lam, np.geomspace(0.1, 10, q) / lam[1]))
def fmap(D1, D2, l1, l2, alpha=1e-3, mu=1e-6):    # Resolution 4.3, one Omega_i per row
    k = len(l1)
    return np.stack([np.linalg.solve(D1 @ D1.T + alpha * np.diag((l1 - l2[i])**2)
                                     + mu * np.eye(k), D1 @ D2[i]) for i in range(k)])

G1 = nx.convert_node_labels_to_integers(nx.karate_club_graph())
perm = np.random.default_rng(0).permutation(len(G1))   # gold: node u of G1 is perm[u] in G2
G2 = nx.relabel_nodes(G1, dict(enumerate(perm)))        # remove a few edges here for noise
l1, P1 = basis(G1, 30); l2, P2 = basis(G2, 30)
C = fmap(P1.T @ hks_cols(l1, P1), P2.T @ hks_cols(l2, P2), l1, l2)
cost = ((P2[:, None, :] - (P1 @ C.T)[None, :, :])**2).sum(-1)  # delta functions, mapped by C
rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(cost)                # node rows[i] of G2 <-> cols[i] of G1
print("accuracy", np.mean(perm[cols] == rows))

  • On Les Misérables it reaches only about 80% even without noise (75–92% across random permutations). Eleven of the 77 characters are exact twins — six seen only with Myriel, five only with Valjean, all with the same edge weight — and no structural method can tell twins apart. Guessing within each group caps the expected accuracy at about 88%: report this ceiling next to the accuracy.

9. Course research notes — Geometric Data Analysis (Ovsjanikov)

Maks Ovsjanikov heads the Geometric Data Analysis group at LIX (École Polytechnique). The four publications below are not merely “references” for this course: they are the course — every chapter from 2 to 6 stems directly from them.

Functional Maps — SIGGRAPH 2012 (the founding paper) M. Ovsjanikov, M. Ben-Chen, J. Solomon, A. Butscher, L. Guibas, arXiv:1204.3967 — Best Paper Award.

The story. Before 2012, shape correspondence was handled point to point: combinatorial matching of tens of thousands of vertices, fragile heuristics. The idea: represent the map as a linear operator on functions (ch. 4), which becomes a small matrix in spectral bases.

Beyond chapter 4. The paper also introduces: the effective computation of the constraints, the inverse conversion (matrix → correspondence), and above all the demonstration that a few well-chosen constraints suffice (descriptors + commutation).

Extensions (by the hundreds): dense coupling, partial maps, the shape Differences formulation (comparing how two shapes transport functions), and the ZoomOut refinement below.

Positioning. It is one of the most influential papers of modern shape analysis; the framework is today the standard and the basis of learned methods (GeomFMaps).

HKS — Sun, Ovsjanikov, Guibas, SGP 2009 A Concise and Provably Informative Multi-Scale Signature.

The genesis. The heat kernel h_t(x, y) (ch. 2) transports information between points; its diagonal h_t(x,x) — the heat that returns to its starting point — is a descriptor of x at every scale t.

Contributions.

  1. the multi-scale characterization (small t = local, large = global);
  2. the proof of isometric invariance and stability (the proof sketch of ch. 3);
  3. the analysis of the space of scales.

Impact. The HKS and its WKS variant are the input descriptors of almost all correspondence pipelines (including functional maps); the scale-invariant version extends it to heterogeneous scans.

ZoomOut — SIGGRAPH Asia 2019 S. Melzi, J. Ren, E. Rodolà, A. Sharma, P. Wonka, M. Ovsjanikov, arXiv:1904.07865.

Problem. The spectrum truncated to k eigenfunctions is a trade-off: small k = robust but coarse; large k = precise but noisy.

Idea. The iterative spectral refinement of ch. 4: reconstruct a hard correspondence at resolution k, reinject it as a coupling constraint at resolution k + \Delta, and repeat. The regularization emerges from the upsampling rhythm; no additional data is required.

Results. Substantial improvements on mediocre initial correspondences, near-zero cost (matrix products); it became the standard post-processor.

Positioning. The “soft ↔︎ hard” loop is the geometric ancestor of iterative refinements; its spirit resurfaces in the local/global solvers of ch. 5.

DiffusionNet — TOG 2022 N. Sharp, S. Attaiki, K. Crane, M. Ovsjanikov, arXiv:2012.00888.

Problem. All “CNN-like” architectures on surfaces depend on the discretization (mesh vs cloud, resolution) and run into the gauge problem (ch. 6).

Idea. A minimal and geometrically honest layer: diffusion with one learned time per channel + gauge-invariant gradient features + pointwise MLP — block equations in ch. 6 (Architecture 6.2).

Results. State of the art at publication on FAUST/SCAPE correspondence (also when training on one dataset and testing on the other), SHREC’11 classification and human-body segmentation; demonstrated robustness to discretization changes; works on point clouds without a mesh, through the point-cloud Laplacian of Sharp & Crane (2020).

Positioning. The prototype of “sober geometric deep learning”: instead of adapting convolution to the surface, inject the physical operator (heat) as a learning building block — the convergence of chapters 2 (operators) and 6 (networks).

The common thread HKS (2009: eigenfunctions as descriptors) → Functional Maps (2012: the map as an operator) → ZoomOut (2019: spectral refinement) → DiffusionNet (2022: the operator inside the network): fifteen years of research that follow exactly the progression of this course — from the spectral to deep learning.

Show that max pooling and the product are permutation-invariant, but that the product is numerically unstable (explosion/underflow of multiplicative gradients) while the mean dilutes local signals; implement the three aggregators and check on a permuted cloud (stability + selectivity of a discriminative sub-region).

Solution 1 — Symmetry and constants Each edge weight w_{ij} = \frac12(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}) is constructed symmetrically in i, j (the two opposite angles favor neither endpoint): W = W^\top.

And L_c\mathbf{1} = 0 because the diagonal term of each row i is the opposite of the sum of the off-diagonal entries: \sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij} - \sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij} = 0 .

On a flat mesh, only affine functions have a zero discrete Laplacian at the interior vertices (linear precision of the cotangent weights). For a coordinate x, (L_cx)_i = \sum_j w_{ij}(x_i - x_j) = 0 at every interior vertex, even on an irregular Delaunay mesh (numerically about 10^{-14}). A generic f, such as \sin(3x)\,y^2, gives values of order 1. At boundary vertices the one-ring is incomplete and (L_c\mathbf{x})_i \ne 0: it points along the in-plane normal to the boundary.

Solution 2 — Gauss–Bonnet on the cube Each corner receives three angles of \pi/2: \text{deficit } 2\pi - 3\times\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \text{total } 8\times\frac{\pi}{2} = 4\pi = 2\pi\chi \text{ with } \chi = 2. The vertices added by subdivision (the edge midpoints, inside a square face or on a crease of the cube) are flat — their angles sum to 2\pi, zero deficit — and the 8 corners keep their \frac{\pi}{2}: the sum is unchanged.

This illustrates that the right-hand side of Gauss–Bonnet is topological; it is a practical integrity test for a closed mesh: \sum_iK_iA_i = 2\pi\chi whatever the resolution.

Solution 4 — The heat kernel solves the PDE Term-by-term derivative: \partial_t h_t(x,y) = -\sum_k\lambda_ke^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y), and \Delta_yh_t(x,y) gives the same sum after applying \Delta\phi_k = -\lambda_k\phi_k to the second factor — equal.

As t \to 0, h_t(x,\cdot) \to \delta_x (an approximate identity family, orthonormal basis); by superposition, u(t) = e^{t\Delta}u_0 with each mode damped by e^{-\lambda_kt}.

Solution 6 — Stiffness of a triangle Case i \ne j: redo step 2 of derivation 2.2. \nabla\psi_i is constant, orthogonal to the opposite side, with norm 1/h_i; the angle between \nabla\psi_i and \nabla\psi_j equals \pi - \theta_k, hence \int_T\langle\nabla\psi_i,\nabla\psi_j\rangle dA = |T|\cdot\big(-\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_ih_j}\big) = -\frac12\cot\theta_k (via h_i = 2|T|/|e_{jk}|, h_j = 2|T|/|e_{ik}|, |T| = \frac12|e_{jk}||e_{ik}|\sin\theta_k).

Case i = j: \int_T\|\nabla\psi_i\|^2 dA = |T|/h_i^2 = \frac{|e_{jk}|}{2h_i} = \frac12(\cot\theta_j + \cot\theta_k), because the height from i splits the opposite side into segments h_i\cot\theta_j and h_i\cot\theta_k (an algebraic identity, also valid for obtuse angles via the signs of the cotangents).

Solution 7 — HKS invariance By theorem 2.5 (proved invariance), \phi_k^{\mathcal N} \circ T is an eigenfunction of \mathcal M for \lambda_k^{\mathcal N}. The spectra coincide after sorting and, if the eigenvalues are simple, \phi_k^{\mathcal N}(Tx) = \pm\phi_k^{\mathcal M}(x). Then \mathrm{HKS}_{\mathcal N}(Tx,t) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k^{\mathcal N}(Tx)^2 = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k^{\mathcal M}(x)^2 = \mathrm{HKS}_{\mathcal M}(x,t) (the square kills the sign; for multiple eigenvalues: equality after a basis rotation within each eigenspace).

On FAUST: at small t, extremities (hands, head) = self-descriptive points; at large t, convergence toward the constant component — hence the logarithmic grid in t.

Solution 9 — Commutation and the structure of C With T_F(g) = g \circ T^{-1}, we have T_F^{-1}f = f \circ T, so Theorem 2.5 gives \Delta_{\mathcal M}T_F^{-1} = T_F^{-1}\Delta_{\mathcal N}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad T_F\Delta_{\mathcal M} = \Delta_{\mathcal N}T_F . The proof of 4.2 then establishes, for simple spectra, T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M} = \pm\phi_{\sigma(j)}^{\mathcal N} \quad\text{with}\quad \lambda_j^{\mathcal M} = \lambda_{\sigma(j)}^{\mathcal N} . In coefficients, C_{ij} vanishes unless \lambda_j^{\mathcal M} = \lambda_i^{\mathcal N}: the matrix of T_F in eigenbases is block-diagonal — C_{ij} \ne 0 only if \lambda_i^{\mathcal N} and \lambda_j^{\mathcal M} coincide.

For quasi-isometric surfaces, the equalities become proximities: the soft constraint \|C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} - \Lambda_{\mathcal N}C\|_F^2 encodes this structure, locks the frequency correspondences, and blocks spectrally absurd matchings. Limitation: close or multiple eigenvalues leave undecided blocks — resolved by the descriptor constraints.

Solution 10 — PointNet aggregators Max and product are permutation-invariant (reordering a set changes neither its max nor its product); experiment confirms this on a permuted cloud. But:

  1. the product of n factors below 1 collapses (underflow) and multiplies the gradients — unstable;
  2. the mean is stable but dilutes: if 1% of the cloud carries the discriminative signal, it drowns it where the max captures it;
  3. the max learns local “detectors” — the building block of universality (proof of 6.1).

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device; four questions.

  1. Question (5 points). A matching pipeline built on raw XYZ coordinates works on test shapes in the training pose, but fails on bent poses and on remeshed copies. Explain both failures, name the tools of the course that fix each one, and give one limit of these fixes. Expected: XYZ is extrinsic, so it changes under rotation and isometric bending, while the spectrum and the HKS are intrinsic (Theorem 2.5). A graph Laplacian depends on connectivity; the cotangent Laplacian converges to \Delta_{\mathcal M} and DiffusionNet needs only operators. Limits: isospectral shapes, intrinsic symmetries, scale, multiple eigenvalues.

  2. Question (7 points). A regular octahedron with unit edges has its six vertices at (\pm r, 0, 0), (0, \pm r, 0), (0, 0, \pm r), with r = 1/\sqrt2.

    1. Compute the angle deficit at each vertex and check Theorem 1.3. (b) Compute the cotangent weight w_{ij} of an edge and the barycentric area A_i. (c) Compute H_i\mathbf{n}_i = \tfrac12(M^{-1}L_c\mathbf{x})_i at (r, 0, 0) and compare with the circumscribed sphere.

    Expected: (a) four angles of \pi/3 meet at each vertex, so the deficit is 2\pi/3, and 6 \times \frac{2\pi}{3} = 4\pi = 2\pi\chi, \qquad \chi = 6 - 12 + 8 = 2 .

    1. Both opposite angles are 60°, so w_{ij} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} = 1/\sqrt3. Four triangles of area \sqrt3/4 meet at each vertex, so A_i = 4 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} = 1/\sqrt3.

    2. The four neighbours of (r,0,0) are (0,\pm r,0) and (0,0,\pm r); their differences with the vertex sum to (4r, 0, 0), so (L_c\mathbf{x})_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\,(4r, 0, 0), \qquad H_i\mathbf{n}_i = \tfrac12\sqrt3\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}(4r,0,0) = (\sqrt2, 0, 0). The normal points outward and H_i = \sqrt2 = 1/r, exactly the mean curvature of the circumscribed sphere.

  3. Question (5 points). Let T : \mathcal M \to \mathcal N be an isometry and T_F(g) = g \circ T^{-1}, and let C be its matrix in orthonormal eigenbases, as in Theorem 4.1: C_{ij} = \big\langle\phi_i^{\mathcal N},\, T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle .

    1. Prove that C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} = \Lambda_{\mathcal N}C. (b) Prove that C^\top C = I when complete bases are used.

    Expected: (a) Theorem 2.5 gives T_F\Delta_{\mathcal M} = \Delta_{\mathcal N}T_F. Compute one inner product in two ways, using the self-adjointness of \Delta_{\mathcal N}: -\lambda_i^{\mathcal N}C_{ij} = \big\langle \Delta_{\mathcal N}\phi_i^{\mathcal N}, T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle = \big\langle \phi_i^{\mathcal N}, T_F\Delta_{\mathcal M}\phi_j^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle = -\lambda_j^{\mathcal M}C_{ij}. Hence, for all i, j, C_{ij}\big(\lambda_j^{\mathcal M} - \lambda_i^{\mathcal N}\big) = 0, which is the entrywise form of C\Lambda_{\mathcal M} = \Lambda_{\mathcal N}C.

    1. An isometry preserves areas, hence the inner products of functions. By Parseval in the complete basis of \mathcal N: (C^\top C)_{jl} = \sum_i C_{ij}C_{il} = \big\langle T_F\phi_j^{\mathcal M}, T_F\phi_l^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle = \big\langle \phi_j^{\mathcal M}, \phi_l^{\mathcal M}\big\rangle = \delta_{jl}.

  4. Question (3 points). In DiffusionNet, the gradient of a feature at a vertex is a complex number z written in an arbitrary tangent frame. Show that z depends on the frame but g = \mathrm{Re}(\bar z\,a z), with a learned a \in \mathbb{C}, does not. Why does the diffusion step need no frame at all?

    Expected: rotating the frame by \theta maps z to e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}z, so z itself rotates, while \overline{e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}z}\;a\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}z = e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\,\bar z\,a z = \bar z\,a z . Diffusion e^{t\Delta} acts on scalar functions through the intrinsic, isotropic operator \Delta: no direction is ever chosen.
