Stakes (Extrapolating)

Extrapolated notes. The seminar has two faces: ethical issues and law (bias, surveillance, accountability, governance) and novel applications of AI and visual computing, presented by institutional and industrial speakers. Graph × LLM systems change the impact surface: agents that act, multimodal generation, sensitive relational data.

After a technical year, ethics risks becoming a final slide. Here it is a work object: cases, readings and arguments, in dialogue with the humanities and with the M2 safety, privacy and explainability modules.

Place in the 2-year path

Upstream: M1 and M2 humanities, the fairness and privacy sections of the mandatory courses (map below), the research internship. Parallel: M2 LLMs, GNNs, geometric deep learning and the announced privacy and security slots.

Downstream: the transverse project, whose responsible-AI checklist asks for an AI Act memo, and the final internship, where ethical trade-offs become operational again.

The law in one page

Scope A summary for engineers, not legal advice. Each statement cites its article so that you can read it: both texts are on EUR-Lex (AI Act, GDPR). The statements were checked against the Official Journal texts in October 2026.

The AI Act: who and what

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, regulates AI systems placed on the EU market or used in the EU, and general-purpose AI models. Duties fall on the provider, who develops a system and places it on the market under its own name, and on the deployer, who uses it in a professional capacity. Systems are sorted by risk.

  • Prohibited practices (art. 5). Among them: manipulative or deceptive techniques and the exploitation of vulnerabilities that distort behaviour and cause significant harm; social scoring; predicting crime based solely on profiling; untargeted scraping of facial images; emotion recognition at work and in education (medical and safety uses excepted); biometric categorisation inferring sensitive attributes; real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, save narrow exceptions.
  • High-risk systems, two routes (art. 6). Annex I: the system is a product, or a safety component of a product, covered by the EU legislation listed there (machinery, toys, medical devices…) and subject to third-party conformity assessment. Annex III: listed uses in biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services (credit scoring, life and health insurance pricing, eligibility for public benefits, emergency triage), law enforcement, migration, justice and elections.
  • Escaping the high-risk tier. An Annex III system is not high-risk when it does not materially influence decisions, for instance a narrow procedural task (art. 6(3)); a system that profiles people is always high-risk. The provider documents that assessment.
  • What high-risk means. Providers run a risk-management system and meet requirements on data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency to deployers, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity (arts. 9–15). Deployers have their own duties (art. 26). Public bodies, private entities providing public services, and deployers of credit-scoring or life- and health-insurance pricing systems assess the impact on fundamental rights (art. 27). A person affected by a decision can ask for an explanation of the system’s role (art. 86).
  • Transparency duties (art. 50). Tell people when they interact with an AI system. Providers mark synthetic audio, image, video and text in a machine-readable, detectable way. Deployers disclose deep fakes, and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest unless it was editorially reviewed. People exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation are informed.
  • General-purpose AI models (arts. 51–55). Every provider keeps technical documentation, informs downstream providers, follows a copyright policy that respects text-and-data-mining opt-outs, and publishes a summary of the training content (art. 53); open-source models are exempt from the first two duties unless they carry systemic risk. Systemic risk is presumed above 10^{25} training FLOPs (art. 51(2)) and adds evaluation with adversarial testing, risk mitigation, incident reporting and cybersecurity (art. 55).
  • Research. The Regulation does not apply to systems developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research, nor to research, testing and development before a system is placed on the market; testing in real-world conditions is not covered by that exclusion (art. 2(6) and 2(8)). A student project is usually outside its scope, so the capstone memo is written as if the system were placed on the market.

The AI Act: timeline

Date What applies (Regulation as adopted, art. 113)
1 August 2024 Entry into force, the twentieth day after publication in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024
2 February 2025 Chapters I and II: definitions, AI literacy (art. 4), prohibited practices (art. 5)
2 August 2025 General-purpose AI models (Chapter V), governance, penalties (except the fines on GPAI providers, art. 101), notified bodies
2 August 2026 General date of application: most remaining obligations, including art. 50 and the Annex III high-risk regime
2 August 2027 Art. 6(1): high-risk systems that are Annex I products or safety components

Later amendments. In November 2025 the Commission proposed a “digital omnibus” to postpone some high-risk deadlines. It was adopted as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026 (Official Journal of 24 July 2026, in force on 27 July 2026). As read in the Official Journal, it moves the high-risk requirements (Chapter III, Sections 1–3) to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and to 2 August 2028 for Annex I systems.

The same act gives providers of generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to mark their outputs (art. 50(2)). It also adds two prohibitions from 2 December 2026: AI systems that generate or manipulate sexual images, video or audio of an identifiable person without consent, or child sexual abuse material (new art. 5(1), points (ba) and (bb), with their conditions in art. 5(1a) and (1b)).

Always check the consolidated text on EUR-Lex before relying on a date: further amendments, guidelines and codes of practice may follow.

GDPR basics for ML

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 applies whenever a dataset, a log or a model output concerns people. Six points matter most to an ML engineer.

  • Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person (art. 4(1)), judged by the means “reasonably likely to be used” to identify someone (recital 26). Pseudonymised data remain personal data; only anonymous data leave the Regulation. A synthetic dataset or a trained model must therefore be tested for re-identification before it is called anonymous.
  • Lawful basis (art. 6). Every processing needs one: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests balanced against the person’s rights (not available to public authorities for their tasks). Health data, biometric data used to identify someone and the other special categories need an additional condition (art. 9).
  • Purpose limitation and data minimisation (art. 5(1)(b)–(c)). Data collected for one purpose are not reused for an incompatible one; further processing for scientific research is not considered incompatible under the safeguards of art. 89(1). Collect only what the purpose needs: for ML, that covers features, retention periods and logs.
  • Rights of access and erasure (arts. 15 and 17). People can obtain a copy of their data and, on the grounds listed in art. 17, its erasure. Deleting a record from a dataset is easy; removing its influence from trained weights means retraining or machine unlearning (LLMs 9.6).
  • Automated decisions (art. 22). A person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal or similarly significant effects. The exceptions are contract, law and explicit consent. Under the contract and consent exceptions the controller must still offer at least human intervention, the right to express one’s view and the right to contest the decision (art. 22(3)); a law that allows such decisions must lay down its own safeguards.
  • By design (arts. 25 and 35). Data protection by design and by default; a data-protection impact assessment when processing is likely to result in a high risk to people’s rights and freedoms.

These notes do not track amendments of the GDPR, some of which have been proposed since 2025: check the consolidated text on EUR-Lex before relying on an article.

Three cases

Each case follows the template of the seminar: facts (what the system does, what the law says), values in tension, options including abstention. The questions are those a written brief should answer.

Case 1 · GNN credit scoring, an Annex III use

Part Content
Facts A lender scores consumer-loan applicants with a GNN over a graph that links them through shared employers, addresses and money transfers; on its validation set the GNN beats a gradient-boosting baseline. Evaluating the creditworthiness of natural persons is an Annex III use (point 5(b)), so the system is high-risk, and since it profiles people the art. 6(3) derogation cannot apply. The lender, as deployer, runs a fundamental-rights impact assessment (art. 27); a refused applicant can ask for an explanation (art. 86) and, if the decision is solely automated, for human intervention (GDPR art. 22). Message passing scores each applicant partly on the behaviour of the neighbours.
Values in tension Accuracy and access to credit; non-discrimination, since a neighbourhood can act as a proxy for a protected attribute; contestability (can a refusal be explained in terms the applicant can act on?); the privacy of third parties whose data enter someone else’s score; accountability split between provider and deployer.
Options (a) Drop the graph: gradient boosting with monotone constraints and reason codes. (b) Keep the GNN on consented, relevant edges only, with error rates per group (ML 4.8) and per-decision attributions checked by a deletion test. (c) Use the graph for fraud detection only, which point 5(b) excludes, and keep scoring tabular. (d) Abstain on borderline scores and send them to a human.
Questions for the brief Who is the provider, who is the deployer? Which requirements of arts. 9–15 bite hardest on a GNN? From which date do they apply after the 2026 amendment?
Material UCI Default of Credit Card Clients — CC BY 4.0 — 30,000 clients, 23 features, no graph: build a fully specified synthetic graph (10 nearest neighbours on standardised features, seed 0) to measure how neighbours shift scores. Chapters: Graph ML ch. 8, ML 4.8, LLMs 9.6.

Case 2 · Voice-cloning consent

Part Content
Facts An audiobook studio wants to clone the voice of a narrator who recorded for it years ago, and to produce new titles by zero-shot TTS from a few seconds of reference audio (Speech ch. 9). The old contract covers the recordings, not cloning. Under art. 50 the provider of the TTS system marks its audio as synthetic, and the deployer discloses deep fakes: audio that resembles an existing person and would falsely appear authentic (art. 3(60)). Voice recordings are personal data; speaker embeddings used to identify someone are biometric data (GDPR art. 4(14)). National law may also protect the voice as an attribute of personality.
Values in tension The narrator’s consent, control and livelihood; the listeners’ right to know; accessibility (a synthetic voice for people who lost theirs); the fraud risk of a voice model that leaks; the studio’s investment.
Options (a) Refuse. (b) Clone only with specific, informed, revocable written consent, a revenue share and a usage log. (c) Option (b) plus an audio watermark, a spoken or written disclosure and a public detector. (d) Design a new synthetic voice that resembles no one.
Questions for the brief Is an audiobook read by a cloned voice a deep fake in the sense of art. 3(60), and does the lighter regime for evidently artistic works apply? What survives MP3 compression: the watermark, the disclosure, or neither? What happens to the voice model when consent is withdrawn?
Material Speech ch. 9 (cloning, WavMark and AudioSeal); AudioSeal — MIT — for a detection experiment on authorised voices only.

Case 3 · Releasing a synthetic contact network

Part Content
Facts A hospital consortium wants to share a contact network (proximity sensors between staff and patients) so that researchers can model epidemics. Health data are a special category (GDPR art. 9), and the structure of a graph re-identifies people even without names (Graph Generative Models ch. 8, Prop. 8.4). The plan is to release a synthetic graph from a generative model that keeps degrees, clustering and contact patterns by role. Whether that graph is still personal data depends on the means reasonably likely to be used to re-identify someone (recital 26): it must be tested, not assumed.
Values in tension Public-health utility and reproducible science; the privacy of patients and staff; data minimisation; equal protection across roles and wards, since people in rare roles are the easiest to re-identify.
Options (a) No release: analysis inside a secure enclave. (b) Release aggregate statistics only (degree distribution, contact matrix by role). (c) Release a synthetic graph after node-level membership-inference tests and fidelity checks (MMD on degrees, clustering and orbits). (d) Release an edge-level differentially private synthetic graph with a stated ε, accepting lower fidelity.
Questions for the brief Which attack would the release survive, and which not? Who signs off? What does the GDPR still require if the AI Act research exemption applies to the generator?
Material Stand-in network: SNAP email-Eu-core — no licence stated on the page, cite the dataset paper — 1,005 nodes, 25,571 edges, 42 departments. Chapters: Graph Generative Models ch. 6, ch. 8, Privacy & UQ ch. 1–4, Advanced GNN ch. 7.

Industry and research links

Industry: AI governance, risk and compliance, product red-teaming, usage policies, AI Act documentation. Research: fairness, privacy attacks, socio-technical studies, and fast-moving novel applications (agents, generative models on graphs).

How to approach it

  • For each case, separate technical facts, conflicting values and action options (design, policy, abstention).
  • After each talk, write down the speaker’s main claim and check one technical fact against a chapter of the map.
  • Cite the article, not a summary of it: the texts are on EUR-Lex, and dates move.

Assessment and projects

Status Independent proposals of these notes, not the official assessment. The mode of IME_50430_EP is not displayed: no SynapseS sheet is published, and the curriculum wiki gives none. The scheme below is one possible design for a weekly seminar of 6 ECTS.

A classical assessment

Component Weight Format When
Reflective notes 30 % six notes of 400–600 words, each after a talk: the speaker’s main claim, one technical fact checked against a chapter of the map, one open question within a week of the talk
Case-study essay 50 % 2,500 words on one of the three cases or on a case from a talk, in the facts / values / options format, graded with the rubric below end of P2
Oral or debate 20 % a 10-minute structured debate between teams holding assigned positions, then 5 minutes of individual questions; or an individual 10-minute oral last sessions

Reflective notes are marked out of 5: the claim restated accurately (1), the fact checked with a source (2), a question that evidence could settle (2).

Rubric of the case-study essay.

Criterion Weight
Facts: the system is described correctly, with chapters cited and uncertainties stated 25 %
Law: correct qualification (AI Act tier and role, GDPR basis), articles cited, dates checked on EUR-Lex 20 %
Values: at least two values in tension, each argued from both sides 20 %
Options: at least three, including abstention, each with a measurable test (metric, threshold, who decides) 25 %
Writing: structure, sources, length 10 %

Debate. Positions are drawn a week before. The grade weighs the use of facts, the answers to objections and the individual questions.

Resit. A new essay on another case, with the same rubric.

Group variant

The three cases can also run as group briefs (3–4 students, about 12 h each): a 4-page facts / values / options brief, then a 10-minute debate against a team holding another option. The capstones of the transverse project reuse the same reasoning in their AI Act memo.
