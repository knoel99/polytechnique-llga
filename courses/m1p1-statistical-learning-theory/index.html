0. Review: probability, estimation, convexity

L3 review material used

  • Probability: modes of convergence, law of large numbers, central limit theorem (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 4; the proved version of these theorems appears in chapter 2 of the Probability Theory for ML module); Markov and Chebyshev inequalities; conditional expectation; binomial and Gaussian distributions;
  • Estimation: bias and variance of an estimator, quadratic error, maximum likelihood (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 5), confidence intervals and elementary tests (chapter 6);
  • Linear regression: least-squares estimator, normal equations, Gauss-Markov theorem (chapter 7; and the Machine Learning module, chapter 2, for ridge regularization);
  • Convex analysis: convex sets and functions, strong convexity, subgradients (the \ell_1 norm is not differentiable at 0 — this is the nerve of the Lasso in chapter 8), directional derivatives.

Proposition 0.1 — Union bound (proved) and first use For events A_1, \dots, A_M: \mathbb{P}\big(\bigcup_{j=1}^M A_j\big) \leq \sum_{j=1}^M \mathbb{P}(A_j).

Proof. By induction on M starting from \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) \leq \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B).∎

This trivial lemma does half the work of this course: controlling a supremum over M functions means controlling each one (Hoeffding, ch. 2) then taking \delta/M in each event — the price to pay is a logarithm, \log M, omnipresent in high-dimensional statistics (where M will be the number p of variables, or the number of dichotomies (2n)^d of a class of Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension d, ch. 3-4).

Position in the curriculum This module is the theoretical counterpart of the Machine Learning module: the statistical framework is laid out there in chapter 1 and “generalization theory” is surveyed in chapter 7 — we take up these two points and prove them. It also prepares chapter 2 of the Machine Learning module (Lasso), the choice of hyperparameters by cross-validation (Machine Learning module, chapter 9), and provides the concentration background used everywhere else (Deep Learning and Emerging ML modules).

1. The empirical risk minimization framework

Concrete scene: a classifier with 106 parameters, 5,000 examples — train error 0%, test 28%. The model “learns”… and memorizes. Conversely, a too-poor model stalls at 40% everywhere. This chapter formalizes the diagnosis: risk vs empirical risk, ERM, what must be proved to talk about generalization — before VC and Rademacher.

What breaks Confusing empirical error with risk; tuning on the test set; citing a numerical VC bound as an operational guarantee (often vacuous). All three sabotage a model audit as badly as a theory homework.

Definition 1.1 — Statistical model of learning We observe D_n = ((X_1,Y_1), \dots, (X_n,Y_n)), i.i.d. with distribution \mathbb{P} on \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} (\mathcal{Y} = \{-1,+1\} in binary classification, \mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R} in regression). A predictor is a measurable function h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} (or into \mathbb{R}, the decision being t \mapsto \mathrm{sign}(t)). A loss \ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathbb{R} \to [0,\infty) measures the error (0-1: \ell(y,t) = \mathbf{1}_{t \neq y}; quadratic: (y-t)^2; hinge: \max(0, 1-yt)). The risk and the empirical risk are L(h) = \mathbb{E}\big[\ell(Y, h(X))\big], \qquad \hat L(h) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(Y_i, h(X_i)). For a class \mathcal{H} of predictors, the ERM estimator (empirical risk minimization) is \hat h \in \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \hat L(h). This is the paradigm of almost all of supervised machine learning: least squares, logistic regression, SVMs, neural networks (see the Machine Learning module, chapter 1).

Proposition 1.2 — Risk decomposition (proved) Let h^\star_\mathcal{H} \in \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L(h) be the best hypothesis in the class (the oracle). For every ERM \hat h: L(\hat h) - L(h^\star) \;\leq\; 2 \sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \big\|L(h) - \hat L(h)\big\|.

Proof. By definition of \hat h (empirical optimality): \hat L(\hat h) \leq \hat L(h^\star). Then L(\hat h) - L(h^\star) = \big[L(\hat h) - \hat L(\hat h)\big] + \big[\hat L(\hat h) - \hat L(h^\star)\big] + \big[\hat L(h^\star) - L(h^\star)\big] \leq \big[L(\hat h) - \hat L(\hat h)\big] + 0 + \big[\hat L(h^\star) - L(h^\star)\big], and each of the two brackets is bounded in absolute value by \sup_{h} \|L(h) - \hat L(h)\|.∎

All the theory that follows consists in bounding this uniform supremum of gaps — a random quantity, controlled with high probability. The classic risk decomposition reads there: L(\hat h) = \underbrace{\inf_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L(h)}_{\text{approximation}} + \underbrace{L(\hat h) - L(h^\star)}_{\text{estimation}}: richness of the class versus sample size.

Definition 1.3 — Consistency and learnability A learning rule h_n = h(D_n) is universally consistent if L(h_n) \to \inf_{h} L(h) = L^\star in probability for every distribution \mathbb{P} (the minimum over all measurable functions; L^\star is attained by the Bayes rule \eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid X=x) in 0-1 classification). Central question of the course: at what speed, and for which classes \mathcal{H}? Chapters 3-6 bound the speed by the “size” of \mathcal{H}; chapter 7 shows that these speeds are optimal in the minimax sense.

Example 1.4 — Thresholds on the line (running example) \mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}, \mathcal{H} = \{h_t : t \in \mathbb{R},\; h_t(x) = \mathbf{1}_{x \leq t}\}. Why this small example? The class is continuously parametrized, but: (i) on n points it produces only n+1 distinct dichotomies (the order of the X_i suffices to describe everything); (ii) its Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension equals d = 2 (ch. 3); (iii) the associated minimax speed is \sqrt{d/n} (ch. 7). Theory thus turns an infinite family into a finite combinatorial object — this is the founding gesture of Vapnik and Chervonenkis.

2. Concentration inequalities

Definition 2.1 — Sub-Gaussian variables A centered r.v. X is sub-Gaussian with parameter \sigma if \mathbb{E}\,e^{\lambda X} \leq e^{\sigma^2\lambda^2/2} for all \lambda \in \mathbb{R}. Examples: centered Gaussian (\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}), centered bounded (lemma 2.2, with \sigma = (b-a)/2), any L-Lipschitz function of a Gaussian vector (parameter L\sigma_G, “Gaussian concentration”; see the Probability Theory for ML module, chapter 2, for the CLT in the same spirit). (Independent) sums of sub-Gaussians are sub-Gaussian with additive \sigma^2: the whole “Gaussian paradigm” of concentration fits in this semigroup of the Laplace transform.

Lemma 2.2 — Hoeffding’s lemma (proved) If X \in [a,b] almost surely and \mathbb{E}X = 0, then for all \lambda: \mathbb{E}\,e^{\lambda X} \leq e^{\lambda^2 (b-a)^2/8}.

Proof. Step 1 (secant line). The convexity of u \mapsto e^{\lambda u} gives, for u \in [a,b]: e^{\lambda u} \leq \frac{b-u}{b-a}\,e^{\lambda a} + \frac{u-a}{b-a}\,e^{\lambda b}. Taking the expectation with \mathbb{E}X = 0 (average weights b/(b-a) and -a/(b-a)): \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X} \leq \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda Z} where Z is the two-point variable taking values a and b with \mathbb{E}Z = 0. Step 2 (variance of a tilted Bernoulli). Set L(\lambda) = \log \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda Z}. Differentiating twice under the expectation (everything is bounded): L''(\lambda) = \mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}(Z) \leq \frac{(b-a)^2}{4}, where \mathrm{Var}_\lambda is the variance under the tilted distribution d\mathbb{P}_\lambda \propto e^{\lambda z}\,d\mathbb{P}; the variance of a two-point variable with points separated by b-a is p(1-p)(b-a)^2 \leq (b-a)^2/4, whatever the tilt. Step 3 (Taylor). L(0) = 0, L'(0) = \mathbb{E}Z = 0, hence L(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{2}L''(\xi) \leq \lambda^2 (b-a)^2/8 for some \xi \in [0,\lambda].∎

Theorem 2.3 — Hoeffding’s inequality (proved) Let X_1, \dots, X_n be independent, X_i \in [a_i, b_i], and S = \sum_i (X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i). For every t \> 0: \mathbb{P}(S \geq t) \leq \exp\Big(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{P}(\|S\| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2}\Big).

Proof. Chernoff’s method: for \lambda \> 0, by Markov then independence and lemma 2.2 applied to each X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i: \mathbb{P}(S \geq t) \leq e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda S} = e^{-\lambda t} \prod_i \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda(X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i)} \leq \exp\Big(-\lambda t + \frac{\lambda^2}{8}\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2\Big). The right-hand side is minimized at \lambda = 4t/\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2, where the exponent equals -2t^2/\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2. The two-sided version follows by applying the result to -S and summing.∎

Immediate application: for a binomial empirical frequency (0-1 loss of a fixed h: n\hat L(h) is binomial), \mathbb{P}\big(\hat L(h) - L(h) \geq \varepsilon\big) \leq e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.

Example 2.4 — Sample size (numbers) To guarantee \|\hat L(h) - L(h)\| \leq \varepsilon with probability 1 - \delta for a fixed h: n \geq \frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}.

Theorem 2.5 — Bernstein and McDiarmid (statements)

  • Bernstein: if the independent centered X_i satisfy \|X_i\| \leq M and \sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = v, then \mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_i X_i \geq \sqrt{2vt} + \tfrac{Mt}{3}\Big) \leq e^{-t}, that is, after inversion, \mathbb{P}(\sum X_i \geq u) \leq \exp(-u^2/(2v + 2Mu/3)). Regime u \lesssim v/M: Gaussian (e^{-u^2/2v}, variance); large u: Poissonian (e^{-3u/2M}, amplitude);
  • McDiarmid (bounded differences): if f : \prod \mathcal{X}_i \to \mathbb{R} satisfies \|f(x) - f(x')\| \leq c_i whenever x and x' differ only in the i-th coordinate, then \mathbb{P}\big(f - \mathbb{E}f \geq u\big) \leq \exp\Big(-\frac{2u^2}{\sum_i c_i^2}\Big). Proof: Doob’s martingale + lemma 2.2 conditionally on each coordinate (admitted here).

Why McDiarmid is the tool of the course: the supremum f(D_n) = \sup_{h} \|L(h) - \hat L(h)\| is 1-Lipschitz with respect to each datum (X_i,Y_i) (changing one observation shifts \hat L by at most 1/n if the losses are bounded by 1): c_i = 2/n and McDiarmid transports the concentration of the mean to the supremum — all that remains is to control \mathbb{E}f, the heart of chapters 3-5.

Proposition 2.6 — Maximum of sub-Gaussians (proved) If X_1, \dots, X_M are centered sub-Gaussian with parameter \sigma (not necessarily independent): \mathbb{E}\max_{j \leq M} X_j \;\leq\; \sigma\sqrt{2 \log M}.

Proof. For \lambda \> 0, monotonicity of the log then of the expectation (Jensen), then sub-additivity of the max and finally the sub-Gaussian assumption: \mathbb{E}\max_j X_j = \frac1{\lambda}\,\mathbb{E}\log e^{\lambda\max_j X_j} \leq \frac1{\lambda}\log \mathbb{E} e^{\lambda \max_j X_j} = \frac1{\lambda}\log \mathbb{E} \max_j e^{\lambda X_j} \leq \frac1{\lambda}\log \sum_j \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X_j} \leq \frac{\log M}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda \sigma^2}{2}. Optimization at \lambda = \sqrt{2\log M}/\sigma.∎

Interpretation: the max of M Gaussian noises equals \sigma\sqrt{2\log M} up to a constant — the “price of the max”. It is the key ingredient for controlling suprema: combined with the union bound (Prop. 0.1), it gives \sqrt{\log M/n} for any finite class of M bounded losses.

From the finite max to the infinite Proposition 2.6 and the union bound (Prop. 0.1) control any finite class of M predictors (bounds in \sqrt{\log M/n}). For infinite classes (M = \infty), one must count the realizable dichotomies — the subject of the next two chapters; for “geometric” classes, one will go through Rademacher (ch. 5).

3. Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension and Sauer’s lemma

Definition 3.1 — Shattering, VC dimension, growth function Let \mathcal{H} be a class of classifiers \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\} (or of real functions of which we take the sign).

  • A finite set S = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \subset \mathcal{X} is shattered if every binary labeling of S is realized: \|\mathcal{H} \cap S\| = 2^N, where \mathcal{H} \cap S = \{(h(x_1),\dots,h(x_N)) : h \in \mathcal{H}\};
  • the VC dimension d = \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}) is the maximal size of a shattered set (or \infty);
  • the growth function \Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(N) = \max_{\|S\| = N} \|\mathcal{H} \cap S\| counts the realizable dichotomies on N points in the worst case.

Example 3.2 — Computing VC dimensions

  • Thresholds on \mathbb{R} (Example 1.4): two points are shattered (4 dichotomies via the placement of t), three points x_1 \< x_2 \< x_3 are not (impossible to label \{x_1, x_3\} without x_2: a threshold returns an interval of the form (-\infty,t]): d = 2. Likewise intervals: d = 2; unions of k intervals: d = 2k;
  • Affine hyperplanes in \mathbb{R}^p: d = p + 1. \geq: the p+1 points 0, e_1, \dots, e_p are shattered by the hyperplanes \langle w, x\rangle = b (around each vertex of the simplex, one isolates any subset: for a label y \in \{0,1\}^{p+1} take w = \sum_j (2y_j - 1)e_j, with b well chosen); \leq: p+2 points in general position are linked by an affine dependence \sum_j \alpha_j x_j = 0 with \sum \alpha_j = 0; the labeling y_j = \mathrm{sign}(\alpha_j) is not realizable (dot product of the signs with the \alpha_j strictly positive forbids strict separation). Hence, for the perceptron and unregularized logistic regression, a complexity that grows with the dimension — to be contrasted with the margin (ch. 6);
  • Axis-aligned rectangles in \mathbb{R}^2: d = 4 (the 4 “cardinal points” of a cross-shaped configuration); sinusoids \{x \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{\sin(tx) \> 0}\}: d = \infty (for every N, well-placed points can follow the sign of any alternation): exponential growth \Pi(N) = 2^N, no universal learnability.

Theorem 3.3 — Sauer-Shelah lemma (proved) If \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}) = d, then for every N \geq d: \Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(N) \;\leq\; \sum_{k=0}^{d} \binom{N}{k} \;\leq\; \Big(\frac{eN}{d}\Big)^{d}.

Proof. The polynomial upper bound by (eN/d)^d is the classic inequality on binomial sums (each \binom{N}{k} \leq (eN/k)^k, sum dominated by k = d; admitted). Let us prove the first inequality by double induction on N and d. Write s(N,d) = \sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{N}{k}. Base: s(N,0) = 1 (class reduced to a single function); s(0,d) = 1 (an empty point set: a single dichotomy). Induction step: fix S = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} and write S' = \{x_1, \dots, x_{N-1}\}. Partition the traces on S' into two families:

  • \mathcal{A}: traces realizable in both ways on x_N (there exist h, h' \in \mathcal{H} coinciding on S' with h(x_N) = 0, h'(x_N) = 1);
  • \mathcal{B}: traces realizable in at least one way on x_N (the complement of \mathcal{A} within the traces).

Then \|\mathcal{H} \cap S\| = \|\mathcal{A}\| + \|\mathcal{B}\|: each trace of \mathcal{B} gives one dichotomy (label 0 on x_N), each trace of \mathcal{A} gives one more (label 1). Now:

  • \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{B}) \leq d (subfamily): by induction, \|\mathcal{B}\| \leq s(N-1, d);
  • \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{A}) \leq d - 1: if \mathcal{A} shattered T \subseteq S' with \|T\| = d, then T \cup \{x_N\} (size d+1) would be shattered by \mathcal{H} — the traces of \mathcal{A} on T give everything and the point x_N separates in both ways: contradiction with \mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}) = d. Hence \|\mathcal{A}\| \leq s(N-1, d-1).

Conclusion by Pascal’s identity: s(N-1,d) + s(N-1,d-1) = s(N,d).∎

Message: a class of VC dimension d emits only (eN/d)^d dichotomies on N points — polynomial growth with exponent d, versus 2^N in the worst case. The “effective number of hypotheses” is finite and controlled.

4. VC generalization bounds

Theorem 4.1 — Vapnik-Chervonenkis inequality (statement, proof sketched) Let \mathcal{H} have VC dimension d \geq 1, with losses in [0,1]. With probability at least 1 - \delta, simultaneously for all h \in \mathcal{H}: \big\|L(h) - \hat L(h)\big\| \;\leq\; \varepsilon_n(\delta) = \sqrt{\frac{32}{n}\Big(d\,\log\frac{2en}{d} + \log\frac{4}{\delta}\Big)}.

Proof. Sketch (double sample). The constants are not optimal; the structure is what matters.

  • Step 1 — symmetrization: introduce a ghost sample D'_n, i.i.d. with the same distribution. On the deviation event \{L - \hat L \geq \varepsilon\}, the ghost version \hat L' is at distance \geq \varepsilon/2 from \hat L with probability \geq 1 - e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8}: for large n\varepsilon^2, controlling L - \hat L amounts to controlling \hat L' - \hat L — two empirical quantities, depending on the distributions only through the 2n sampled points;
  • Step 2 — combinatorial reduction: conditionally on the 2n points, \hat L' - \hat L is an average of Bernoulli variables (each ghost datum is a uniform draw among the 2n points); the supremum over \mathcal{H} now bears only on the \Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(2n) \leq (2en/d)^d realizable dichotomies; binomial concentration (Hoeffding, Thm 2.3) then union bound (Prop. 0.1) over these dichotomies;
  • Step 3 — assembly: choosing the thresholds so that the three failure probabilities (symmetrization, binomial, union) sum to \delta, one inverts \varepsilon and obtains the announced bound (classical form of Vapnik-Chervonenkis 1974; see Devroye, Györfi and Lugosi, A Probabilistic Theory of Pattern Recognition, Theorem 12.5, for the details).

∎

Corollary 4.2 — Risk bound for ERM Combining Prop. 1.2 and Thm 4.1: with probability 1 - \delta, L(\hat h) \;\leq\; \hat L(\hat h) + 2\,\varepsilon_n(\delta) \;\leq\; L(h^\star_\mathcal{H}) + 4\,\varepsilon_n(\delta). Consequences: (i) every class with finite VC dimension is universally consistent (Def. 1.3); (ii) the sample cost to guarantee an excess risk \varepsilon is n = \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/\varepsilon^2) (up to logarithms); (iii) it suffices that d \ll n — and not p \ll n — to learn: this is the first appearance of the inverted “curse” of high dimension.

Theorem 4.4 — “No free lunch” (statement) For every learning rule h_n, there exists a distribution \mathbb{P} on \mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\} with L^\star = 0 (separable data!) such that \mathbb{E}[L(h_n)] \geq 1/8 whenever n \leq d/2, where d is the size of a set shattered by the class used. Without a class restriction (or a regularity assumption), learning over all distributions is impossible: the VC dimension (or any complexity measure) is the currency with which one pays for generalization. Proof by a counting argument over the 2^{2n} consistent labelings (see Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, chapter 5).

5. Rademacher complexity

Definition 5.1 — Rademacher complexities Let \mathcal{F} be a class of measurable functions \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} (our families of losses \ell \circ h). The empirical Rademacher complexity on a sample S = (X_1,\dots,X_n) is \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) = \mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Big[ \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac1n \Big\| \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(X_i) \Big\|\Big], \qquad \sigma_i \ \text{i.i.d. Rademacher } (\pm1 \text{ equiprobable}), and the Rademacher complexity is \mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}) = \mathbb{E}_S[\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F})]. Reading: it is the maximal correlation of the class with pure sign noise — the capacity of the class to “fit” randomness. Finite classes: \mathcal{R}_n \leq \sqrt{2\log M/n} (Prop. 2.6); VC classes with bounded losses: \mathcal{R}_n \leq C\sqrt{d\log n/n}, and even, by the chaining technique, \mathcal{R}_n \leq \sqrt{2 d \log(en/d)/n}. The key idea: Rademacher complexity replaces combinatorial counting by a geometry of the class — normalization, dimension, convexity.

Proposition 5.2 — Symmetrization (proved) \mathbb{E}_S\Big[\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \big(L(f) - \hat L(f)\big)\Big] \;\leq\; 2\,\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}).

Proof. Introduce a ghost sample S' = (X'_1,\dots,X'_n), an independent copy of S. Since L(f) = \mathbb{E}_{S'}[\hat L'(f)]: \mathbb{E}_S\big[\sup_f (L - \hat L)\big] = \mathbb{E}_S\big[\sup_f \mathbb{E}_{S'}(\hat L'(f) - \hat L(f))\big] \underset{\text{Jensen}}{\leq} \mathbb{E}_{S,S'}\big[\sup_f (\hat L'(f) - \hat L(f))\big]. Now \hat L'(f) - \hat L(f) = \frac1n\sum_i \big(f(X'_i) - f(X_i)\big), and since (X'_i, X_i) and (X_i, X'_i) have the same distribution, we may insert symmetric signs \sigma_i: \mathbb{E}_{S,S'}\Big[\sup_f \frac1n\sum_i \big(f(X'_i) - f(X_i)\big)\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_f \frac1n\sum_i \sigma_i\big(f(X'_i) - f(X_i)\big)\Big]. Finally, sub-additivity of the supremum and symmetry -\sigma \overset{d}{=} \sigma: \sup_f \frac1n \sum_i \sigma_i f(X'_i) - \sup_g \frac1n\sum_i \sigma_i g(X_i) \;\leq\; \sup_f \Big\|\frac1n \sum_i \sigma_i f(X'_i)\Big\| + \sup_g \Big\|\frac1n \sum_i \sigma_i g(X_i)\Big\|, whose expectation equals 2\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}) (the two samples play the same role).∎

Theorem 5.3 — Oracle bound for ERM (proved) With probability at least 1-\delta, for every empirical minimizer \hat h of a class with losses in [0,1]: L(\hat h) - \inf_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L(h) \;\leq\; 2\,\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}) + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

Proof. Step 1 (decomposition): by Prop. 1.2, it suffices to bound Z(S) = \sup_f (L - \hat L). Step 2 (expectation): by Prop. 5.2, \mathbb{E}Z \leq 2\mathcal{R}_n. Step 3 (concentration): Z is Lipschitz in each datum with constant 1/n (changing X_i shifts \hat L by at most 1/n); McDiarmid (Thm 2.5) gives \mathbb{P}(Z \geq \mathbb{E}Z + u) \leq e^{-2nu^2}; taking u = \sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/(2n)} (two-sided, \delta/2) and assembling: Z \leq 2\mathcal{R}_n + \sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/(2n)} \leq 2\mathcal{R}_n + \sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)/n}.∎

Numerical comparison (d = 2, n = 2000): Rademacher \sqrt{2d\log(en/d)/n} \approx 0.12 versus \varepsilon_n \approx 0.59 for the crude VC bound (ch. 4): a factor ~5 gained, and above all a quantity computable from data (code below).

Lemma 5.4 — Talagrand’s contraction (statement) If \varphi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} is L-Lipschitz with \varphi(0) = 0, then \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\varphi \circ \mathcal{F}) \leq L\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}). Proof by coordinate-wise contraction (Ledoux and Talagrand, Probability in Banach Spaces, Theorem 4.12); immediate consequence: in Theorem 5.3, the complexity of the loss class can be replaced by that of the raw class — hinge loss (L=1), logistic, all the margin classifiers of chapter 6 follow from it.

Proposition 5.5 — Linear classes (proved) On data with norm \\|X_i\\| \leq R, the class \mathcal{F} = \{x \mapsto \langle w, x\rangle : \\|w\\|_2 \leq W\} satisfies \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) \;\leq\; \frac{RW}{\sqrt n} \qquad \big(\text{and } \mathcal{R}_n \leq RW\sqrt{2/n}\ \text{by Gaussian comparison}\big).

Proof. By Cauchy-Schwarz then Jensen (the expectation of a norm bounds the norm of the expectation — convexity): \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) = \frac{W}{n}\, \mathbb{E}_\sigma\Big\\|\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i X_i\Big\\| \;\leq\; \frac{W}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_\sigma\Big\\|\sum_i \sigma_i X_i\Big\\|^2} = \frac{W}{n}\sqrt{\sum_i \\|X_i\\|^2} \;\leq\; \frac{RW}{\sqrt n}, where the second-to-last equality uses \mathbb{E}[\sigma_i\sigma_j] = 0 for i \neq j (zero cross terms).∎

Consequence (with Lemma 5.4): linear functions regularized by the norm W learn at rate RW/\sqrt n — the “effective dimension” is R^2W^2, with no dependence on the ambient dimension: this is the gateway to margin SVMs (ch. 6) and kernels (Machine Learning module, chapter 4: bounds in RKHS norm).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

    n, reps = len(X), B
    sigma = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(reps, n))
    return W * np.mean([np.linalg.norm(s @ X) for s in sigma]) / n

Complement to the official syllabus: stability and Dudley’s chaining The detailed syllabus of the course unit lists two additional tools of empirical process theory, absent from the outline above: here they are. (1) Stability (algorithm-dependent analysis): instead of bounding \sup_{h \in \mathcal H} \|\hat R(h) - R(h)\| uniformly over the class, one requires the good algorithm to be stable under the replacement of a sample point (for instance \beta-uniform: inserting or removing a point changes the loss of h_{\mathcal{S}} by at most \beta on average). The decomposition of the excess risk then gives \mathbb{E}\,[R(h_{\mathcal{S}}) - R(h^\*)] \leq \mathbb{E}\,[R(h_{\mathcal{S}}) - \hat R(h_{\mathcal{S}})] \lesssim \beta: no complexity dimension appears, only a quantity controllable by regularization — the framework in which the generalization of regularized empirical risk is proved (ridge, Lasso of ch. 8) and, further on, the stability of SGD (Machine Learning module, ch. 7). (2) Dudley’s chaining: a technique to bound the Gaussian complexity (hence the Rademacher complexity, by symmetry) of an infinite class by approximating it with a chain of finite discretizations of increasing metric entropy, \mathfrak{G}(\mathcal H) \;\lesssim\; \int_0^\infty \sqrt{\log\,\mathcal{N}(\mathcal H, \varepsilon)}\; d\varepsilon, which replaces Sauer’s lemma (ch. 3) when the class has a continuous structure (Lipschitz parametrized classes, networks with bounded norms): it is the bridge between the VC theory of this chapter and the margin bounds of ch. 6.

6. Margin theory

Definition 6.1 — Functional and geometric margin For an affine classifier h_{w,b}(x) = \mathrm{sign}(\langle w, x\rangle + b), the functional margin of an example (x,y) is y(\langle w,x\rangle + b) (positive if correctly classified), and the geometric margin is this quantity divided by \\|w\\|_2 — it is the Euclidean distance to the hyperplane. The margin of a sample is the minimum over the examples. The maximum margin problem (hard-margin SVM, see the Machine Learning module, chapter 3) seeks \max \gamma under the constraints \\|w\\| \leq 1 and y_i\langle w,x_i\rangle \geq \gamma: minimizing \\|w\\| is maximizing the margin.

Theorem 6.2 — Margin bound (stated with sketch) Let \mathcal{F} be a class of score functions (e.g. linear), with data satisfying \\|X\\| \leq R. For every \gamma \> 0, denote by \hat L_\gamma the empirical margin error at \gamma (proportion of examples with y f(x) \< \gamma). With probability 1 - \delta, for all score functions f in the class: L\big(\mathrm{sign}\, f\big) \;\leq\; \hat L_\gamma(f) + \frac{2\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F})}{\gamma} + 3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}. Sketch: (i) chain of losses \mathbf{1}_{t \< 0} \;\leq\; \varphi_\gamma(t) \;\leq\; \mathbf{1}_{t \< \gamma} with the margin loss \varphi_\gamma(t) = \min\big(1, \max(0, 1 - t/\gamma)\big) (immediate case check): the 0-1 loss is dominated by the margin loss, which is itself dominated by the margin-\gamma error; (ii) Talagrand’s contraction (Lemma 5.4) on \varphi_\gamma, which is 1/\gamma-Lipschitz; (iii) oracle bound of Theorem 5.3 on the contracted class. See Mohri, Rostamizadeh and Talwalkar, Theorem 5.9, for the full version with exact constants.

Corollary 6.3 — SVM: dimension-independent complexity For linear functions with \\|w\\| \leq W, \\|X\\| \leq R and \hat L_\gamma = 0 (data separated with margin \gamma = 1/W): L(h_{w,b}) \;\leq\; \frac{2\sqrt2\,R}{\gamma\sqrt n} + 3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}} \;\approx\; \frac{3R}{\gamma\sqrt n} \quad \text{(Prop. 5.5 substituted).} Numbers (R = 1, \gamma = 0.1): n = 10^4 gives a bound \approx 0.29; n = 10^6, \approx 0.03. The crucial point: neither p nor the VC dimension p+1 appear — the effective complexity is R^2/\gamma^2. High-dimensional but well-separable data generalize; this is the theorem that justifies SVMs, norm-based regularization, and by extension deep networks with controlled margins (see the Deep Learning module, chapter 3: the role of weight norms in controlling Lipschitzness).

Margins and deep learning Neural networks have infinite VC dimension (or growing with the parameters); yet they generalize. The modern explanation goes through margin- and norm-based bounds of the same type as 6.3 (products of matrix norms with margins), smoothed by depth — and not through VC counting. The “contraction + Lipschitz classes + norms” chain of this chapter is exactly the tool used in that literature.

Complement to the official syllabus: low-noise condition and fast rates The detailed syllabus of the course unit mentions the “low-noise condition and fast rates”: this is the probabilistic generalization of the geometric margin of this chapter. Tsybakov’s margin condition bears on the joint distribution rather than on the sample: for a threshold \Delta \> 0, \mathbb{P}\big(\|\eta(X) - 1/2\| \leq \Delta\big) \leq c\,\Delta^{s}, where \eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x) is the posterior probability and s \geq 0 the noise parameter: s = 0 gives back the general case (no useful assumption); large s means little mass near the boundary — the limit s \to \infty corresponds to a strict margin almost surely. Its effect: the classification excess risk is bounded by a power of the “approximate” excess risk (that of \eta), with exponent increasing in s — typically of the order of (s+1)/(s+2) for plug-in and convex classifiers (logistic, boosting — Machine Learning module, ch. 5 and 9). Convergence rates then leave the “slow” n^{-1/2} regime of Corollary 6.3 for fast rates, up to parametric order when the margin is strict. In other words: the sample margin (\gamma, Corollary 6.3) is the geometric object, Tsybakov’s condition is its statistical assumption, and together they explain why large-margin separators often do better than the 1/\sqrt n bound in practice.

7. Lower bounds and minimax

Definition 7.1 — Minimax risk For a model \{\mathbb{P}_\theta^n : \theta \in \Theta\} (n i.i.d. observations) and a loss d: \mathcal{M}_n = \inf_{\hat\theta}\ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta}\ \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[d(\hat\theta(D_n), \theta)\big], the infimum ranging over all measurable estimators. The previous chapters show \mathcal{M}_n \leq \sqrt{d/n} (upper bounds); this chapter establishes the converse: no algorithm can do better in general — the rates obtained are optimal.

Theorem 7.2 — Le Cam’s two-point method (proved) If there exist \theta_1, \theta_2 \in \Theta and s \> 0 with d(\theta_1,\theta_2) \geq 2s and \\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n\\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \delta \< 1, then \mathcal{M}_n \;\geq\; \frac{s}{2}\,(1 - \delta).

Proof. Fix an estimator \hat\theta and set A = \{d(\hat\theta, \theta_1) \leq s\}. Two observations:

  • on A^c: d(\hat\theta, \theta_1) \> s, a contribution s\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c) to the risk under \theta_1;
  • on A: by the triangle inequality and the separation, d(\hat\theta, \theta_2) \geq d(\theta_1,\theta_2) - d(\hat\theta,\theta_1) \geq 2s - s = s, a contribution s\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A) under \theta_2.

Hence \sup_{j} \mathbb{E}_{\theta_j} d(\hat\theta,\theta_j) \geq \frac12\sum_j \mathbb{E}_{\theta_j}[\dots] \geq \frac{s}{2}\big( \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c) + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A)\big). Now \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c) + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A) = 1 + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A) - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A) \geq 1 - \\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n\\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \geq 1 - \delta, by the definition of total variation (supremum over events, including A).∎

Moral: two separated but statistically barely distinguishable parameters (small TV) trap any estimator; the distances between product distributions are controlled via divergences (Kullback, see the next application).

Example 7.3 — Gaussian mean: 1/\sqrt n is unavoidable (computation) Model X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2), \theta \in \mathbb{R}, absolute loss. Take \theta_1 = 0, \theta_2 = \sigma/\sqrt n, so s = \sigma/(2\sqrt n). The Kullback-Leibler divergence of the product distributions equals \mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n \,\\|\, \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n) = n \times \frac{(\theta_1 - \theta_2)^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac12, and Pinsker’s inequality \\|\cdot\\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}/2} gives \delta \leq 1/2. Theorem 7.2 concludes: \mathcal{M}_n \geq \sigma/(8\sqrt n). Under quadratic loss, the same refined argument (exercise 6) gives \mathcal{M}_n \geq c\,\sigma^2/n — the classic parametric rate, attained by the empirical mean: it is optimal.

Theorem 7.4 — Lower bounds for VC classes (sketch) For 0-1 classification with \mathcal{H} of VC dimension d and n \geq c d, there exists a data distribution for which every ERM learner (or any other) incurs \mathbb{E}[L(\hat h) - L^\star] \geq c'\sqrt{d/n}: the bound of Corollary 4.2 is attainable up to constants. Sketch: let the data live on \{1,\dots,d\} (uniform) and consider the 2^d classifiers h_A = \mathbf{1}_A. Two random classifiers differ on d/4 points on average (separation \sqrt{d} in risk); one observation informs about a single coordinate only, so the KL between product distributions \leq n/d; a variant of Le Cam with a mixture over A (Fano’s or Assouad’s method) converts the bundle of 2^d hypotheses into the \sqrt{d/n} bound. See Tsybakov, Introduction to Nonparametric Estimation, chapter 2. The same structure holds for nonparametric rates: Lipschitz regression with \beta = 1, minimax rate n^{-1/3} in L^2 (two points becoming two blocks of separated balls).

Loop closed with “no free lunch” Theorem 4.4 says: without assumptions, nothing works. The minimax approach refines it: with a dimension-d assumption, \sqrt{d/n} is exactly the price. Learning is a bargain between expressivity (small L^\star, large d) and estimation (large n); the cross-validation of chapter 9 is its empirical version.

8. High dimension: sparsity and the Lasso

Definition 8.1 — Sparse linear model and the Lasso Y = X\beta^\star + \varepsilon with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n), a normalized design matrix X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} (\\|X_{\cdot j}\\|_2^2/n = 1), p \gg n, and a sparse vector \beta^\star supported on S, \|S\| = s \ll n. The Lasso (Tibshirani 1996; see the Machine Learning module, chapter 2) is \hat\beta \in \arg\min_{b \in \mathbb{R}^p} \Big\{ \frac{1}{2n}\\|Y - Xb\\|_2^2 + \lambda \\|b\\|_1 \Big\}. The Dantzig selector (Candès-Tao) replaces the minimization by \min \\|b\\|_1 under \\|X^\top(Y - Xb)\\|_\infty \leq \lambda — the two are asymptotically equivalent (Bickel, Ritov and Tsybakov, 2009).

Proposition 8.2 — KKT conditions of the Lasso (proved) \hat\beta is a solution if and only if \frac{1}{n} X^\top\big(Y - X\hat\beta\big) = \lambda\, \hat z, \qquad \hat z_j = \mathrm{sign}(\hat\beta_j) \text{ if } \hat\beta_j \neq 0, \quad \hat z_j \in [-1,1] \text{ otherwise.}

Proof. The objective function is convex and proper; its subdifferential at b is \frac1n X^\top(Y - Xb) + \lambda\,\partial\\|b\\|_1 with \partial\\|b\\|_1 = \prod_j \partial\|b_j\| and \partial\|t\| = \{\mathrm{sign}(t)\} if t \neq 0, [-1,1] if t = 0. b \mapsto \frac{1}{2n}\\|Y-Xb\\|^2 + \lambda\\|b\\|_1 attains its minimum at \hat\beta iff 0 belongs to this subdifferential.∎

These conditions are the stem of all the proofs: applied to the “empirical noise” z^\star_j = \frac1n X_{\cdot j}^\top \varepsilon, they show that the choice \lambda \geq 2\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2p)/n} makes the event \{\|z^\star_j\| \leq \lambda/2,\ \forall j\} highly probable: each z^\star_j is \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2/n), so \mathbb{P}(\|z^\star_j\| \> \lambda/2) \leq 1/(2p) (Gaussian tail), and the union bound (Prop. 0.1) gives at least 1/2 — or 1-\delta by replacing \log(2p) with \log(2p/\delta) (Probability Theory for ML module, chapter 2, for Gaussian tails).

Definition 8.3 — Compatibility condition The design X satisfies the compatibility condition on the sparsity cones if there exists \kappa \> 0 such that, for every h \in \mathbb{R}^p with \\|h_{S^c}\\|_1 \leq 3\\|h_S\\|_1: \frac{1}{n}\\|Xh\\|_2^2 \;\geq\; \kappa^2\, \\|h_S\\|_2^2. Interpretation: the columns linked to the support must carry enough Euclidean norm, even in directions nearly orthogonal to S — a localized (on the cone) version of invertibility. Verified with high probability for Gaussian or bounded designs as soon as n \gtrsim s\log p (see Wainwright, High-Dimensional Statistics, chapter 7).

Theorem 8.4 — Oracle inequality of the Lasso (stated, proof skeleton) On the event \{\|z^\star_j\| \leq \lambda/2\ \forall j\} and under the compatibility condition, with \lambda = 2\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2p)/n}: \frac{\\|X(\hat\beta - \beta^\star)\\|_2^2}{n} \;\leq\; \frac{72}{\kappa^2}\, \frac{\sigma^2 s \log(2p)}{n}, \qquad \\|\hat\beta - \beta^\star\\|_1 \;\leq\; \frac{24}{\kappa^2}\, \sigma s \sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p)}{n}} (constants not optimal, consistent with the skeleton below; the sharp version of Bickel, Ritov and Tsybakov, 2009, has the same structure). Skeleton (the four steps to be completed as an exercise):

  • (a) Basic inequality: optimality of \hat\beta against b = \beta^\star gives, with h = \hat\beta - \beta^\star: \frac{1}{2n}\\|Xh\\|^2 \leq \frac1n h^\top X^\top\varepsilon + \lambda(\\|\beta^\star\\|_1 - \\|\beta^\star + h\\|_1);
  • (b) Cone: the KKT conditions (Prop. 8.2) and the event \{\|z^\star_j\| \leq \lambda/2\} turn (a) into \frac{1}{2n}\\|Xh\\|^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\\|h_{S^c}\\|_1 \leq \frac{3\lambda}{2}\\|h_S\\|_1: in particular h lies in the cone \\|h_{S^c}\\|_1 \leq 3\\|h_S\\|_1;
  • (c) Conversion: dropping the positive term of (b), then Cauchy-Schwarz \\|h_S\\|_1 \leq \sqrt s\,\\|h_S\\|_2, then compatibility (Def. 8.3) on the cone: \kappa^2\\|h_S\\|_2^2 \leq \\|Xh\\|^2/n \leq 3\lambda\sqrt s\,\\|h_S\\|_2;
  • (d) Assembly: the quadratic inequality of (c) gives \\|h_S\\|_2 \leq 3\lambda\sqrt s/\kappa^2; hence the prediction \\|Xh\\|^2/n \leq 9\lambda^2 s/\kappa^2, and return to (b) for the full \ell_1 norm: \\|h\\|_1 \leq 4\\|h_S\\|_1 \leq 12\lambda s/\kappa^2.

“Fast” rate s\log p/n: the Lasso estimates as well as if the true support S were known, up to the logarithm. Numbers (n = 100, p = 1000, s = 5, \sigma = 1): s\log(2p)/n \approx 0.38; ordinary least squares: not defined (p \> n); regression on the true support: s/n = 0.05 — the logarithmic surcharge is the price of the search among \binom{1000}{5} \approx 8.3\times10^{12} possible supports.

Beyond prediction: selection and inference The oracle inequality controls prediction; variable selection requires more: a sup-norm bound \\|\hat\beta - \beta^\star\\|_\infty \lesssim \sigma\sqrt{\log p/n} and “sign concentration” (exact support recovery under a \beta_{\min}-condition) — exactly the results of Lounici 2008, see ch. 10; for inference (p-values, confidence bands) in high dimension: debiasing, and the results of ch. 10 on the group Lasso. In practice: the theoretical \lambda is a starting point; calibration goes through cross-validation (ch. 9, code below).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Lasso, LassoCV
rng = np.random.default_rng(20)

n, p, s, sigma = 100, 1000, 5, 1.0
X = rng.normal(size=(n, p)) / np.sqrt(n)          # normalized columns
beta = np.zeros(p); beta[:s] = rng.choice([-1,1], s) * 1.5
y = X @ beta + sigma * rng.standard_normal(n)

lam_theo = 2*sigma*np.sqrt(2*np.log(2*p)/n)        # prop. 8.2
lasso = Lasso(alpha=lam_theo/2).fit(X, y)          # sklearn penalizes 1/(2n)
support = np.flatnonzero(lasso.coef_)
print(f"theoretical lambda {lam_theo:.3f}  support {support} (true [:5])")

cv = LassoCV(cv=5, n_alphas=60).fit(X, y)         # empirical calibration
err = np.linalg.norm(X @ cv.coef_ - X @ beta)**2 / n
print(f"CV lambda {cv.alpha_:.3f}  prediction error {err:.3f}"
      f"  (oracle s/n = {s/n:.3f})")

9. Aggregation of estimators

Definition 9.1 — The aggregation problem We have a dictionary f_1, \dots, f_M of estimators (hyperparameters, models, features); we want to build an aggregate \tilde f whose risk approaches that of the best of them (or of a combination). Three frameworks: (MS) model selection \tilde f = f_{\hat j}; (C) convex combination \tilde f = \sum_j \pi_j f_j, with \pi in the simplex; (L) linear combination (“L^2-convex” aggregation, for the quadratic loss). Quantitative goal: an oracle excess risk L(\tilde f) - \min_{j \leq M} L(f_j) \;\leq\; \text{price}(M, n) with a price that is small compared to the risk itself — typically \sqrt{\log M/n} or even \log M/n (quadratic loss, see Thm 9.3).

Definition 9.2 — Exponential weights With a temperature \eta \> 0 (and a prior \rho on the dictionary): \pi_j = \frac{\rho_j\, e^{-\eta n \hat L(f_j)}}{\sum_{k} \rho_k\, e^{-\eta n \hat L(f_k)}}, \qquad \tilde f = \sum_j \pi_j f_j \quad (\text{convex loss}). The weights reward good empirical performance exponentially; \rho penalizes complexity (uniform prior: \log M in the bound; sparse prior: recovery of Lasso-type results, ch. 10).

Theorem 9.3 — Convex aggregation oracle (proved for (i)) Losses \ell convex in the prediction, \ell \in [0,1], uniform prior.

  • (i) With probability \geq 1 - \delta: L(\tilde f) - \min_j L(f_j) \leq \sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}};
  • (ii) for the quadratic loss, the “progression” argument improves the price to \frac{2\log M}{n} + \sqrt{\ldots} (order \log M/n) — sharp form due to Catoni, Tsybakov; this is the regime attained by mirror averaging (ch. 10, Lounici 2007).

Proof. (i), full proof in three steps. Convexity: \ell convex in the prediction and the \pi_j sum to 1, so (Jensen conditionally on the weights): L(\tilde f) \;\leq\; \sum_j \pi_j \hat L(f_j) + \max_j \big\|L(f_j) - \hat L(f_j)\big\| \;\leq\; \min_j \hat L(f_j) + \max_j \big\|L(f_j) - \hat L(f_j)\big\|. Uniformization: each \hat L(f_j) is an average of bounded i.i.d. r.v.; Hoeffding (Thm 2.3) + union bound (Prop. 0.1) over j \leq M give, with probability 1-\delta, \max_j \|L - \hat L\| \leq \sqrt{\log(2M/\delta)/(2n)}. Back to the true risk: on this event, L(\tilde f) \leq \min_j \hat L(f_j) + \sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}} \leq \min_j L(f_j) + 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}}, and twice the square root is exactly \sqrt{2\log(2M/\delta)/n}.∎

Proposition 9.4 — Cross-validation as aggregation (statement) K-fold cross-validation (Machine Learning module, chapter 9) is a robust (MS) aggregation: the risk of the selected model \hat j_{\mathrm{CV}} satisfies oracle inequalities of the same type, with M replaced by the number of candidates and a correction for the validation bias (the training sample has size n(1-1/K)); the “one standard error” rule (pick the most parsimonious model within the error bar of the CV minimum) is the empirical analogue of the approximation/estimation trade-off of Prop. 1.2. Bounds of the Thm 9.3 type for CV: selection by cross-validation inherits oracle inequalities of the same type — literature on aggregation and sequential prediction (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

n, M = 400, 12
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, n); y = np.sin(2*x) + 0.3*rng.standard_normal(n)
target = lambda z: np.sin(2*z)

dictionary = [lambda z, d=d: np.polyval(np.polyfit(x, y, d), z) for d in range(1, M+1)]
losses = np.array([np.mean((f(x) - y)**2) for f in dictionary])   # empirical risk

eta = 20.0
pi = np.exp(-eta*n*(losses - losses.min())); pi /= pi.sum()
agg = lambda z: sum(w*f(z) for w, f in zip(pi, dictionary))

z = np.linspace(-2, 2, 400)
risk = lambda f: np.mean((f(z) - target(z))**2)
print("best model:", int(np.argmin(losses))+1,
      f"risk {risk(dictionary[int(np.argmin(losses))]):.4f}")
print(f"exponential aggregate: risk {risk(agg):.4f}")
print("weights (first 3):", np.round(pi[:3], 3))

PAC-Bayesian: the bridge Exponential weights are a posterior distribution; PAC-Bayesian bounds directly control L(\tilde f) against the weighted average of the risks, with a KL(weights, prior) divergence term instead of \log M — hence structured priors (sparse: recovery of \log p rates without a design condition): this is the direction of Alquier and Lounici (ch. 10). This literature connects Bayesian model choice and frequentist aggregation in a single inequality.

10. Research at CMAP (Lounici)

Karim Lounici — research directions Karim Lounici (CMAP, École Polytechnique) works on the statistical theory of high-dimensional learning: sparse estimation, oracle bounds for the Lasso and its extensions, aggregation of estimators. The four papers below structure chapters 8 and 9 of this module.

Paper 1 — “Sup-norm convergence rate and sign concentration property of Lasso and Dantzig estimators” K. Lounici, Electronic Journal of Statistics 2, 2008 — arXiv:0801.4610. After Knight and Fu (consistency in estimation), this paper establishes the rate of the Lasso and of the Dantzig selector in sup norm: \\|\hat\beta - \beta^\star\\|_\infty decreases exactly at \sigma\sqrt{\log p/n} (an optimal 1/\sqrt{n} factor), and deduces sign concentration: under a \beta_{\min} condition and “columns in general position”, the support and the signs are exactly recovered with probability tending to 1. It is the natural complement of Theorem 8.4 (prediction → selection).

Paper 2 — “Oracle inequalities and optimal inference under group sparsity” K. Lounici, M. Pontil, S. van de Geer, A. B. Tsybakov, Annals of Statistics 39(4), 2011 — arXiv:1007.1771. The group Lasso penalizes by blocks \sum_g \sqrt{d_g}\\|b_g\\|_2. The authors prove oracle inequalities for the prediction error and in \ell_2 under a group-wise restricted eigenvalue condition, showing the gain from grouping (the “effective dimension” becomes the number of active groups); above all, they construct simultaneous inference procedures (confidence bands for the components) achieving the minimal price s \log p/n: the first stone of high-dimensional inference of the “debiasing” type.

Paper 3 — “Generalized mirror averaging and D-convex aggregation” K. Lounici, Mathematical Methods of Statistics 16(3), 2007. Standard exponential-weights aggregation requires convex sets; this paper extends mirror averaging (aggregation by mirror averages, i.e. mirror descent in the geometry of the simplex) to the D-convex framework, with oracle inequalities for linear aggregation — the \log M/n regime of Theorem 9.3(ii), in a more general geometry (in the lineage of Juditsky-Nemirovski and Rigollet-Tsybakov).

Paper 4 — “PAC-Bayesian bounds for sparse regression estimation with exponential weights” P. Alquier, K. Lounici, Electronic Journal of Statistics 5, 2011 — HAL version. Sparse priors (decreasing masses on supports according to their size) + exponential weights: PAC-Bayesian oracle bounds for high-dimensional regression without a restrictive design condition, with the right \log p. The exact bridge between the PAC-Bayesian note of chapter 9 and the sparsity of chapter 8: aggregation replaces convex penalization.

Exercises

  1. Sample size (Hoeffding). A fixed classifier has a 0-1 loss. How many examples to guarantee \|\hat L - L\| \leq 0.02 with probability 0.99? And if the same guarantee is wanted simultaneously for M = 100 classifiers? Solution. n \geq \log(2/\delta)/(2\varepsilon^2) = \log 200/0.0008 = 5.298/0.0008 \approx 6623. For M classifiers, union bound: replace \delta by \delta/M = 10^{-4}: n \geq \log(2\times10^4)/0.0008 = 9.903/0.0008 \approx 12\,379 — the price of “simultaneous” is a factor \log M, not M.

  2. VC dimension of intervals. Show that the class of intervals \{x \mapsto \mathbf{1}_{x \in [a,b]}\} on \mathbb{R} has \mathrm{VC} = 2. Solution. Two points x_1 \< x_2 are shattered: the four labelings are obtained with [a,b] avoiding everything, containing only x_1, only x_2, or both. Three points x_1 \< x_2 \< x_3: the labeling \{x_1, x_3\} without x_2 is impossible (an interval containing the extremes contains the middle): no shattering, \mathrm{VC} = 2. (Bonus question: unions of k intervals → 2k, by the same parity argument on the components.)

  3. Sauer in small dimensions. Verify s(N,2) = 1 + N + N(N-1)/2 and compute s(10,2); show that the class of thresholds (Example 1.4) achieves exactly N+1 dichotomies, and conclude that the Sauer bound is not always tight. Solution. \sum_{k\leq2}\binom{N}{k} = 1 + N + N(N-1)/2; s(10,2) = 1 + 10 + 45 = 56. Thresholds produce N+1 dichotomies on N sorted points (cut after the k-th): 11 for N=10, far below 56 — the upper bound is generic, not attained by every class of dimension 2.

  4. Symmetrization to complete (Prop. 5.2). Redo the proof and justify: (a) the use of Jensen to swap \sup and \mathbb{E}_{S'}; (b) the legality of introducing the signs \sigma_i; (c) the final sub-additivity. Solution. (a) S \mapsto \sup_f \mathbb{E}_{S'}(\dots): for each S, the sup of an affine function of the distribution of S' bounds the expectation of the sup… more precisely \sup_f \mathbb{E}[g_f] \leq \mathbb{E}[\sup_f g_f] since for every f_0, \mathbb{E}g_{f_0} \leq \mathbb{E}\sup_f g_f (inequalities pass to the expectation, then take the supremum on the left). (b) Conditionally on (X_i, X'_i), the quantity \sigma_i(f(X'_i) - f(X_i)) has the same distribution as f(X'_i) - f(X_i) because \sigma_i is symmetric and independent — one may therefore insert \sigma_i without changing the distribution of the supremum. (c) \sup_f (A_f + B_f) \leq \sup_f A_f + \sup_f(-B_f) for any choice of decomposition, applied to A_f = \frac1n\sum\sigma_if(X'_i), B_f = -\frac1n\sum\sigma_if(X_i).

  5. Rademacher of constants (exact computation). Compute \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\{x \mapsto c : c \in [0,1]\}) and its equivalent as n \to \infty. Solution. \sup_{c \in [0,1]} \frac1n\|c\sum_i\sigma_i\| = \frac1n \max\big(\sum_i\sigma_i, 0\big). By symmetry of the signs, \mathbb{E}\max(\Sigma, 0) = \frac12\mathbb{E}\|\Sigma\| with \Sigma = \sum_i\sigma_i; by the CLT (Probability Theory for ML module, chapter 2), \mathbb{E}\|\Sigma\| \sim \sqrt{2n/\pi}, hence \hat{\mathcal{R}}_S \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}} \approx \frac{0.399}{\sqrt n}: even a “one-dimensional” class has nonzero complexity, of order exactly 1/\sqrt n.

  6. Le Cam for the Bernoulli mean (quadratic loss). Show that the quadratic minimax risk for estimating p from n Bernoulli draws satisfies \mathcal{M}_n \geq 1/(64n). Hint. Two parameters p_{1,2} = 1/2 \pm s, quadratic loss, separation \Delta = 2s; test A = \{\|\hat p - p_1\| \leq s\} and bound the binomial KL.

    On A^c: (\hat p - p_1)^2 \geq s^2 contributes s^2\,\mathbb{P}_1(A^c); on A: \|\hat p - p_2\| \geq s contributes s^2\,\mathbb{P}_2(A). Summing then averaging over the two problems: \mathcal{M}_n \geq \frac{s^2}{2}\big(\mathbb{P}_1(A^c) + \mathbb{P}_2(A)\big) \geq \frac{s^2}{2}(1-\delta). KL of the product distributions: n\,\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Bern}(p_1)\\|\mathrm{Bern}(p_2)) \approx n \times \frac{(2s)^2}{2p(1-p)} = 8ns^2 (to first order at p = 1/2). Taking s = 1/(4\sqrt n): \mathrm{KL} \approx 1/2, Pinsker gives \delta \leq 1/2, hence \mathcal{M}_n \geq \frac{s^2}{4} = \frac{1}{64n} — the 1/n order is what matters; the empirical mean achieves p(1-p)/n \leq 1/(4n): the parametric rate is optimal.

  7. Compatibility under orthonormal design. n = p, X orthonormal (X^\top X = n I, normalized columns). (a) Show that the compatibility condition holds with \kappa = 1. (b) Show that the Lasso reduces to soft-thresholding of the correlations z = X^\top Y/n: \hat\beta_j = \mathrm{sign}(z_j)(\|z_j\| - \lambda)_+. (c) Deduce \\|\hat\beta - \beta^\star\\|_2^2 \leq \sum_j \min(z_j^2, \lambda^2) and comment on the (s\log p)/n rate. Solution (sketch). (a) \\|Xh\\|^2/n = \\|h\\|_2^2 \geq \\|h_S\\|_2^2 (the cone restricts nothing). (b) The objective function separates coordinate by coordinate: \frac12(z_j - b_j)^2 + \lambda\|b_j\|, whose minimizer is the soft-thresholding. (c) Each “signal” coordinate loses at most \lambda^2, each “noise” coordinate at most z_j^2 \leq \lambda^2 on the event of Prop. 8.2: \\|\hat\beta - \beta^\star\\|_2^2 \lesssim s\lambda^2 + (p - s)\mathbb{E}[z^2 \wedge \lambda^2] \lesssim \sigma^2 s\log p/n (each z_j Gaussian with variance \sigma^2/n, tails bounded via the maximum, Prop. 2.6).

  8. Convex aggregation (Thm 9.3(i)) to prove step by step. Redo the three steps (convexity, uniformization, assembly) and write each inequality with its exact constants; where does the proof use (i) the uniform prior, (ii) the convexity of the loss, (iii) independence? Hint. Follow the proof of the theorem; for the uniformization step, write the Hoeffding bound for each j, take \delta/(2M) and sum.

    (i) The uniform prior gives \pi charging the best j: the bound depends only on \log M, the number of candidates; a non-uniform prior would replace \log M by \log(1/\rho_{j^\star}). (ii) Convexity allows L(\tilde f) \leq \sum_j\pi_j L(f_j); without convexity, one would be limited to selection (MS). (iii) Independence between the observations is used exactly in Hoeffding; it is also what makes \hat L(f_j) computable and concentrated — the same role as everywhere in the course since Prop. 1.2.
