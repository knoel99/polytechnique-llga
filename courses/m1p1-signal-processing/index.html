0. Review: Fourier series in the L² sense, Fubini

Every chapter of this course decomposes a signal on the complex exponentials e^{ik\omega_0 t}, and almost every proof exchanges a sum with an integral. This review fixes the two tools that make these manipulations legitimate: the geometry of the Hilbert space L^2, in which the exponentials form an orthonormal basis, and Fubini’s theorem.

L3 background used

  • The Hilbert space L^2: hermitian inner product \langle f, g\rangle = \int f(t)\,\overline{g(t)}\,dt, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace — Fourier = a change of basis in an infinite orthonormal basis;
  • Fubini’s theorem: if \iint |f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy < \infty (or for positive functions), the order of integration is free — it justifies all the convolution computations of chapter 3;
  • Integration by parts and changes of variable;
  • Complex geometric sequences, which give the orthogonality of the exponentials.

Proposition 0.1 — The exponentials form an orthogonal system On [0, T_0], set e_k(t) = e^{ik\omega_0 t} with \omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0. Then \langle e_k, e_\ell\rangle = \int_0^{T_0} e^{i(k-\ell)\omega_0 t}\,dt = \begin{cases} T_0 & \text{if } k = \ell,\\[2pt] \dfrac{e^{i(k-\ell)\omega_0 T_0} - 1}{i(k-\ell)\omega_0} = 0 & \text{if } k \neq \ell,\end{cases} since e^{i(k-\ell)\omega_0 T_0} = e^{2i\pi(k-\ell)} = 1. The e_k / \sqrt{T_0} therefore form an orthonormal system of L^2([0,T_0]).

Definition 0.2 — The Dirichlet and Fejér kernels The partial sum of order N of a Fourier series can be written as a convolution with the Dirichlet kernel D_N(\theta) = \sum_{k=-N}^{N} e^{ik\theta} = \frac{\sin\big((N+\tfrac12)\theta\big)}{\sin(\theta/2)}, while the Cesàro mean of the partial sums (arithmetic mean S_0, \dots, S_{N-1}) is written with the Fejér kernel F_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n<N} D_n, which has the decisive property of being positive (F_N \geq 0) and of mass 1.

Theorem 0.3 — Fejér convergence, density, completeness

  • Fejér: for f continuous and T_0-periodic, \sigma_N f = f * F_N \to f uniformly. The proof uses F_N \geq 0, \int F_N = 1 and the concentration of the mass of F_N near 0.
  • Consequence (density): the trigonometric polynomials \sum_{|k| \leq N} c_k e^{ik\omega_0 t} are dense in L^2([0,T_0]).
  • Consequence (Hilbert basis): the orthonormal system \{e_k/\sqrt{T_0}\} is complete: it is a Hilbert basis of L^2. The whole theory of Fourier series “in the L^2 sense” follows from it.

Dirichlet versus Fejér — the message to remember The partial sums themselves (Dirichlet kernel, which changes sign) do not converge uniformly for every continuous function: they “oscillate” near discontinuities (Gibbs phenomenon, quantified in ch. 2). The Cesàro average smooths these oscillations. Moral: in L^2, everything converges nicely; uniformly, one must regularize (averaging, windows) — exactly the role of the Hann windows of ch. 5.

1. Signals and linear systems

Concrete scene: a fine-tuned speech model whose accuracy drops because the Mel spectrogram was badly parameterized upstream. Before any spectrogram, one needs a precise language for signals and for the systems that transform them. This chapter sets it up with linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, which are entirely described by one convolution.

What breaks Three classic failures: sampling below the Nyquist rate, choosing an analysis window too short for the frequency resolution one needs, and treating filtering as a black box inside the ML pipeline.

Definition 1.1 — Signal, LTI system A signal is a function x : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} (or \mathbb{C}), or a sequence x : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{R} (discrete signal).

A system transforms one signal into another; it is LTI (linear time-invariant) if x \mapsto y = T(x) satisfies: linearity, and x(\cdot - \tau) \mapsto y(\cdot - \tau) for every delay \tau.

We write \delta_\tau for the Dirac mass at \tau (discrete: \delta_{n} = the sequence equal to 1 at n, 0 elsewhere).

Proposition 1.2 — Convolution representation (proved) Every LTI system (discrete, and continuous subject to a continuity condition) is entirely described by its impulse response h = T(\delta_0): y(t) = (x * h)(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} x(\tau)\, h(t - \tau)\, d\tau \qquad \Big(\text{discrete: } y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]\Big).

Proof. Discrete version (the continuous argument replaces the sums by integrals against Dirac masses, x = \int x(\tau)\,\delta_\tau\,d\tau, which requires a minimum of distribution theory — admitted here).

In discrete time, x = \sum_k x[k]\,\delta_k (a sum of “column” sequences). By linearity, T(x) = \sum_k x[k]\,T(\delta_k). By invariance, T(\delta_k) = \tau_k h, where (\tau_k h)[n] = h[n-k]. Hence y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]. Commutativity follows from the change of variable \tau' = t - \tau (Fubini: the integral is absolutely convergent under the stability assumptions below).∎

Theorem 1.3 — BIBO stability and causality (proved) The LTI system with impulse response h is:

  • BIBO stable (bounded input ⇒ bounded output) if and only if \sum_n |h[n]| < \infty (continuous: \int |h| < \infty);
  • causal (the output at time t depends only on the past) if and only if h[n] = 0 for n < 0.

Proof.

  • Stability (⇐). If \|x\|_\infty \leq M then |y[n]| \leq \sum_k |x[k]|\,|h[n-k]| \leq M \sum_k |h[k]|: the convolution series converges absolutely.
  • Stability (⇒). Suppose \sum |h[n]| = \infty and take the bounded input x[k] = \mathrm{sign}(h[-k]) (\|x\|_\infty \leq 1). Then y[0] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[-k] = \sum_k |h[k]| = \infty: the output explodes.
  • Causality (⇒). If h[n_0] \neq 0 for some n_0 < 0, the response to \delta_0 (a causal input, zero for t < 0) would be nonzero at n_0 < 0, before the impulse: contradiction.
  • Causality (⇐). If h[n] = 0 for n < 0, the sum y[n] = \sum_{k \leq n} x[k]\,h[n-k] involves only x[0], \dots, x[n].∎

Proposition 1.4 — Frequency response: the exponentials are eigenvectors (proved) For a stable LTI system, the input x(t) = e^{i2\pi\nu t} (a pure frequency) produces y(t) = H(\nu)\,e^{i2\pi\nu t} with H(\nu) = \hat h(\nu) = \int h(\tau)\, e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,d\tau. H is the frequency response: the system merely multiplies each frequency by a complex number (gain |H(\nu)|, phase \arg H(\nu)).

Proof. y(t) = \int h(\tau)\,x(t-\tau)\,d\tau = \int h(\tau)\,e^{i2\pi\nu(t-\tau)}\,d\tau = e^{i2\pi\nu t} \int h(\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\,d\tau. The exponential comes out unchanged up to a complex factor: it is an eigenvector, with eigenvalue H(\nu). This computational triviality is the heart of the whole course: filtering = multiplying the spectrum.∎

Example 1.5 — Elementary convolutions

  • Moving average (h = \frac1M \mathbf{1}_{[0,M]}): smooths, frequency response in \mathrm{sinc};
  • discrete differentiation (h = \delta_{1} - \delta_0): H(\nu) = e^{-i2\pi\nu} - 1, amplifies the high frequencies;
  • integrator (h = \mathbf{1}_{t \geq 0}): unstable in the BIBO sense (\int|h| = \infty).

A filter = a choice of h (or of H); the whole of chapter 5 consists in designing h from a desired H.

A computation by hand fixes the mechanics. Take the two-point average h = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12) (that is, h[0] = h[1] = \tfrac12) and the input x = (1, 2, 3). The formula y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k] gives y[0] = \tfrac12, \quad y[1] = \tfrac12(2 + 1) = 1.5, \quad y[2] = \tfrac12(3 + 2) = 2.5 \quad\text{and}\quad y[3] = \tfrac32: the output is one sample longer than the input, and each value is the mean of the current and previous inputs.

Its frequency response is H(\nu) = \tfrac12(1 + e^{-i2\pi\nu}), so |H(0)| = 1 (a constant passes unchanged) and |H(\tfrac12)| = 0 (the fastest alternation 1, -1, 1, \dots is erased exactly): a first low-pass filter.

2. The Fourier series

A periodic signal — a sustained note, the rotation of a motor, a periodic texture — is entirely described by the amplitudes and phases of its harmonics. This chapter computes these amplitudes (the Fourier coefficients), proves that the energy is the same in time and in frequency (Parseval), and links the smoothness of a signal to the speed at which its coefficients decay.

Theorem 2.1 — Fourier series decomposition (coefficients by projection) Let f be T_0-periodic, square integrable over one period. Then f = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} c_k\, e^{ik\omega_0 t} \quad \text{in } L^2, \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f(t)\, e^{-ik\omega_0 t}\, dt, the convergence taking place in L^2 norm (and pointwise toward \frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}{2} at discontinuity points if f is piecewise C^1: Dirichlet’s theorem).

The c_k form the spectrum: |c_k| magnitude, \arg c_k phase.

Proof. The e_k = e^{ik\omega_0t} are orthogonal (prop. 0.1) and form a Hilbert basis (completeness, th. 0.3). The decomposition in an infinite orthonormal basis follows the logic of finite dimension: the partial sum S_N f = \sum_{|k|\leq N} c_k e_k is the orthogonal projection of f onto \mathrm{Vect}\{e_k\}_{|k|\leq N}, since \langle f - S_Nf, e_j\rangle = 0 for |j| \leq N.

Since the basis is complete, \|f - S_N f\|_{L^2} \to 0. Explicitly: for every trigonometric polynomial p = \sum_{|k|\leq N} d_k e_k, Pythagoras gives \|f - p\|^2 = \|f - S_Nf\|^2 + \|S_Nf - p\|^2 \geq \|f - S_Nf\|^2: the projection is the best approximation — and the d_k that cancel the cross terms are exactly d_k = \langle f, e_k\rangle / \|e_k\|^2 = c_k.∎

Theorem 2.2 — Parseval’s identity (proved by density) \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} |f(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} |c_k|^2.

Proof. Pythagoras on f = S_N f + (f - S_N f) with \langle S_Nf, f - S_Nf\rangle = 0: \|f\|^2 = \|S_Nf\|^2 + \|f - S_Nf\|^2. Now \|S_Nf\|^2 = \sum_{|k|\leq N}|c_k|^2\|e_k\|^2 = T_0 \sum_{|k|\leq N}|c_k|^2, and \|f - S_Nf\|^2 \to 0 by theorem 2.1 (whose proof rests on Fejér density, th. 0.3). Dividing by T_0 and passing to the limit, the “time” energy equals the “frequency” energy.∎

Lemma 2.3 — Regularity and decay of the coefficients (by integrations by parts) If f is T_0-periodic, of class C^{p-1}, and f^{(p-1)} is piecewise C^1 (so that f^{(p)} is defined except at finitely many points, and integrable), then c_k = \frac{1}{(ik\omega_0)^p}\, c_k\big(f^{(p)}\big), \qquad k \neq 0. In particular:

  • a piecewise C^1 signal with a jump [f] ⇒ decay in 1/k: c_k \sim \frac{[f]}{T_0\,ik\omega_0} = \frac{[f]}{2i\pi k};
  • f continuous and piecewise C^2 ⇒ c_k = O(1/k^2);
  • f of class C^p ⇒ c_k = o(1/k^p).

Proof. Integrate by parts the coefficient of f' over one period. The boundary term \frac{1}{T_0}\big[ f(t)\, e^{-ik\omega_0t}\big]_0^{T_0} vanishes because f is continuous and periodic, so c_k(f') = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f'(t)\,e^{-ik\omega_0t}dt = \frac{ik\omega_0}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f(t)\,e^{-ik\omega_0t}dt = ik\omega_0\, c_k(f). Iterating p times (every derivative up to f^{(p-1)} is continuous) gives the formula. Since f^{(p)} is integrable, its coefficients tend to 0 (Riemann–Lebesgue lemma), hence c_k = o(k^{-p}).

If instead f has a jump [f] = f(0^+) - f(0^-) at t = 0, the boundary term no longer vanishes and contributes exactly \frac{[f]}{T_0\,ik\omega_0} to c_k. The remaining term is \frac{1}{ik\omega_0}c_k(f') = o(1/k), so the jump dictates the 1/k decay.∎

Example 2.4 — The square wave: explicit coefficients and the Gibbs phenomenon For the odd square wave f = \mathrm{sign}(\sin) (value ±1, jump of height 2): the direct computation (exercise 1, with solution) gives c_k = 0 \ (k \text{ even}), \qquad c_k = \frac{-2i}{\pi k} \ (k \text{ odd}) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{m \geq 0} \frac{\sin\big((2m+1)\omega_0 t\big)}{2m+1}. Decay in 1/k, consistent with lemma 2.3 (discontinuity).

The Gibbs phenomenon: near the jump, the partial sum S_N f overshoots the target by about \Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin u}{u}\,du\Big) - \frac12 \approx 8.95\,\% of the height of the jump, whatever N: the oscillation concentrates (width in O(1/N)) but its amplitude does not tend to 0.

Origin: the Dirichlet kernel changes sign (ch. 0); by averaging (Fejér) or by windowing (ch. 5), the overshoot disappears — at the price of a widened transition.

Why these three results structure the whole course Parseval: the energy can be read in the spectrum (noise, filtering, SNR).

Lemma 2.3: a smooth signal has a fast-decaying spectrum — the foundation of compression (one throws away the high frequencies) and of sampling (a fast-decaying spectrum is quickly negligible: ch. 4).

Gibbs: any abrupt cutoff in frequency costs oscillations in time — hence the soft windows of ch. 5.

3. The Fourier transform

Most signals are not periodic: a spoken word, a transient, an image patch. The Fourier transform extends the Fourier coefficients to such signals, replacing the discrete set of harmonics by a continuum of frequencies. This chapter lists its properties — the most important being that convolution becomes a product — and proves the uncertainty principle, which limits how concentrated a signal can be in time and in frequency at once.

Definition 3.1 — The Fourier transform For x \in L^1(\mathbb{R}) (then L^2 by density): \hat x(\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi \nu t}\, dt, \qquad x(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \hat x(\nu)\, e^{i 2\pi \nu t}\, d\nu. \nu in Hz (or \omega in rad/s with a factor 2\pi); the “continuous” transform generalizes the Fourier coefficients to non-periodic signals: a signal of infinite duration has a continuous spectrum.

Proposition 3.2 — Fundamental properties (proved)

Time Frequency Proof (sketch)

dilation x(at) \frac1{|a|}\hat x(\nu/a) change of variable + axis reversal if a < 0

Proof. Convolution (via Fubini). \widehat{x*h}(\nu) = \iint x(\tau)h(t-\tau)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt\,d\tau. The modulus of the integrand is |x|\,|h| \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2) (a product of integrable functions), so Fubini allows the exchange; set u = t - \tau: \int x(\tau)e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}d\tau \cdot \int h(u)e^{-i2\pi\nu u}du = \hat x\,\hat h.

Differentiation. By parts, \widehat{x'}(\nu) = [x e^{-i2\pi\nu t}] + i2\pi\nu\int x e^{-i2\pi\nu t}; the boundary terms vanish for x the derivative of an L^1 function.

Duality. \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}x)(t) = \int \hat x(\nu) e^{-i2\pi\nu t}d\nu = x(-t) (inversion formula evaluated at -t): Fourier² reverses time — hence product ↔︎ convolution by the previous property applied twice.

Dilation. With u = at: \int x(at)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt = \frac{1}{|a|}\int x(u)\,e^{-i2\pi(\nu/a)u}du = \frac{1}{|a|}\hat x(\nu/a). ∎

Proposition 3.3 — The Gaussian is its own transform (computed) \mathcal{F}\big(e^{-\pi t^2}\big)(\nu) = e^{-\pi \nu^2}.

Proof. Set g(t) = e^{-\pi t^2} and consider \hat g. We exploit the differential equation g' = -2\pi t\,g. Integrating by parts, \int t\,g(t)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\int g'(t)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\,i2\pi\nu\,\hat g = -i\nu\,\hat g. Hence \hat g'(\nu) = -i2\pi \int t g e^{-i2\pi\nu t} = -i2\pi(-i\nu)\hat g = -2\pi\nu\,\hat g — the same differential equation. Solution: \hat g(\nu) = \hat g(0)\,e^{-\pi\nu^2}, and \hat g(0) = \int g = 1 (Gaussian integral).∎

The key duo: convolution ↔︎ multiplication Filtering = convolving in time = multiplying in frequency:

Key result — filtering is a product in frequency y = x*h \iff \hat y = \hat x\,H.

Here H(\nu) = \hat h(\nu) is the frequency response (prop. 1.4). Low-pass: H \approx 1 for |\nu| < \nu_c, \approx 0 beyond.

This is also why the FFT speeds up the convolutions of CNNs: complexity \mathcal{O}(n \log n) instead of \mathcal{O}(n^2) (see the Deep Learning module). With proposition 3.3, a “narrow” Gaussian in time is “wide” in frequency — a foretaste of the next theorem.

Worked example — the rectangular pulse and its sinc Let x = \mathbf{1}_{[-T/2,\, T/2]} be a pulse of duration T. Direct integration gives \hat x(\nu) = \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt = \frac{e^{-i\pi\nu T} - e^{i\pi\nu T}}{-i2\pi\nu} = \frac{\sin(\pi\nu T)}{\pi\nu} = T\,\mathrm{sinc}(\nu T). Three readings.

  1. The spectrum first vanishes at \nu = \pm 1/T: a pulse of T = 1 ms occupies a main lobe of \pm 1 kHz, and shortening it to 0.1 ms widens the lobe to \pm 10 kHz — duration and bandwidth vary inversely, as theorem 3.4 will make precise.
  2. The value at zero, \hat x(0) = T, is the area of the pulse, as it must be since \hat x(0) = \int x.
  3. |\hat x(\nu)| decays only like 1/(\pi|\nu|), because x jumps: this is the continuous analogue of lemma 2.3, and the reason why the rectangular window of chapter 5 leaks so much.

By duality, the same computation gives the impulse response of the ideal low-pass filter, used in the proof of the Shannon theorem (ch. 4).

Theorem 3.4 — Heisenberg-Gabor uncertainty (proved) For x of energy 1, with standard deviations \sigma_t (time, around t_0) and \sigma_\nu (frequency): \sigma_t \, \sigma_\nu \ \geq\ \frac{1}{4\pi}, with equality if and only if x is a Gaussian (modulated and translated).

Proof. Time translations and frequency modulations do not change the product \sigma_t\sigma_\nu (delay = pure phase; modulation = a translation of the spectrum): one assumes t_0 = \nu_0 = 0, \|x\|_2 = 1.

Let us derive the key identity by parts (vanishing boundary terms): \int t\,\frac{d}{dt}|x|^2 dt = -\int |x|^2 = -1, \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{d}{dt}|x|^2 = 2\mathrm{Re}(x\,\overline{x'}), hence 2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle t\,x, x'\rangle = -1, i.e. |\langle tx, x'\rangle| \geq 1/2.

Cauchy-Schwarz: \tfrac12 \leq \|t x\|_2\,\|x'\|_2 = \sigma_t\,\|x'\|_2. Finally \|x'\|_2^2 = \|\widehat{x'}\|_2^2 = \|i2\pi\nu\,\hat x\|_2^2 = (2\pi\sigma_\nu)^2 (Parseval + differentiation). Hence \sigma_t\sigma_\nu \geq 1/(4\pi).

Equality: Cauchy-Schwarz must be saturated, x' = \lambda t x: the solutions are the Gaussians e^{-\pi t^2/\sigma^2} (up to a linear phase and a translation).∎

Definition 3.5 — Useful distributions: Dirac and comb Without formalism: the Dirac mass \delta is “the function” of mass 1 concentrated at 0, defined by \int f\,\delta = f(0) (a distribution, i.e. a continuous linear form on test functions).

We admit: \hat\delta = 1, \hat 1 = \delta (duality), and the Dirac comb \,\text{Ш}_{T_e}(t) = \sum_n \delta(t - nT_e), whose transform is a comb: \widehat{\text{Ш}_{T_e}}(\nu) = \frac{1}{T_e}\,\text{Ш}_{1/T_e}(\nu). These objects make rigorous: the spectrum of a sinusoid (two \delta lines), sampling (a product with a comb, ch. 4), white noise (ch. 7).

Applications of analog processing: modulation and Fourier optics. The official syllabus illustrates Fourier analysis and analog filtering with two applications, modulation and Fourier optics in astronomy. Both are direct consequences of the properties above.

Example 3.6 — Amplitude modulation and coherent demodulation A voice signal x of bandwidth B = 4 kHz cannot be radiated efficiently as is. Multiply it by a carrier at f_c = 1 MHz: y(t) = x(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t). Since \cos(2\pi f_c t) = \tfrac12(e^{i2\pi f_c t} + e^{-i2\pi f_c t}), the modulation property gives \hat y(\nu) = \tfrac12\big[\hat x(\nu - f_c) + \hat x(\nu + f_c)\big]: the spectrum is copied around \pm f_c and occupies [996, 1004] kHz. Stations with carriers at least 2B = 8 kHz apart do not overlap (frequency-division multiplexing).

To demodulate, multiply again by the carrier: y(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t) = \tfrac12 x(t) + \tfrac12 x(t)\cos(4\pi f_c t), whose second term lives around \pm 2f_c; a low-pass filter with cutoff between B and 2f_c - B keeps \tfrac12 x. The receiver is thus a product followed by a filter, two operations that the Fourier transform makes transparent.

Example 3.7 — Fourier optics: a telescope is a low-pass filter In the far field, or in the focal plane of a lens of focal length f, the complex amplitude of light diffracted by an aperture with transmission p(x, y) is the two-dimensional Fourier transform of p, evaluated at the spatial frequencies (u, v) = (x'/\lambda f,\ y'/\lambda f) (Fraunhofer diffraction); the observed intensity is its squared modulus.

Slit. A slit of width a is a rectangle, so by the worked example above the intensity is proportional to \mathrm{sinc}^2(a x'/\lambda f), with first zeros at x' = \pm\lambda f/a: the narrower the slit, the wider the pattern, as in the uncertainty principle.

Telescope. A circular pupil of diameter D gives the Airy pattern, whose first dark ring is at the angle \theta \approx 1.22\,\lambda/D. A star, a point source, is imaged as this pattern, the point spread function, and the image of an extended object is the convolution of the object with it: the telescope is a two-dimensional linear time-invariant system (chapter 1).

Its transfer function, the Fourier transform of the point spread function, is the autocorrelation of the pupil and vanishes beyond the spatial frequency D/\lambda: no detail finer than about \lambda/D survives. For D = 8 m and \lambda = 500 nm, \theta \approx 1.22 \times 500 \times 10^{-9}/8 \approx 7.6 \times 10^{-8} \ \text{rad}, about 0.016 arcsecond.

Radio interferometers push the same logic further: each pair of antennas separated by a baseline b measures one Fourier coefficient of the sky image at spatial frequency b/\lambda, and the image is reconstructed by an inverse Fourier transform from these samples.

4. Sampling: the Shannon theorem

A computer stores only the values of a signal at regularly spaced instants. This chapter answers the question every acquisition chain must face: when do these samples determine the continuous signal exactly, and what goes wrong — aliasing — when they do not?

Theorem 4.1 — Shannon-Nyquist (proved) Let x be band-limited: \hat x(\nu) = 0 for |\nu| \geq B, and f_s = 1/T_e > 2B. Then the samples x_n = x(nT_e) suffice to reconstruct exactly x:

Key result — Shannon interpolation x(t) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} x_n\, \mathrm{sinc}\Big(\frac{t}{T_e} - n\Big), \qquad \mathrm{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}.

Proof.

  • Step 1 (sampling = periodizing the spectrum). Ideal sampling is written x_s(t) = x(t)\cdot\text{Ш}_{T_e}(t) = \sum_n x_n\,\delta(t - nT_e). Product in time ⇒ convolution in frequency (prop. 3.2): \hat x_s(\nu) = \hat x * \widehat{\text{Ш}_{T_e}}(\nu) = \frac{1}{T_e}\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \hat x\Big(\nu - \frac{k}{T_e}\Big). The spectrum of x_s is the periodization of the spectrum of x, of period f_s.
  • Step 2 (no overlap). If f_s > 2B, the copies (centered at 0, \pm f_s, \pm 2f_s, \dots, each of half-width B) are disjoint: in the central band |\nu| < f_s/2 we read \hat x = T_e\,\hat x_s.
  • Step 3 (ideal low-pass filtering). Let us multiply by R(\nu) = T_e\,\mathbf{1}_{|\nu| < f_s/2}: \hat x = R\,\hat x_s, that is, x = r * x_s where r = \check R is the inverse transform of the rectangle — computed by direct integration: r(t) = T_e\int_{-f_s/2}^{f_s/2} e^{i2\pi\nu t}d\nu = \frac{\sin(\pi f_s t)}{\pi f_s t}\cdot f_s T_e = \mathrm{sinc}(f_s t).
  • Step 4 (interpolation formula). Convolving sinc with the comb of samples gives, since \mathrm{sinc}\big(f_s(t - nT_e)\big) = \mathrm{sinc}(f_st - n) and since the sinc equals 1 at 0 and 0 at the other integers (the samples do not “talk” to each other): x(t) = \sum_n x_n\, \mathrm{sinc}(f_s t - n).

If f_s \leq 2B, the copies overlap: the information is lost without return (two different signals have the same samples).∎

Proposition 4.2 — Aliasing, in formulas After sampling at f_s, every frequency \nu is seen at the apparent frequency \nu_{\text{app}} = \min_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}\ \big|\nu - k f_s\big| \ \in\ \Big[0, \frac{f_s}{2}\Big], the image of \nu in the fundamental band [-f_s/2, f_s/2] modulo f_s.

Example: f_s = 80 Hz, \nu = 60 Hz ⇒ \nu_{\text{app}} = |60 - 80| = 20 Hz (the line “moves backward”); a rising chirp passes f_s/2, then goes back down — the ghost trace of the spectrogram of exercise 3. Wheels that seem to turn backward in the movies, moiré of images: the same phenomenon.

Bandpass sampling For a signal modulating only a narrow band [f_c - B, f_c + B] (radio, sensors), the useful condition bears on the width 2B and not on the highest frequency: it suffices to choose f_s such that the copies of the band do not overlap.

Example: band [19, 21] MHz (f_c = 20, B = 1), f_s = 4.2 MHz, so that 21 = 5 f_s.

Every frequency f of the band folds back to 21 - f \in [0, 2] MHz, inside the fundamental band [0, 2.1] MHz and without collision — the spectrum is transposed to baseband, here flipped (the spectral reversal depends on the parity of the multiple of f_s).

Beware that f_s = 4 MHz, although equal to 2 \times the width, would fail: 20 = 5 \times 4 sits in the middle of the band, and the band and its mirror image fold onto the same interval [0, 1] MHz. This is the basis of software receivers (SDR).

Example 4.3 — Acquisition chain and practical reconstruction Chain: analog signal → analog anti-aliasing filter (cuts at f_s/2, indispensable before sampling) → sampling f_s → quantization (b bits: quantization noise at \approx 6b dB of dynamic range) → digital. Audio CD: B = 20 kHz ⇒ f_s = 44.1 kHz, 16 bits.

The Shannon sinc is perfectly localized in frequency but infinitely long in time: one truncates and windows it (sinc × Hann over M points) — hence a slight aliasing and a soft transition, quantifiable with the tools of ch. 5; linear interpolation (triangle) is its cheap version.

import numpy as np
fs = 200                       # sampling frequency
t  = np.arange(0, 1, 1/fs)
f0 = 60                        # signal 60 Hz
x  = np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*t)

X = np.fft.rfft(x)             # spectrum (positive frequencies)
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1/fs)
peak = freqs[np.argmax(np.abs(X))]
print("dominant frequency:", peak, "Hz")

# Aliasing: sampling a 60 Hz signal at 80 Hz "sees" it at 20 Hz
fs2 = 80; t2 = np.arange(0, 1, 1/fs2)
x2 = np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*t2)
print("seen at:", np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x2),1/fs2)[np.argmax(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x2)))], "Hz")

# Shannon reconstruction on a fine grid (sum over the 200 available samples;
# the error is largest near the ends, where the missing samples are felt)
tr = np.arange(0, 1, 1/2000); xr = np.zeros_like(tr)
for n in range(len(x)):
    xr += x[n]*np.sinc(fs*tr - n)
print("max error after reconstruction:", np.abs(np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*tr) - xr).max())

5. DFT, FFT and digital filtering

Once a signal is a finite list of N samples, its spectrum must be computed by a finite sum, and filters become finite recursions. This chapter defines the discrete Fourier transform, shows how the FFT computes it in about N \log_2 N operations instead of N^2, and explains how to design a digital filter with a prescribed frequency response.

Definition 5.1 — The DFT For x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}:

Key result — the DFT X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n\, e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, N-1.

The DFT samples the transform of the implicitly periodized signal (k \leftrightarrow \nu_k = k f_s/N). Two links to know:

  • DFT ↔︎ Fourier series: if x is T_0-periodic sampled with N points per period and f_s > 2B: X_k = N\,c_k \ \ (\text{for } k < N/2), \qquad X_{N-k} = N c_{-k};
  • DFT ↔︎ Fourier transform: for a segment of duration T = NT_e of an aperiodic signal: \hat x(\nu_k) \approx T_e\,X_k (the so-called “rectangular” approximation, with the spectral leakage of prop. 5.4).

Proposition 5.2 — Circular convolution (proved) Let x \circledast h be the circular convolution (indices modulo N): y_n = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x_m\,h_{(n-m) \bmod N}. Then \mathrm{DFT}(x \circledast h)_k = X_k\,H_k.

Proof. Y_k = \sum_n \sum_m x_m h_{(n-m) \bmod N}\,\omega^{kn} \qquad\text{with } \omega = e^{-2\pi i/N}. For m fixed, n \mapsto p = (n-m) \bmod N is a bijection of \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} and \omega^{kn} = \omega^{k(m+p)} (since \omega^{kN} = 1). Hence Y_k = \sum_m x_m\omega^{km}\sum_p h_p\omega^{kp} = X_k H_k. The linear convolution is obtained by zero-padding both signals to N' \geq n + m - 1 points: the “circular” wrap-around then no longer has any effect (the overflowing indices fall on zeros).∎

Theorem 5.3 — Radix-2 FFT (proved: the butterfly recurrence) If N = 2^q, the DFT is computed in T(N) = \mathcal{O}(N \log N) operations.

Proof. Separate the even and odd sums: X_k = \underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x_{2m}\,\omega^{2mk}}_{E_k} +\ \omega^{k}\,\underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x_{2m+1}\,\omega^{2mk}}_{O_k}. Now \omega^2 = e^{-4\pi i/N} is an N/2-th root of unity: E and O are two DFTs of size N/2, periodic with period N/2.

Moreover \omega^{k + N/2} = -\omega^k. Hence the butterfly: X_k = E_k + \omega^k O_k, \qquad X_{k+N/2} = E_k - \omega^k O_k, \qquad k = 0,\dots,\tfrac{N}{2}-1, which produces the N outputs from the N inputs in O(N) multiply-adds once E, O are known.

Recurrence: T(N) = 2T(N/2) + cN = cN\log_2 N. For N = 2^{20} (one second of audio): \approx 2\times10^7 units versus 10^{12} for the definition — a factor of 50,000.∎

Worked example — a 4-point DFT, directly and by butterflies Take x = (1, 2, 0, -1), so N = 4 and \omega = e^{-i\pi/2} = -i.

  • Directly: \begin{aligned} X_0 &= 1 + 2 + 0 - 1 = 2, \\ X_1 &= 1 + 2(-i) + 0 + (-1)(-i)^3 = 1 - 2i - i = 1 - 3i, \\ X_2 &= 1 - 2 + 0 + 1 = 0, \\ X_3 &= \overline{X_1} = 1 + 3i \end{aligned} (for a real signal, X_{N-k} = \overline{X_k}).
  • By butterflies (theorem 5.3): the even samples (1, 0) have the 2-point DFT E = (1, 1), the odd samples (2, -1) have O = (1, 3), and \begin{aligned} X_0 &= E_0 + O_0 = 2, & X_1 &= E_1 + \omega O_1 = 1 - 3i, \\ X_2 &= E_0 - O_0 = 0, & X_3 &= E_1 - \omega O_1 = 1 + 3i: \end{aligned} the same four numbers, assembled from two DFTs of size 2.
  • Check by Parseval: \sum_n |x_n|^2 = 6 and \frac1N\sum_k |X_k|^2 = \frac14(4 + 10 + 0 + 10) = 6.
  • Reading: X_2 = 0 says that x contains no component at the alternating frequency f_s/2, which can be checked directly since 1 - 2 + 0 - (-1) = 0.

Proposition 5.4 — Windows: quantified lobes Analyzing a segment of signal = multiplying it by a window w ⇒ convolving the spectrum with \hat w (prop. 3.2): a pure line becomes the profile \hat w. Characteristics (window of length M, bin = f_s/M, classical values of Harris 1978):

Window Main lobe 1st side lobe Decay
rectangular ±1 bin −13 dB −6 dB/octave
Hann ±2 bins −31 dB −18 dB/octave
Hamming ±2 bins −41 dB −6 dB/octave
Blackman ±3 bins −58 dB −18 dB/octave

Universal trade-off: low side lobes ⇒ a wide main lobe (resolution lost). The rectangular one “leaks” at −13 dB: a strong line masks a weak neighboring line at −60 dB; Hann is the default choice.

The Z-transform and the transfer function The Z-transform of a discrete signal (x_n) is the power series X(z) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} x_n z^{-n}, defined on its region of convergence, an annulus of the complex plane. On the unit circle z = e^{i2\pi\nu} it reduces to the DTFT, when the circle lies in that region.

Three properties make it the algebra of digital filters: it is linear; a delay by one sample multiplies it by z^{-1}; and a convolution becomes a product, Y(z) = H(z)X(z), where H(z) = \sum_n h_n z^{-n} is the transfer function of the filter.

A recursion y_n + \sum_{j \geq 1} a_j y_{n-j} = \sum_k b_k x_{n-k} therefore becomes A(z)Y(z) = B(z)X(z), so H(z) = B(z)/A(z): a rational function whose poles are the roots of A.

A filter is causal when its region of convergence is the outside of a circle, |z| > r, and then stable exactly when r < 1, that is when all its poles lie inside the unit disk.

Worked example — a first-order recursive filter Take y_n = a\,y_{n-1} + x_n. The delay rule gives Y(z)(1 - a z^{-1}) = X(z), so H(z) = 1/(1 - az^{-1}), with a single pole at z = a.

Expanding the geometric series for |z| > |a| gives H(z) = \sum_{n \geq 0} a^n z^{-n}, so the impulse response is h_n = a^n for n \geq 0: it is summable, hence the filter is stable, if and only if |a| < 1, as theorem 5.5 states.

On the unit circle, |H(\nu)|^2 = 1/(1 - 2a\cos 2\pi\nu + a^2); for a = 0.9 the gain is 1/(1 - 0.9)^2 = 100 (20 dB) at \nu = 0 and 1/1.9^2 \approx 0.28 at \nu = 1/2: an exponential smoother, that is, a low-pass filter. Its output driven by white noise is exactly the AR(1) process of chapter 7.

Digital filters: FIR and IIR FIR (finite impulse response): y_n = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k x_{n-k} — always stable (\sum_k |b_k| < \infty: th. 1.3), linear phase if the b_k are symmetric, designed by windowing.

IIR (infinite): y_n = \sum b_k x_{n-k} - \sum_{j\geq1} a_j y_{n-j}, transfer function H(z) = B(z)/A(z) — more selective at equal coefficient counts, but stability must be checked.

Theorem 5.5 — Stability of an IIR: the poles (proved) For a rational IIR, BIBO stable ⇔ all the poles of H(z) lie in the open disk |z| < 1.

Proof. The impulse response of a system with (simple) poles p_1, \dots, p_r is a combination of \mathcal{O}(n^s)\,p_j^n: each mode contributes \sum_n |p_j|^n, a geometric series convergent ⇔ |p_j| < 1. A pole on the unit disk ⇒ a marginal or divergent mode; outside ⇒ a divergent exponential (the accumulator h = \mathbf{1}, pole at 1, illustrates this).∎

Example 5.6 — Designing a low-pass FIR by windowing, step by step Objective: H_d(\nu) = \mathbf{1}_{|\nu| \leq \nu_c} (ν normalized by f_s).

  1. Ideal response in time: h_d[n] = 2\nu_c\,\mathrm{sinc}(2\nu_c n) (inverse FT of the rectangle, cf. proof of th. 4.1) — infinite and non-causal.
  2. Delay of M/2 for causality (and symmetry ⇒ linear phase).
  3. Windowing: h[n] = h_d[n - M/2]\,w[n - M/2], n = 0,\dots,M.
  4. Analysis: H = H_d * \hat w; the transition band equals the width of the main lobe of \hat w — rule of thumb M \cdot \Delta\nu \approx 4 (Hann), \approx 6 (Blackman); the in-band ripple equals the level of the side lobes (prop. 5.4).

The effective frequency response is the DTFT of h, H(\nu) = \sum_n h[n]e^{-2\pi i\nu n}, evaluated by zero-padding the FFT (trick: a DFT with N_{\text{fft}} \gg M points interpolates the DTFT).

A finer alternative: frequency-domain least squares — minimize \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} |H_b(\omega) - D(\omega)|^2 over a frequency grid, a quadratic problem in b, hence a linear system (Parks-McClellan equiripple method: the same spirit in infinite norm).

For IIRs, one projects an analog prototype via the bilinear transform s \mapsto \frac{2}{T_e}\frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 + z^{-1}} (Butterworth, Chebyshev…) while keeping the poles inside the disk — the “smooth” filter of example 5.7.

Images: two-dimensional convolution and DFT A grey-level image is a two-dimensional discrete signal x[m, n]. A linear shift-invariant operation on it is a 2D convolution y[m, n] = \sum_{k,l} h[k, l]\,x[m-k, n-l] with a small kernel h, and the 2D DFT X[k, l] = \sum_{m,n} x[m, n]\,e^{-i2\pi(km/M + ln/N)} turns it into a product, exactly as in one dimension (proposition 5.2).

The 2D DFT is separable: it is computed by 1D FFTs along the rows, then along the columns. Low spatial frequencies carry the smooth regions, high ones the edges and textures, and sampling an image too coarsely aliases fine periodic patterns into moiré (proposition 4.2).

Example 5.8 — Blur and edge detection, read in frequency The 3 \times 3 box blur averages each pixel with its 8 neighbours. Its kernel is separable, the product of (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13) along each axis, so its frequency response is H(\nu_1, \nu_2) = g(\nu_1)g(\nu_2) \quad\text{with}\quad g(\nu) = (1 + 2\cos 2\pi\nu)/3. It equals 1 at \nu = 0 (flat regions pass unchanged), vanishes at \nu = 1/3, but equals -1/3 at \nu = 1/2: the finest checkerboard is attenuated but survives with inverted sign, an artefact that a Gaussian kernel avoids because its transform stays positive (proposition 3.3).

The discrete Laplacian kernel, with -4 at the centre and 1 at the four neighbours, has response H(\nu_1, \nu_2) = 2\cos 2\pi\nu_1 + 2\cos 2\pi\nu_2 - 4 = -4\big(\sin^2\pi\nu_1 + \sin^2\pi\nu_2\big). It is 0 at the origin, so constant regions are erased, and its magnitude is largest, 8, at (\tfrac12, \tfrac12): a high-pass filter that responds to edges. The first layer of a convolutional network learns kernels of exactly this kind (Deep Learning module, chapter 4).

6. Time-frequency analysis and wavelets

The Fourier transform of a whole recording says which frequencies are present, but not when: a melody and the same melody played backward have spectra of the same magnitude. This chapter localizes the analysis in time, first with the short-time Fourier transform (one fixed window), then with wavelets (windows whose length adapts to the frequency).

Definition 6.1 — STFT and spectrogram \mathrm{STFT}_x(t, \nu) = \int x(\tau)\, w(\tau - t)\, e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}\, d\tau, with w a short window (Hann). The spectrogram |\mathrm{STFT}_x|^2 describes the energy across time and frequency: it is the image of sound that a convolutional network “sees” (ch. 8).

Proposition 6.2 — Exact resolution of the STFT At each instant, the STFT is the FT of the windowed segment x\cdot w(\cdot - t): its frequency resolution is the width of the main lobe of \hat w (prop. 5.4); its time resolution is the duration T of the window. For Hann: main lobe \pm 2/T in frequency, hence \Delta t = T, \qquad \Delta\nu \approx \frac{2}{T} \ \ (\text{Hann}), \qquad \Delta t\,\Delta\nu \approx 2, to be compared with the floor 1/(4\pi) \approx 0.08 (standard deviations) of th. 3.4: the STFT works at a resolution fixed in advance, far from the floor, but with time-frequency cells all identical.

Long window ⇒ good frequency resolution, poor time resolution — and conversely: the trade-off is irreversible for a given window.

Gabor frames: sampling the STFT A continuously stored STFT is redundant; one samples it on a grid (t,\nu) = (m\,a,\ n\,b). Results (admitted): if ab < 1 (a fine enough grid), the family of translated-modulated windows w_{m,n} forms a Gabor frame: stable reconstruction by a dual window; at the critical density ab = 1, no well-localized orthonormal basis exists (the Balian-Low theorem): one must accept redundancy (or change the geometry: wavelets below).

This is exactly the structure of spectrograms: frames every 10 ms, N frequencies — a frame, not a basis.

Definition 6.3 — Wavelets: analysis at variable scale Mother wavelet \psi (zero mean, localized); \psi_{a,b}(t) = \frac1{\sqrt a}\psi\big(\frac{t-b}{a}\big); the CWT samples (a,b), often in octaves a = 2^{-j} (scalogram, Q = \Delta\nu/\nu constant): good time localization at high frequency, frequency localization at low frequency — suited to sounds (closely spaced harmonics at the bottom of the spectrum, brief transients at the top), where the STFT is uniform.

Example 6.4 — Haar, a complete example down to reconstruction The Haar wavelet: \varphi = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1)} (scale), \psi = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2)} - \mathbf{1}_{[1/2,1)} (detail). Signal x = (9, 7, 3, 5):

  • level 1: averages a_1 = (8, 4), details d_1 = (1, -1);
  • level 2: a_2 = (6), d_2 = (2);
  • coding: x \leftrightarrow (a_2, d_2, d_1) = (6, 2, 1, -1);
  • reconstruction: 9 = 6+2+1, 7 = 6+2-1, 3 = 6-2+1, 5 = 6-2-1.

The multiresolution equations: \varphi(t) = \sqrt2\sum_k h_k\varphi(2t-k), \qquad \psi(t) = \sqrt2\sum_k g_k\varphi(2t-k); for Haar, h = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}), g = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}) — h = averaging filter (low-pass), g = differentiator (high-pass).

The Daubechies D_N compactify this scheme with 2N coefficients and N vanishing moments: on a piecewise regular signal, the detail coefficients decay like 2^{-Nj} — this is what makes the coding sparse (a polynomial of degree < N has exactly zero details: Haar = D_1 annihilates constants).

DWT = a filter bank, and the JPEG 2000 link The DWT is implemented without any integral: a cascade of low-pass (h) / high-pass (g) filterings followed by decimation by a factor 2, iterated on the low branch; the “quadrature mirror” conditions on (h,g) cancel the aliasing introduced by the decimations. JPEG 2000 codes the subbands of a biorthogonal DWT (5/3 lossless, 9/7 lossy): an image = a 2D signal, and compression exploits lemma 2.3 (regularity ⇒ small coefficients). Same bank, from denoising to compression: the theory does not change.

Definition 6.5 — The discrete cosine transform (DCT-II) For x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}, X_k = s_k\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \cos\Big(\frac{\pi}{N}\big(n + \tfrac12\big)k\Big), \qquad k = 0, \dots, N-1, with s_0 = \sqrt{1/N} and s_k = \sqrt{2/N} otherwise, which makes the transform orthonormal. Up to a phase factor, it is the DFT of the even, mirror-symmetric extension of x to length 2N.

The DFT implicitly repeats the block periodically and so creates a jump between x_{N-1} and x_0; the mirror extension has no jump. By lemma 2.3 (regularity implies fast decay of the coefficients), the DCT coefficients of a smooth block decay much faster: the energy is compacted into few coefficients.

For strongly correlated signals, such as an AR(1) with correlation close to 1, the DCT is close to the Karhunen–Loève transform, the PCA of the block.

Example 6.6 — Energy compaction, then JPEG in four steps For the ramp x = (1, 2, \dots, 8), the orthonormal DCT is approximately (12.73,\ -6.44,\ 0,\ -0.67,\ 0,\ -0.20,\ 0,\ -0.05): the first two coefficients hold 99.8% of the energy.

The unitary DFT of the same block has moduli 12.73, 3.70, 2.00, 1.53, 1.41, \dots, and its three lowest frequencies hold only 92.8% of the energy, because of the jump from 8 back to 1.

The JPEG standard exploits this.

  1. The image is cut into 8 \times 8 blocks.
  2. Each block is transformed by the 2D DCT, which is separable: a 1D DCT on the rows, then on the columns.
  3. Each coefficient X[k, l] is quantized to \mathrm{round}(X[k, l]/Q[k, l]), with steps Q that grow with frequency, because the eye is less sensitive to fine detail; most high-frequency coefficients become 0. This is the only lossy step.
  4. The coefficients are read in zig-zag order from low to high frequency, so that the zeros form long runs, which are coded by run lengths and Huffman codes.

At high compression the blocks become visible at their borders; the wavelet transform of JPEG 2000 (note above) avoids this blocking.

7. Random signals and spectral estimation

Noise, speech and sensor readings are never the same twice: each recording is one realization of a random process, and only averages are reproducible. This chapter defines stationary processes, their autocorrelation and their power spectral density, and shows how to estimate that density from a finite recording without being fooled by the variance of the periodogram.

Definition 7.1 — Process, wide-sense stationarity (WSS) A random signal is a family X(t, \omega) of random variables. It is wide-sense stationary if its mean is constant m = \mathbb{E}[X(t)] and if its autocorrelation R_X(t, t+\tau) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X(t)-m)\,\overline{(X(t+\tau)-m)}\big] depends only on the lag \tau (written R_X(\tau)). This is the right framework for noise, speech, sensors: each realization is a signal of this course, but the interesting quantities are expectations.

Proposition 7.2 — Properties of the autocorrelation R_X(0) = \mathbb{E}|X - m|^2 is the average power; |R_X(\tau)| \leq R_X(0) \qquad \big(\text{Cauchy-Schwarz: } |\mathbb{E}[X_0\overline{X_\tau}]| \leq \sqrt{R(0)R(0)}\big); R_X is hermitian (R_X(-\tau) = \overline{R_X(\tau)}) and positive definite. “Noise” is decorrelated at a distance: R(\tau) \to 0.

Theorem 7.3 — Wiener-Khinchin (statement) and the spectral density For a WSS process, there exists a positive measure S_X, the power spectral density, such that R_X(\tau) = \int e^{i2\pi\nu\tau}\, dS_X(\nu), \qquad\text{and if } R_X \in L^1:\quad S_X(\nu) = \widehat{R_X}(\nu). The power is distributed across the spectrum: \mathbb{E}|X-m|^2 = R_X(0) = \int dS_X: Parseval (th. 2.2) becomes a definition.

(The proof — Bochner’s theorem — goes beyond the scope of the course; the intuition: a WSS process is an incoherent superposition of sinusoids, each carrying its share of the power.)

  • White noise: decorrelated to all orders, R = \sigma^2\delta ⇒ S = \sigma^2 constant — “all frequencies on equal footing”. A band-limited white noise (physically realizable): S = \sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{|\nu| \leq B} ⇒ R(\tau) = 2B\sigma^2\,\mathrm{sinc}(2B\tau): decorrelation at the scale 1/B (direct computation of the FT of the rectangle, cf. th. 4.1).
  • Filtered noise: Y = h * X ⇒ S_Y = |H|^2\,S_X (colored noise): the filter shapes the spectrum squared.
  • AR(1) (auto-regressive model X_n = \rho X_{n-1} + W_n, |\rho| < 1): R[\tau] = \frac{\sigma_W^2}{1-\rho^2}\,\rho^{|\tau|} and S(\nu) = \frac{\sigma_W^2}{|1 - \rho e^{-i2\pi\nu}|^2} — the simplest model of colored noise, ubiquitous in finance and sensors.

Worked example — the spectrum of an AR(1) noise, in numbers Take \rho = 0.9 and \sigma_W^2 = 1. The power is R[0] = 1/(1 - 0.81) \approx 5.26, and the correlation between neighbours is R[1]/R[0] = \rho = 0.9: consecutive samples are strongly alike.

The density at zero frequency is S(0) = 1/|1 - 0.9|^2 = 100, and at the highest frequency \nu = \tfrac12 (where e^{-i\pi} = -1) it is S(\tfrac12) = 1/|1 + 0.9|^2 \approx 0.28: a ratio of about 360, that is 25.6 dB. The process is a low-pass (“red”) noise; with \rho = -0.9 the two values swap and the noise becomes high-pass.

A useful check: integrating S over one period of frequencies, \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} S(\nu)\,d\nu, gives back R[0] \approx 5.26, which is Wiener-Khinchin at \tau = 0.

Definition 7.4 — The periodogram “Natural” estimator of S_X from N samples: \hat S_N(\nu) = \frac{1}{N}\Big|\sum_{n<N} X_n\, e^{-i2\pi\nu n}\Big|^2 \qquad (\text{on the DFT grid: } |X_k|^2/N).

Theorem 7.5 — Bias and variance of the periodogram

  • Bias (leakage): \mathbb{E}[\hat S_N] = S * W_N where W_N is the Dirichlet kernel (prop. 0.2): the expectation “smears” over ±1 bin — a strong line masks a weak line (hence the windows, prop. 5.4). The bias tends to 0 as N \to \infty (W_N \to \delta).
  • Variance: for a Gaussian process, \mathrm{Var}[\hat S_N(\nu)] \approx S_X(\nu)^2 for every N — the relative error is 100% and does not decrease: the periodogram alone is a poor estimator, whatever the observation duration.

Proof. Sketch. Bias: \hat S_N = \widehat{z_N}\,\overline{\widehat{z_N}} with z_N = X\cdot w_N (truncation = rectangular window); the product in time becomes a convolution of the spectrum with \hat w_N = W_N (prop. 3.2), then one takes the expectation.

Variance: on the DFT grid, the \hat S_N(\nu_k) are asymptotically decorrelated and, for a Gaussian, \hat S_N(\nu) approximately follows S(\nu)\cdot \chi^2_2/2 (squared modulus of two independent Gaussians). Now \mathrm{Var}[\chi^2_2/2] = 1, hence a variance of order S^2, independent of N.∎

Bartlett and Welch methods — reducing the variance by averaging

  • Bartlett: split the N points into L disjoint segments of M = N/L points and average the periodograms: \mathrm{Var} \approx S^2/L… at the price of an increased bias (windows M times shorter ⇒ leakage multiplied) — the central bias/variance trade-off of spectral estimation.
  • Welch: overlapping segments (50%) and Hann windows: the same variance gain (Hann segments overlap “for free”), leakage controlled at −31 dB; this is the default method of instrumentation.

n  = 20000; fs = 500.0

Linear prediction and the Yule–Walker equations Let X be a centred WSS process with autocorrelation R. The best linear predictor of X_n from its p previous values, \hat X_n = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k X_{n-k}, minimizes \mathbb{E}|X_n - \hat X_n|^2.

Setting the derivatives with respect to the a_j to zero shows that the error must be orthogonal to each past value, \mathbb{E}[(X_n - \hat X_n)X_{n-j}] = 0, which gives the Yule–Walker equations \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,R[j - k] = R[j], \qquad j = 1, \dots, p, a Toeplitz linear system, with prediction-error variance \sigma^2 = R[0] - \sum_k a_k R[k].

If X is an AR(p) process, X_n = \sum_{k=1}^p a_k X_{n-k} + W_n with white noise W, its coefficients solve these equations: the AR model is the optimal predictor, and the innovation W_n is the part of X_n that no linear function of the past can predict.

Replacing R by the empirical autocorrelation fits an AR model to data, and the fitted model gives a parametric spectral estimate \hat S(\nu) = \sigma^2/|1 - \sum_k a_k e^{-i2\pi\nu k}|^2, smooth by construction, an alternative to the averaged periodograms above.

Worked example — fitting an AR(2) by Yule–Walker Suppose the empirical autocorrelations of a recording are \hat R[0] = 2.0, \hat R[1] = 1.2 and \hat R[2] = 0.5. The equations read 2.0\,a_1 + 1.2\,a_2 = 1.2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 1.2\,a_1 + 2.0\,a_2 = 0.5. The determinant is 4 - 1.44 = 2.56, so a_1 = (2.4 - 0.6)/2.56 \approx 0.703 \qquad\text{and}\qquad a_2 = (1.0 - 1.44)/2.56 \approx -0.172. The predictor is \hat X_n = 0.703\,X_{n-1} - 0.172\,X_{n-2}, and the error variance is 2.0 - 0.703 \times 1.2 + 0.172 \times 0.5 \approx 1.24: prediction removes 38% of the power.

The poles of 1/(1 - 0.703z^{-1} + 0.172z^{-2}) are complex with modulus \sqrt{0.172} \approx 0.41 < 1, so the fitted model is stable. For an AR(1) with \rho = 0.9, the single equation gives back a_1 = R[1]/R[0] = 0.9. Linear prediction of this kind is the basis of classical speech coding (LPC).

Complement to the official syllabus: Wiener filtering The detailed syllabus of the course unit explicitly mentions “filtering and prediction of stationary random signals” and “Wiener filtering”: here is the object. We observe Y = X + W (signal X WSS, noise W WSS, with spectra S_X, S_W, assumed uncorrelated) and we seek the LTI filter h that minimizes the mean squared error \mathbb{E}|X - h * Y|^2. The (non-causal) solution is the Wiener filter:

Key result — the Wiener filter H(\nu) = \frac{S_X(\nu)}{S_X(\nu) + S_W(\nu)}.

Reading: at frequencies where the signal dominates (S_X \gg S_W) the filter transmits (H \approx 1); where the noise dominates, it cancels out — the spectrum decides, optimally in the L^2 sense.

This is exactly the “filtered noise” of Theorem 7.3 (S_Y = |H|^2 S_X) inverted: instead of computing the effect of a filter on a spectrum, we design the filter that best separates signal and noise, from their estimated spectral densities (Definition 7.4 and Theorem 7.5 — estimated in practice by Bartlett/Welch).

The causal filter is built by spectral factorization (via the transfer function H(z) of ch. 5); the linear prediction of an AR process (the X_n = \rho X_{n-1} + W_n of Theorem 7.3) is its “zero noise” case: predicting is filtering toward the future. The Kalman filter is its recursive state-space variant, seen in the downstream modules of the program.

8. The signal for modern ML

Speech recognizers, audio generators and many sensor models do not consume raw samples but a time-frequency representation built with the tools of chapters 5 and 6. This chapter assembles the standard Mel pipeline, explains how generated signals are compared through distances between distributions of spectra, and points out where Fourier analysis hides inside modern architectures.

Mel spectrogram and MFCC: the standard pipeline

  1. Frames: 25 ms cuts every 10 ms, Hann window (a Gabor frame, ch. 6); STFT.
  2. Mel scale (perceptive, linear < 1 kHz then logarithmic): m = 2595\,\log_{10}\Big(1 + \frac{f}{700}\Big), \qquad f = 700\,\big(10^{m/2595} - 1\big).
  3. Mel filter bank: 20-80 triangular filters with edges uniformly spaced in Mel — band-pass filters of increasing width in Hz: E_m = \sum_\nu W_m(\nu)\,|\mathrm{STFT}(\nu)|^2 (a sparse weighting of the spectrogram).
  4. Log (amplitude perception); DCT along the Mel axis (a DCT = a “real FT”): the c_j are the MFCC; one keeps 13-20 coefficients + derivatives \Delta, \Delta\Delta, and normalizes in mean/variance per speaker.

Historically features for lightweight models (GMM-HMM), the MFCC remain the compact entry point; deep networks rather consume the raw Mel spectrogram.

Networks on spectrograms The Mel spectrogram is the image that 2D CNNs and audio transformers consume: the first layers of audio CNNs learn oriented frequency detectors — Gabor filters (ch. 6), see the Deep Learning module. The emblematic data augmentation (masking of frequency bands and time frames) operates directly in the time-frequency plane: the spectrogram is treated as a texture to be repaired. Speech technology, transcription, audio-multimodal transformers: this whole pipeline is the signal preprocessing of modern ML.

Distances between spectral distributions: FID and OT spectral loss Comparing two sets of signals = comparing two distributions of spectrograms (or of features). Standard: fit a Gaussian to the features of each set and measure the Fréchet distance d^2\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\Sigma_1), \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\Sigma_2)\big) = \|\mu_1 - \mu_2\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\Big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2\big(\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big). This is nothing but W_2^2 between Gaussians (the Bures metric): the FID of image generators and the “audio Fréchet distance” of audio generators are Wasserstein distances in disguise.

The OT spectral loss of audio generative models generalizes the idea: comparing real/generated batches by Gaussian W_2 (closed form above, differentiable) or by Sinkhorn on the spectra. The complete proof of the Bures formula and the Sinkhorn algorithm are on the syllabus of the Computational Optimal Transport module (M1P2, ch. 2 and 3, same instructor) — this chapter is its preparation.

In 1D (spectra or one-dimensional signals), everything reduces to the quantile coupling (proved there): W_2^2 = \int_0^1\big(F_1^{-1}(u) - F_2^{-1}(u)\big)^2du.

Example 8.1 — 1D domain adaptation on signals Two sensors measure the same phenomenon with different drifts (offset, gain, frequency response): the distributions of the frames differ, not the semantics. Alignment by 1D OT: transport the quantiles of sensor A onto those of sensor B (monotone transport, computable by sorting); the same principle at d-dimensional scale (feature clouds) is the optimal transport domain adaptation detailed in the OT module (ch. 4), whose instructor is a co-author. The signal provides the spectrum; OT provides the geometry.

Fourier in the architectures: convolutions and positions

  • Convolution of CNNs = the discrete convolution of ch. 1: the learned kernel is a filter whose frequency response (DTFT, ch. 5) explains its behavior — CNNs learn banks of Gabor filters; convolution via FFT in \mathcal{O}(n\log n) when the kernels are large (th. 5.3).
  • Convolution on graphs: the GNN message passing h \leftarrow \sigma(\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2} h W) is a convolution in the spectral sense — the Kipf & Welling filter is a degree-1 polynomial of the Laplacian (box below, with \tilde A = A + I; spectral filters in Graph ML ch. 4, the GCN layer in Derivation 5.2 of ch. 5, Period 2).
  • Positional encodings: the sine/cosine of transformers (\mathrm{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin(p/10000^{2i/d})…) = Fourier bases; positional encodings on graphs (LapEig) reuse the eigenvectors of the Laplacian — Fourier again (box below).

Background — Fourier on a graph

A signal can also live on the nodes of a graph instead of a time axis: one speed per road sensor, with a weight a_{ij} \geq 0 on each link. With the adjacency matrix A and the degree matrix D (the row sums of A on the diagonal), the graph Laplacian is L = D - A, and x^\top L\,x = \sum_{\text{edges } \{i,j\}} a_{ij}\,(x_i - x_j)^2 \;\geq\; 0 .

So L is symmetric positive semidefinite. Its orthonormal eigenvectors u_k, with eigenvalues 0 = \lambda_1 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_n, play the role of the complex exponentials: since u_k^\top L u_k = \lambda_k, a small \lambda_k is a signal that varies slowly across the edges, a low graph frequency.

On a path of three nodes the eigenvalues are 0, 1, 3, with eigenvectors proportional to (1, 1, 1), (1, 0, -1) and (1, -2, 1).

The graph Fourier transform is \hat x = U^\top x with U = [u_1, \dots, u_n], and a graph filter multiplies each coefficient by a gain, as the frequency response does in ch. 1: y = g(L)\,x = U\,\mathrm{diag}\big(g(\lambda_1), \dots, g(\lambda_n)\big)\,U^\top x .

The low-pass gain g(\lambda) = 1/(1 + \tau\lambda) minimizes \|y - x\|^2 + \tau\, y^\top L y: it is the Wiener filter of ch. 7 for unit white noise and a signal whose graph spectrum is 1/(\tau\lambda). A polynomial gain \sum_{k \leq K} \theta_k \lambda^k needs no eigenvectors and mixes each node with its K-hop neighbours only; the Kipf & Welling layer is g(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda for the normalized Laplacian of the graph with self-loops.

Complement to the official syllabus: source separation and the learning dictionary The “Signal representation and learning dictionary” block of the detailed syllabus of the course unit names source separation and the learning dictionary: here is the principle. The idea — officially that of the course — is to move from fixed bases (Fourier, wavelets, ch. 2 and 6) to a learned dictionary: a signal is represented as x \approx D\alpha with D = [d_1, \dots, d_K] a dictionary of waveforms and \alpha sparse (few active coefficients).

Learning D (for instance by non-negative matrix factorization — NMF — or K-SVD-type sparse least squares) on spectrograms of mixtures separates the sources: each component d_k\alpha_k concentrates on one source (a speaker, an instrument), since a mixture is a sum of sources and sparsity forces the atoms to specialize.

This is the first historical bridge between signal processing and unsupervised learning: a learned dictionary is a latent representation, \alpha a code — the same grammar as the autoencoders of the Deep Learning module, with the added spectral interpretability of the atoms. The “deep learning” signal reconstruction announced by the sheet is the generalization of this scheme in which the decoder becomes a network trained on degraded/clean pairs (denoising, super-resolution).

Example 8.2 — Dictionary learning and NMF source separation, in numbers Sparse dictionary learning solves \min_{D,\,A}\ \tfrac12\|X - DA\|_F^2 + \lambda\|A\|_1 \quad\text{subject to } \|d_k\|_2 \leq 1, where the columns of X are signal frames, those of D the atoms and those of A the codes.

It alternates two convex steps: sparse coding of A with D fixed (a lasso, solved by iterative soft thresholding), then a least-squares update of D with A fixed.

For audio, non-negative matrix factorization approximates the magnitude spectrogram V \approx WH with W, H \geq 0: the columns of W are spectral templates and the rows of H their activations in time.

Take two notes with templates w_1 = (2, 1, 0) (energy in the low bins) and w_2 = (0, 1, 2) (high bins). A frame v = (4, 3, 2) is explained exactly by the activations h = (2, 1), since 2w_1 + w_2 = (4, 3, 2).

To separate, each time-frequency bin is shared in proportion to the contribution of each source, a Wiener-like soft mask. In the middle bin, source 1 contributes 2 \times 1 = 2 and source 2 contributes 1 \times 1 = 1, so it receives the fraction 2/3. Multiplying the complex STFT of the mixture by each mask and inverting the STFT (chapter 6) yields the two separated signals.

Unrolling a fixed number of soft-thresholding iterations into the layers of a network trained end to end (LISTA, Gregor & LeCun, 2010) is one of the routes from dictionaries to signal reconstruction by deep learning.

# -- the mass connects the spectra of the same contour; compare with simple correlation

One “transports” the distribution of the spectra of one half toward the other: this is exactly the basic building block of the spectral losses of generative audio ML (ch. 8), and the first lab of the M1P2 module.

A recent bridge: projections, sliced OT and reconstruction Part of the instructor’s recent work deals with sliced transport (random 1D projections): Tanguy, Flamary & Delon, “Reconstructing discrete measures from projections” (C. R. Mathématique, 2024 — arXiv:2304.12029) show how the projections of a discrete measure determine it, with consequences for the empirical sliced Wasserstein distance. The vocabulary is that of the course: projecting a cloud (a measure of spectrograms) onto directions means computing marginal spectra; 1D OT by quantiles (ch. 8) does the rest. Flamary’s other works (FGW, domain adaptation, COOT) are detailed in the Computational Optimal Transport module (M1P2, ch. 7).

Exercises

  1. ★ Square wave series (with solution). Compute the c_k of the odd square wave f = \mathrm{sign}(\sin), check the decay in 1/k and illustrate the Gibbs phenomenon (partial sums at 5, 21, 101 harmonics).

    Solution. f odd and real ⇒ c_k purely imaginary. For k \neq 0 even, the integral of \sin(k\omega_0 t) over the two half-periods cancels. For k odd: c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}f e^{-ik\omega_0t}dt = \frac{-2i}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0/2}\sin(k\omega_0 t)dt = \frac{-2i}{T_0}\cdot\frac{2\,(1-(-1)^k)}{2k\omega_0} = \frac{-2i}{\pi k}, that is |c_k| = 2/(\pi|k|).

    This is consistent with lemma 2.3: the period carries two jumps (of +2 at 0, of -2 at T_0/2), whose contributions \frac{2}{2\pi i k} and \frac{-2\,(-1)^k}{2\pi i k} add up to \frac{2(1-(-1)^k)}{2\pi i k} = \frac{-2i}{\pi k} \ \text{ for } k \text{ odd}, \qquad 0 \ \text{ for } k \text{ even}. Recombining: f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{\sin((2m+1)\omega_0t)}{2m+1}.

  2. ★ Numerical Parseval. Check Parseval’s identity on a sampled periodic signal (compare \sum|x_n|^2/N and \sum|X_k|^2/N^2), then explain why the DFT preserves the energy up to a factor N (a Vandermonde matrix, unitary up to a factor).

  3. ★ Aliasing. Generate a chirp f(t) = 100 + 400t Hz sampled at f_s = 500 Hz; compute the spectrogram and interpret the “ghost” trace that goes back down; what apparent frequency does f = 350 Hz correspond to? (prop. 4.2: |350 - 500| = 150 Hz, and the trace rises up to 250 Hz before going back down.)

  4. ★ Homemade FFT. Implement the butterfly recursion of th. 5.3 (even/odd recursive function), validate it against np.fft.fft, and plot the execution time as a function of q = \log_2 N for N = 2^q, q = 4,\dots,18; check the \log·N slope.

  5. ★ Convolution via FFT. Implement the linear convolution via zero-padding + FFT (prop. 5.2), measure the times against np.convolve for n = m = 2^{14}, and explain the crossing of the complexities.

  6. ★ Hann filter. Low-pass FIR design by windowing (example 5.6): ideal response H(\nu) = \mathbf{1}_{|\nu| \leq \nu_c}, multiplication by a Hann window, inverse transform. Plot the frequency response for M = 31 and comment on the transition/aliasing trade-off; compare with Blackman at the same M.

  7. ★ Mel bank (with solution). Implement a bank of 26 triangular Mel filters between 0 and f_s/2 (formulas of ch. 8), apply it to a spectrogram, then the DCT; check that a vowel and a fricative have very different MFCCs.

    Solution (key). Uniform edges in Mel m_i = i\,2595\log_{10}(1+f_{max}/700)/26, converted to Hz by the inverse formula, triangles of normalized area (the highest ones are wider in Hz).

    Energies E_m = \sum_\nu W_m(\nu)|S(\nu)|^2, log, DCT-II: the vowels (formants, low-Mel energy) concentrate on the first MFCCs, the fricatives (high frequencies) on the following ones.

  8. ★★ Quantitative Gibbs (with solution). Show that for the square wave of exercise 1, the maximum of the partial sum S_N converges to \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin u}{u}du \approx 1.179, i.e. an overshoot of 0.179 above the level 1, about 9% of the jump (which has height 2).

    Solution (sketch). Near the jump at 0, S_N f(t) \approx \frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Si}(N\omega_0 t) \quad\text{with}\quad \mathrm{Si}(x) = \int_0^{x}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du (substitution in the Dirichlet kernel D_N, prop. 0.2) — oscillations concentrated on a width O(1/N).

    The maximum of the sinc integral is reached at the first zero of the numerator, u = \pi, and equals \mathrm{Si}(\pi) \approx 1.852 versus \mathrm{Si}(\infty) = \pi/2. The relative overshoot is \frac{\mathrm{Si}(\pi) - \pi/2}{\pi/2} \approx 17.9\,\% of the half-jump, i.e. \approx 8.95\,\% of the full jump — independent of N; only the width \sim 1/N shrinks.

  9. ★★ Gaussian (with solution). Re-prove prop. 3.3 by completing the square: show that \int e^{-\pi(t + i\nu)^2}dt = \int e^{-\pi t^2}dt = 1 (move the contour, or differentiate \int e^{-\pi(t+i\nu)^2}dt with respect to \nu and conclude), and deduce the two half-widths (standard deviations) \sigma_t, \sigma_\nu and their product.

    Solution (key). -\pi t^2 - 2\pi i\nu t = -\pi(t+i\nu)^2 - \pi\nu^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat g = e^{-\pi\nu^2}\cdot\int e^{-\pi u^2}du with u = t + i\nu on the horizontal segment — the integral does not depend on \nu (holomorphy + decay at the ends).

    For g = e^{-\pi t^2}, the normalized energy density |g|^2/\|g\|_2^2 \propto e^{-2\pi t^2} is a Gaussian of variance 1/(4\pi): \sigma_t^2 = \sigma_\nu^2 = 1/(4\pi), \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \sigma_t\sigma_\nu = 1/(4\pi): the Gaussian saturates Heisenberg-Gabor (th. 3.4).

  10. ★★ Heisenberg (with solution). Check that equality \sigma_t\sigma_\nu = 1/(4\pi) requires x' = \lambda t x in the proof of th. 3.4, solve this differential equation and conclude that only the Gaussians (translated, modulated, dilated) saturate the inequality.

    Solution (key). Solve x'(t) = \lambda t\,x(t): x(t) = C e^{\lambda t^2/2}, which is in L^2 if and only if \lambda < 0. Writing \lambda = -2\pi/\sigma^2 gives x(t) = Ce^{-\pi t^2/\sigma^2}.

    One then adds a translation in time (center t_0) and a modulation (center \nu_0), which do not change the standard deviations.

  11. ★★ Line detection (with solution). A signal contains x(t) = \sin(2\pi\cdot50t) + A\sin(2\pi\cdot80t) + white noise, with A = 0.01 (a line at −40 dB). Show that the raw periodogram does not detect the line at 80 Hz (th. 7.5), then make it emerge with Welch (Hann, segments of 1024).

    Solution (key). Periodogram: leakage of the rectangular window at −13 dB (prop. 5.4) + 100% variance ⇒ the line at −40 dB is drowned.

    Welch: Hann window (first lobe −31 dB, decay −18 dB/octave) ⇒ the leakage of the 50 Hz line falls below −60 dB; L \approx 2N/1024 segments ⇒ the standard deviation of the estimator divided by \sqrt{L}: the line at 80 Hz exceeds the detection threshold. Code: scipy.signal.welch as in ch. 7.

  12. ★★★ Project — denoising and spectral loss. Denoise a recording (white noise added): spectrogram → time-frequency masking (attenuate the cells below an energy threshold) → reconstruction by ISTFT; SNR before/after, musical artifacts. Then compare the distributions of original/denoised spectrograms by Gaussian W_2 (formula of ch. 8, POT: ot.gaussian.wass2) — direct preparation of the M1P2 module.

Q1 (4 pts) — Stability of a recursive filter. A digital filter is defined by y_n = -0.5\,y_{n-1} + x_n . Give its transfer function, its pole and its impulse response. Prove that it is BIBO stable. Compute the gain at \nu = 0 and at \nu = \tfrac12, and say whether the filter is low-pass or high-pass.

Proof. Marking points

  • H(z) = 1/(1 + 0.5\,z^{-1}), single pole at z = -0.5 (1 pt).
  • Geometric expansion for |z| > 0.5: h_n = (-0.5)^n for n \geq 0, and 0 before (1 pt).
  • \sum_n |h_n| = \sum_n 0.5^n = 2 < \infty, hence BIBO stable by Theorem 1.3, as Theorem 5.5 predicts for a pole inside the unit disk (1 pt).
  • |H(0)| = 1/1.5 \approx 0.67 and |H(\tfrac12)| = 1/|1 - 0.5| = 2: the filter amplifies the fast alternations, it is high-pass (1 pt). Common error: calling it low-pass because |a| < 1; stability depends on |a|, the type of filter on the sign of a.

Q2 (4 pts) — A 4-point DFT and a circular convolution. Take x = (1, 1, 0, 0) and h = (1, -1, 0, 0). Compute the DFT of x and check Parseval’s identity. Compute the circular convolution y = x \circledast h directly, then check that its DFT is the product of the two DFTs.

Proof. Marking points

  • With \omega = e^{-i\pi/2} = -i: X = (2,\ 1 - i,\ 0,\ 1 + i) (1.5 pts).
  • Parseval: \sum_n |x_n|^2 = 2 and \tfrac14(4 + 2 + 0 + 2) = 2 (0.5 pt).
  • y_n = \sum_m x_m h_{(n-m) \bmod 4} gives y = (1, 0, -1, 0) (1 pt).
  • H = (0,\ 1 + i,\ 2,\ 1 - i), so X_k H_k = (0, 2, 0, 2), which is the DFT of y computed directly (Proposition 5.2) (1 pt). Common error: taking \omega = +i, which conjugates the spectrum, or forgetting the factor 1/N of the inverse DFT, which gives (4, 0, -4, 0).

Q3 (4 pts) — Diagnosing a silent recording. A sensor delivers x(t) = \sin(2\pi \cdot 1000\, t) + \sin(2\pi \cdot 7000\, t), sampled at f_s = 8 kHz without an anti-aliasing filter. Every recorded sample is zero. Explain why, say whether any processing after sampling can recover the 1 kHz component, and propose two fixes.

Proof. Marking points

  • Proposition 4.2: 7 kHz is seen at |7 - 8| = 1 kHz (1 pt).
  • At t = n/f_s, \sin(2\pi \cdot 7n/8) = \sin(2\pi n - 2\pi n/8) = -\sin(2\pi n/8), which cancels the 1 kHz term exactly (1 pt).
  • Nothing after sampling can help: the samples are those of the zero signal, and two different signals with the same samples cannot be told apart (end of the proof of Theorem 4.1) (1 pt).
  • Fixes: an analog low-pass filter below 4 kHz before the converter (Example 4.3), which keeps the 1 kHz component; or f_s > 14 kHz to keep both (1 pt). Common error: proposing a digital low-pass filter after sampling.

Q4 (4 pts) — Wiener filtering of a red noise. The signal X is the AR(1) process of chapter 7 with \rho = 0.9 and unit innovation variance. One observes Y = X + V, where the noise is white with variance 1 and uncorrelated with the signal. Compute the Wiener gain at the frequencies 0 and \tfrac12, and interpret.

Proof. Marking points

  • S_X(\nu) = 1/|1 - 0.9\,e^{-i2\pi\nu}|^2, so S_X(0) = 100 and S_X(\tfrac12) = 1/1.9^2 \approx 0.277; S_V = 1 (1 pt).
  • Wiener filter H = S_X/(S_X + S_V) (1 pt).
  • H(0) = 100/101 \approx 0.99 and H(\tfrac12) = 0.277/1.277 \approx 0.22, that is about -13 dB (1 pt).
  • Interpretation: the filter is low-pass because the signal dominates the noise at low frequencies only; it keeps the slow variations and removes most of the fast ones, which are mostly noise (1 pt). Common error: writing |H|^2 or S_X/S_V.

Q5 (4 pts) — Reading a spectral estimate. A recording of N = 20\,000 samples at f_s = 500 Hz is analysed by a raw periodogram and by Welch’s method with Hann segments of M = 1024 samples and 50 % overlap. Give the bin width of each, the number L of Welch segments and the relative standard deviation of each estimate. A colleague sees one periodogram bin 6 dB above its neighbours and calls it a line: comment.

Proof. Marking points

  • Bin widths: f_s/N = 0.025 Hz for the periodogram, f_s/M \approx 0.49 Hz for Welch (1 pt).
  • L = \lfloor (N - M)/(M/2) \rfloor + 1 = 38 segments (1 pt).
  • Relative standard deviation: about 100 % for the periodogram whatever N (Theorem 7.5), about 1/\sqrt{38} \approx 16 % for Welch (overlap makes the gain slightly smaller) (1 pt).
  • Under the \chi^2_2/2 law of the proof of Theorem 7.5, a noise bin exceeds 4 times its mean (6 dB) with probability e^{-4} \approx 1.8 %, so about 180 of the 10,000 bins do so by chance: not significant before a Welch estimate confirms it (1 pt). Common error: reading the periodogram as if its variance decreased with N.
