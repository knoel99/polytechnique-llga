0. Background: the message passing foundation

This chapter restates the M1 message passing GNN and the facts about it that the module assumes; its last item, over-smoothing, is the first of the limits that the following chapters study.

Review of the Graph ML module (M1 P2) — what is assumed known

  • Generic layer (M1, ch. 5): h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \phi\big(h_v, \mathrm{AGG}_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}\{\psi(h_v, h_u)\}\big) ; GCN (renormalized averaging, spectral derivation), GraphSAGE (sampling, inductive), GAT (edge attention);
  • Spectral (M1, ch. 4): Laplacian L = D - A, variational characterization of \lambda_2, spectral clustering as a RatioCut relaxation, convolution by Chebyshev polynomials — filtering = a polynomial of L;
  • Embeddings (M1, ch. 3): DeepWalk/node2vec and their NetMF reading (factorization of random-walk matrices); the PathNNs of ch. 9 are their generalization;
  • Link prediction / GAE (M1, ch. 6) and scale (M1, ch. 7: NeighborSampler, Cluster-GCN, GraphSAINT) — the ch. 1 below extends the latter;
  • Over-smoothing (M1, ch. 5): after many layers, all embeddings converge (proved on a regular graph: geometric convergence to the mean) — why one stops at 2-4 layers… and why this module exists.

The module’s four research questions (1) How far can a GNN see? — expressive power (ch. 2); (2) How far can information travel? — over-squashing and depth (ch. 3); (3) Where am I in the graph? — position and structure (ch. 4); (4) At what scale and under what attacks? — systems (ch. 1) and robustness (ch. 7). 1. Scale: sampling, systems

Real graphs have millions of nodes, and a careless experiment can make a method look better than it is. This chapter covers the systems side of training at scale and the experimental protocol (seeds, confidence intervals, leakage checks) that every later chapter relies on.

What is assumed known (M1, ch. 7)

NeighborSampler (fanouts, expansive neighborhood 1 + s_L(1 + s_{L-1}(\dots))), Cluster-GCN (METIS partitioning, partition bias), GraphSAINT (subgraphs + bias-free coverage normalization), and the bias/variance/memory triangle. Here: the systems layer and experimental hygiene.

  • Multi-GPU and partitioning: beyond one GPU, partition the graph (METIS/vertex-cut) across several machines; boundary edges are duplicated (shadow nodes) — the distributed training of the Emerging ML module applies almost as is (parameter server vs all-reduce); PinSage (M1, ch. 6) is the industrial reference;
  • Caching and pipelining: neighborhood sampling is a system problem (I/O, reuse of subgraphs across batches — hence PinSage’s importance sampling that freezes the entry points); the frameworks: DGL, PyG + torch_geometric.sampler, Quiver;
  • Summary of complexities: Method Memory/batch Bias Notes —————– ————————————- ———– ————————– full-batch \mathcal{O}(|V| + |E|) none breaks beyond ~1M nodes NeighborSampler \mathcal{O}(\prod s_l) variance inductive, standard Cluster-GCN \mathcal{O}(|E|/k) partition deep possible GraphSAINT \mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{G}_s|) corrected dense batches

2. Expressive power: the Weisfeiler-Lehman test

Which graphs can a message passing GNN tell apart, and which ones will it always confuse? This chapter answers exactly, with the Weisfeiler-Lehman test. A concrete scene: take two non-isomorphic 6-regular graphs. Your 3-layer GCN produces the same embeddings for both (up to permutation), and a link-prediction AUC around 0.5. This is not an implementation bug but the 1-WL bound, which this chapter makes operational before any architectural gadget is added.

What breaks Three classic mistakes: believing that a deeper GNN “sees more” (often false); evaluating only on graphs that 1-WL already separates (which gives a false sense of power); and ignoring the initial features (they fix the initial colors). All three mislead the reading of papers as much as a small molecular experiment.

Definition 2.1 — 1-WL (color refinement) Initialize a color c_v^{(0)} from the features; iterate: c_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{HASH}\Big(c_v^{(k)},\ \mathrm{SORT}\big\{c_u^{(k)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\big\}\Big). Two graphs are 1-WL-equivalent if the multisets of colors coincide at convergence.

1-WL distinguishes almost all random graphs (Babai-Erdős-Selkow); but fails on simple families: regular graphs of the same degree, cycles, pairs of molecules discussed since the 1960s (CIR: chemical identity recognition).

Theorem 2.2 — GNN ≤ 1-WL (Xu et al., 2019; Morris et al., 2019) Any message passing GNN whose aggregation and update are functions (continuous, computable) cannot distinguish two 1-WL-equivalent graphs: if the initial colors coincide and the layers saturate the test, the embeddings coincide at every depth.

Proof. By induction on \ell.

  1. Initialization: initial colors = features, identical by assumption, so h_v^{(0)} = h_{v'}^{(0)} for every pair (v, v') of the same color.
  2. Heredity: if all nodes of color c have the same representation h_c, then the input of the aggregation at v is the multiset \{h_c : c \in \mathrm{multicol}_v\}. Now 1-WL-equivalence = equality of color multisets and of neighbor color multisets at each iteration — so AGG receives identical inputs, and h_v^{(\ell+1)} = h_{v'}^{(\ell+1)} .
  3. Readout: in the end, the readouts (permutation-invariant by construction, M1 ch. 2) see only the multiset of the h_v — identical on both graphs. ∎

Theorem 2.3 — GIN reaches exactly 1-WL (Xu et al., 2019) With sum + MLP and discrete features, h_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\Big((1 + \epsilon^{(k)})\, h_v^{(k)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(k)}\Big) the GNN distinguishes exactly what 1-WL distinguishes.

Proof. The ingredient: on a countable domain, there exists an injective function f : \text{finite multisets} \to \mathbb{R}. Encoding each element by an integer (canonical log of a prime: c \mapsto \log p_c) gives \sum_i \log p_{c_i}, and the uniqueness of prime factorization ensures injectivity; a universal MLP approximates it.

Then the sum+MLP layer is injective in (h_v, \mathrm{multiset}(h_u)) — the pair (own color, neighbor multiset) is encoded without collision. By induction, two nodes have the same embedding \iff same 1-WL color. The injective sum readout concludes: GIN = 1-WL. ∎

Immediate consequences: mean and max are not injective — \{a, a, a\} and \{a\} have the same mean; \{a, a\} and \{a\} the same max. So GCN/GraphSAGE (mean) are strictly less powerful than GIN on categorical features.

In practice the difference shows up mainly on tasks where counting neighbors matters (molecules: \mathrm{CH}_4 vs \mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}_3).

Example 2.4 — C6 vs 2×C3: 1-WL fails, 2-WL separates (demonstration)

  • 1-WL: all nodes have degree 2; by induction, at each round each node sees (its color, {its color, its color}). The colors remain uniform on each graph, and identical between the two graphs: indistinguishable.
  • 2-WL (pair coloring): consider an adjacent pair (u, v). In the triangle, \{w : w \sim u, w \sim v\} counts 1 common vertex; in C6, this count is 0 (no triangles). The 2-WL update rule, which aggregates for the pair (u,v) the colors of the pairs (u, w) and (w, v) for all w, encodes the number of triangles — the pair histograms differ: separated. ∎

Moral: 2-WL (hence a 2-GNN) “counts the moments” that the local sum cannot see. To code in the lab (exercise 1).

k-WL and k-GNN hierarchy k-WL colors k-tuples of nodes: k = 2: pairs (above); the hierarchy is strict: (k+1)-WL distinguishes pairs that k-WL confuses (for k \geq 1, 2-WL already being strictly stronger than 1-WL); at the top, k = n+1 → exact isomorphism test (exponential).

The k-GNNs (Morris et al., 2019) implement (a local variant of) k-WL: messages between neighboring k-tuples — memory and compute cost \mathcal{O}(n^k): k = 2 practical, k = 3 reserved for small graphs (molecules). Morris et al.’s survey (2023) draws the complete map (Weisfeiler-Lehman “go machine learning”).

Pragmatic alternatives: subgraphs and motif counting

  • ESGNN (Equivariant Subgraph Aggregation, Bevilacqua et al., 2022): each node is described by the collection of subgraphs (ego-nets of increasing radius, or “node-deletion” — the graph deprived of v); a shared 1-WL GNN encodes them, aggregation across subgraphs adds expressive power without full \mathcal{O}(n^k) cost — strictly between 1-WL and 3-WL;
  • Motif counting (Bouritsas et al., 2020): precompute counts of isomorphic subgraphs (triangles, paths, cycles) and inject them as features — “moments matter”: raising expressive power by preprocessing rather than by architecture; direct link with Example 2.4 (triangles) and with the MMDs of generative evaluation (M1, ch. 9.6);
  • Practical limits: more expressive power = more estimation variance and cost; on real benchmarks (Dwivedi et al., 2023), GIN vs GCN is decided by tenths — expressive power is the bottleneck only on structural tasks (counting, distinguishing) — cf. ch. 1.

3. Over-squashing and deep GNNs

Even when a GNN could in principle tell two nodes apart, the information may never reach them: messages from far away must squeeze through a few edges. This chapter defines this over-squashing precisely, links it to the geometry and spectrum of the graph, and presents the two families of remedies — changing the graph or changing the propagation.

Definition 3.1 — Over-squashing (Alon & Yahav, 2021) — formal definition via Jacobians Let \mathcal{G} be the computation graph of a GNN; the influence of a node u on the final representation of a node v is measured by the Jacobian \big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\|.

There is over-squashing when this quantity is crushed exponentially with the distance d(u, v) — precisely, when the bound \big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\| \leq c\, (f \, r(\lambda_{\max}))^{-d(u,v)} becomes small because the radius of convergence r(\lambda_{\max}) of the propagation (an increasing function of the largest eigenvalue of the propagation operator, e.g. 2\tilde L_{\mathrm{sym}} / \lambda_{\max}) is close to 1.

In words: an exponentially large quantity of information (\sim growth rate \mu of the neighborhood) must pass through a channel that contracts exponentially fast. Bottlenecks (cut edges, bridges between communities) are the physical points of the phenomenon.

Proposition 3.2 — The expansion / \lambda_{\max} link (proof sketch) The edges crossed by the information from u to v form a cut; the “flow” of message passing across a cut is bounded by the conductance of the cut, itself controlled by the spectrum.

By Cheeger (inverted for the top of the spectrum, Tanner’s bound for \lambda_{\max}), a weak conductance forces a large \lambda_{\max}… that is, highly oscillating components which, in the Chebyshev recurrence (M1, ch. 4), make the factor \big( \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{2} \big)^k diverge and crush the radius of convergence: topological bottleneck ⇔ poor spectral conditioning.

Hence the two families of remedies: change the topology (rewiring, below) or change the operator (normalization, implicit propagation, ch. 3.4).

3.3 — Discrete Ricci curvature and SDRF (Topping et al., 2022) The Ollivier-Ricci curvature of an edge (u,v) compares neighborhoods by optimal transport: \kappa(u, v) = 1 - \frac{W_1(\mu_u, \mu_v)}{d(u, v)}, \qquad \mu_u = \text{uniform law on } \mathcal{N}(u), W_1 = Wasserstein-1 distance (Optimal Transport module): “locally dense” edges (triangles) → large \kappa; bridges → negative \kappa.

Worked example (by hand):

  • edge of a triangle: \mu_u = \{\frac12 v, \frac12 w\}, \mu_v = \{\frac12 u, \frac12 w\}; transport plan: keep w (cost 0), transport \frac12 from v to u (cost 1) → W_1 = \frac12, \qquad \kappa = +\frac12 ;
  • edge of a hexagon C6: the best plan transports v \to t, s \to u (cost 1 each) → W_1 = 1, \qquad \kappa = 0 ;
  • bridge edge between two triangles (barbell): the best plan pays W_1 = \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 + 3) = \frac53 , so \kappa = -\frac23 < 0 — the bridge is “hyperbolic”: it is the bottleneck.

SDRF (Spectral-De-Ricci-Flattening): at each iteration,

  1. find the edge (u,v) of minimal curvature;
  2. add the edge (x, y) between two neighbors of the edge (not yet present) that maximizes the sum of curvatures — triangulate the bottleneck;
  3. remove the edge of maximal curvature if it exceeds a threshold (avoid densification).

Effect: the curvature distribution flattens, \lambda_{\max} drops, the bound of Definition 3.1 improves. Looking at the graph as a geometric object — curvatures, distances, volumes — is one of the field’s great turns (cf. ch. 8 and the Geometric DL module).

DIGL — diffusion rewiring (Gasteiger et al., 2021) A “filtering” alternative: replace A by \tilde A = \mathrm{top}\text{-}k\big(\sum_{i=0}^{K} \beta_i T_i(L)\big) — a sparsified heat kernel (sum of Chebyshev polynomials, M1 ch. 4). Each node gains “virtual” edges toward its multihop neighbors weighted by the diffusion; simple, differentiable, and the final GNN trains on the rewired graph.

Comparison: SDRF is topological (local curvature, targeted additions); DIGL is spectral (global diffusion); both target r(\lambda_{\max}) — and both may destroy motif information: measure the trade-off in the lab (exercise 6).

3.4 — Deep GNNs: APPNP and GCNII APPNP (Klicpera et al., 2019) separates predicting from propagating: a network (often simple) predicts H = f_\theta(X); propagation follows personalized PageRank: Z^{(k+1)} = (1 - \alpha)\, \hat P\, Z^{(k)} + \alpha H, \qquad \hat P = \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}. Fixed point: Z^* = \alpha \big(I - (1 - \alpha)\hat P\big)^{-1} H . The series expansion \sum_{k} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k \hat P^k shows that the effective depth is infinite without stacking parameters. The teleport \alpha H bounds the gains (like PageRank damping, M1 ch. 0) and kills over-smoothing (M1, ch. 5.3); K iterations = truncation of the series.

GCNII (Chen et al., 2020) makes GCN truly deep through two mechanisms: h^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big( \big[(1-\gamma_\ell) I + \gamma_\ell \hat P\big]\big[(1-\beta_\ell) I + \beta_\ell W_\ell\big] h^{(\ell)} + \gamma_\ell \hat P h^{(0)} \Big) — initial residual toward h^{(0)} (the same remedy as APPNP’s teleport) and mapped identity (1 - \beta_\ell) I (as in ResNet). With \beta_\ell increasing with depth, 64 layers become trainable.

Positioning: rewiring (3.3) changes the channel; APPNP/GCNII change the dynamics; graph transformers (ch. 5) remove the bottleneck by making the channel all-all; over-smoothing (representation) and over-squashing (channel) remain two distinct limits that must be diagnosed separately.

4. Positional and structural encodings

A GNN only sees the neighborhood of a node, not where the node sits in the whole graph. This chapter shows how to add this information as positional or structural encodings, and how to do it without breaking the invariances a GNN must respect.

Problem: GNNs “do not know where they are” Message passing is equivariant (M1, ch. 2): two nodes with isomorphic neighborhoods have the same embedding — correct… but insufficient: the global position (distance to the center, membership in a cycle, bridge role) is informative. Transformers had the same problem (token order) and solved it with positional encodings — we re-import the idea (and its pathologies).

4.1 — LapEig: the ambiguities, and SignNet to lift them Taking p_v = [u_2(v), \dots, u_k(v)] (coordinates of v on the eigenvectors) suffers from three pathologies:

  1. sign: -u_i is also an eigenvector — two runs give opposite encodings;
  2. basis: if \lambda_i is multiple, any rotation of the basis is admissible;
  3. instability: a small perturbation of the graph crosses the eigenvalues and makes the basis jump.

SignNet (Lim et al., 2022) builds functions invariant to sign and basis: \rho(V) = h\Big( \sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi(v_i) + \phi(-v_i) \Big), with \phi, h MLPs. Enforcing the parity \phi(v) + \phi(-v) makes \rho even (sign invariance), and the sum over the basis gives invariance under rotation.

The completeness theorem (in the spirit of results on orbit invariants): SignNet universally represents the continuous functions invariant in the eigenvectors — the exact analogue of Deep Sets’ “sort before sum” (ch. 2). In practice: LapEig + SignNet is the default PE of spectral graph transformers.

Example 4.2 — RWPE computed by hand (and why it matters) The random-walk positional encoding (RWSE, Dwivedi et al., 2020) of a node v is the vector of return probabilities \big[ (P)_{vv}, (P^2)_{vv}, \dots, (P^K)_{vv} \big], \qquad P = D^{-1} A. Computation:

  • node of a triangle: the back-and-forths give (P^2)_{vv} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac12 , and going around the triangle, in both directions, gives (P^3)_{vv} = 2 \times (\frac12)^3 = \frac14 ;
  • node of a hexagon C6: (P^2)_{vv} = \frac12 — identical (same back-and-forths); but (P^3)_{vv} = 0: a return of odd length requires an odd cycle, and C6 has none. The first “full tour” appears only at k = 6, with (P^6)_{vv} = 2 \times (1/2)^6 = \frac{1}{32}.

Now C3 and C6 are exactly the pair that 1-WL cannot distinguish (Example 2.4). The RWSE — a single scalar, p_3, suffices here (\frac14 versus 0) — separates them immediately.

Structural PEs “buy” expressive power at cost \mathcal{O}(|E| K); this is why the winning models of positional benchmarks (ch. 5) systematically carry a PE.

4.3 — SE(3) invariants for molecules, and the theory of positions In 3D (ch. 8), the encoding must respect rotations/translations/reflections: invariants = interatomic distances \|x_i - x_j\|, angles, dihedrals; it is the continuous version of the principle “use only what is invariant”.

The theoretical question: how many coordinates to inject without breaking isomorphism invariance? A perfect positional encoding should distinguish the nodes of a regular graph (where 1-WL is constant) while remaining invariant under automorphism — canonical encodings (canonical numbering) make the learning variance explode; the practical compromise: relative encodings (RWSE, anchor distances, the SPD bias of ch. 5) — never absolute coordinates.

The inductive vs expressive debate Positional encodings partially “break” equivariance — a delicate choice between positional information and invariance guarantees. In industry (evolving graphs), one favors inductive PEs (generalizable to new nodes/graphs — RWSE, anchoring); in chemistry, exact invariants dominate. Cross-reference: Graphormer’s SPD bias (ch. 5) is a relative PE — the only category that survives both constraints.

5. Graph transformers

Attention lets every node exchange information with every other node in one step, which removes bottlenecks but forgets the graph. This chapter shows how graph transformers put the structure back into attention, what they cost, and when a plain message passing GNN is enough.

Idea: all-all attention + structural bias Global attention \mathcal{O}(n^2) removes bottlenecks and over-squashing at once (ch. 3); but without an encoding of the graph, a transformer on features alone is blind to structure: the topology must be reinjected via (i) positional encodings (ch. 4) and (ii) additive attention biases in the scores — structure steers attention rather than masking it.

5.1 — Graphormer (Ying et al., 2021) — the three structural encoders

  1. centrality encoding: an embedding of the degree (in/out if directed) added to the features — degree is the first “where am I”;
  2. spatial encoding: a bias learned per shortest-path distance: \mathrm{Attention}(Q, K)_{ij} = \frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d}} + b_{\phi(d_{ij})}, \qquad d_{ij} = \mathrm{SPD}(i, j), \quad b_{\phi(\cdot)} \text{ learned (table)}, with a dedicated value for d_{ij} = \infty (disconnected nodes); the bias is additive before the softmax — structure conditions attention;
  3. edge encoding: on the shortest path i \rightsquigarrow j, the mean of the embeddings of the crossed edges is added to the score — for molecules, the type of the bonds along the path matters.

Plus a global virtual node (connected to all) for the readout. Result: 1st rank on OGB-LSC PCQM4M (3.7M molecules) at its release. Implementation:

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch_geometric.utils import shortest_path_distance   # PyG >= 2.6 (or homemade BFS)

class GraphormerLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, h, max_spd=5):
        super().__init__()
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, h, batch_first=True)
        self.bias = nn.Embedding(max_spd + 2, h)     # b_phi table: SPD -> h heads
        self.ffn = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, 2*d), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(2*d, d))
    def forward(self, x, spd):                       # spd: (n, n) integer distances
        b = self.bias(spd.clamp(max=5).long()).permute(2, 0, 1)  # (h, n, n)
        x = self.attn(x, x, x, attn_bias=b)[0] + x
        return self.ffn(x) + x

PyG trick: the per-head bias is built with an embedding table indexed by the distance matrix (exercise 7).

5.2 — GPS (Rampasek et al., 2022): the two channels, layer by layer H^{(\ell+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}\Big( \mathrm{BN}\big(\underbrace{\mathrm{MSA}(H^{(\ell)} + \mathrm{PE})}_{\text{global channel}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{MPNN}(H^{(\ell)}, A)}_{\text{local channel}}\big) \Big) — global attention (all-all; kills over-squashing) + local message passing (locality bias, convolution); PE injected at the input (ch. 4); graph batches by concatenation-padding.

Per-layer complexity: \mathcal{O}(n^2 d) (attention) + \mathcal{O}(|E| d) (MPNN) — the global channel dominates. GPS is a recipe: any MPNN (GCN, GINE, GAT) and any PE (RWSE, LapEig+SignNet) plug into it — hence its status as the reference framework.

5.3 — Efficient transformers: Exphormer and sampled attention For large n (ogbn-products…), \mathcal{O}(n^2) no longer fits; Exphormer (Shirzad et al., 2023) replaces full attention with a sparse attention graph:

  1. the edges of the real graph;
  2. the neighbors of a random d-regular expander graph — an expander has logarithmic diameter: information crosses the graph in \mathcal{O}(\log n) layers, precisely what ch. 3 demands (expansion!);
  3. global virtual nodes.

Complexity linear in n + |E|; combined with the local MPNN, this gives “Exphormer-GPS”, the scalable state of the art.

Generalization: sampled attention (neighborhood, kernels, hashing) — the same recipes as the long-context transformers of the M2 LLMs module, transposed: the graph transformer and the LLM live the same algorithmic life.

5.4 — When is an MPNN enough? (decision guide)

  • homophilous graph, local dependencies (Cora, transactional fraud): GCN/GAT 2-3 layers — no point paying n^2;
  • motifs, distances, long-range dependencies (molecules, QA over KGs): transformer or ESGNN (ch. 2) — structure exceeds the neighborhood;
  • large n: MPNN + sampling (ch. 1) or Exphormer;
  • empirical benchmark rule (Dwivedi et al., 2023): at equal size, the gain of the transformer over MPNN+PE is real but modest — the hybrid (GPS) wins most often; the PE is the dominant factor, the architecture second.

LLGA connection: “attention = complete dynamic graph” versus “GNN = attention on a given graph” (M1, ch. 5); Graphormer is the synthesis — a given and learned attention — the transformers ↔︎ GNN convergence is THE guiding thread of the master.

6. Dynamic and temporal graphs

Many graphs change over time — transactions, edits, interactions — and the order of events carries the signal. This chapter presents the two ways of representing such graphs and the two reference architectures, TGAT and TGN.

Two formalisms: snapshots vs event-based

  • snapshots: G_1, G_2, \dots, G_T (graph aggregated per window); models: GCN+RNN (encode each snapshot, make recurrent); simple, but the granularity loses the order within the window;
  • event-based: the stream of timestamped interactions (u, v, t_1), (u, w, t_2), \dots — the “true” formulation of the problem; it is the regime of fraud (millisecond) and sequential recommendation.

Recap — TGAT and TGN (Graph ML Models 5.7) Both models were introduced in Graph ML ch. 5 (Models 5.7, Example 5.8); this box only restates them.

TGAT (Xu et al., 2020) is a causal graph attention: a node attends over its strictly past neighbours, each message concatenated with a learned time encoding of the elapsed time, \phi(\Delta t) = \big[\cos(\omega_1 \Delta t), \sin(\omega_1 \Delta t), \dots, \cos(\omega_d \Delta t), \sin(\omega_d \Delta t)\big].

TGN (Rossi et al., 2020) adds a memory s_i(t) per node. Each event (i, j, t) produces a message from the two memories, the elapsed time and the edge features, and a recurrent cell updates the memory: s_i(t) = \mathrm{GRU}\big(s_i(t^-),\ \mathrm{msg}(s_i(t^-), s_j(t^-), \Delta t, e_{ij})\big). The embedding of a node is then computed by TGAT-style attention over its recent neighbours and their memories.

6.2 — Training a memory model without leaking the future Two facts of the TGN paper that any reproduction must respect.

  1. Batch order. Events are processed in chronological batches. For each batch, the model first updates the memories with the messages stored from the previous batch, then computes the embeddings and the loss of the current events, and only then stores the current messages. The memory modules thus receive gradients, while no event sees its own interaction in the memory. The price: events of one batch cannot see each other, so larger batches mean staler memories.
  2. Staleness. A node that has not interacted for a long time keeps an old memory; the attention embedding over its neighbours, which may have interacted recently, is what limits this staleness.

The standard benchmarks are the Wikipedia and Reddit interaction streams (Exercise 8), with future link prediction against sampled negatives, as in Graph ML ch. 6 but in time order; the ablation of the paper shows that both the memory and the attention embedding matter.

6.3 — LLGA application: the Elliptic fraud case (worked) Data. The Elliptic dataset (Weber et al., 2019) is a graph of 203,769 Bitcoin transactions (nodes) and 234,355 directed payment flows (edges), each node carrying 166 features, over 49 time steps about two weeks apart. Only 4,545 transactions are labelled illicit and 42,019 licit; the other 77 % are unknown. Each time step is a single connected component: no edge links two time steps.

Protocol. Train on steps 1–34 and test on steps 35–49, the temporal split of the paper; a random split would put the same laundering activity on both sides, the leakage of Graph ML Example 5.8. Among the labelled nodes, the licit share is \frac{42{,}019}{42{,}019 + 4{,}545} \approx 0.902, so a model that always answers “licit” reaches 90 % accuracy: report the F1 score of the illicit class, time step by time step.

What the paper found. A random forest on the node features beat the GCN, and all methods failed after step 43, when a large dark market closed and the illicit patterns changed; even a random forest retrained after every test step did not recover.

Lessons for this chapter.

  1. The time steps are disconnected, so Elliptic is a snapshot dataset: a TGN memory has nothing to carry from one step to the next unless links across steps are added, for instance through the wallet addresses of Elliptic++ (Elmougy & Liu, 2023).
  2. A temporal split exposes distribution shift that no architecture removes: monitor drift, retrain, and report the step-43 drop as the expected failure case.
  3. Errors cost differently: a false alert burdens a legitimate user and an analyst, a missed transaction lets laundering through. Choose the alert threshold for an investigation budget, not for accuracy.

Data: PyG EllipticBitcoinDataset or the Kaggle release (research use). The Year-1 project Y1-2 and the capstone C2 build on this case.

7. Robustness and privacy on graphs

Graph models are exposed to attackers who can add or remove a few edges, and graph data often reveals private information. This chapter studies attacks at test and training time, defenses and certification, then privacy and fairness on graphs.

7.1 — Nettack (Zügner et al., 2018): gradient attack, in detail The scenario: evasion (the attacker modifies the graph at test time) on a target u and its class y_u; budget: \Delta modifications (edge additions/removals, feature changes). The attacker’s dilemma: the model is not differentiable in the discrete structure. Nettack’s solution:

  1. train a linearized surrogate (one-layer GCN, softmax); its output Z_u(A) is then polynomial in A — continuous, differentiable;
  2. first-order score: for each candidate modification \delta (flip of an edge (a,b) or of a feature), \mathrm{score}(\delta) = -\nabla_{A}\, \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(Z_u(A)\big)\big|_{(a,b)} \cdot \big(\delta_{ab} - A_{ab}\big), the Taylor approximation of the loss change (product of the gradient with the direction of the change); keep the modification with maximal score;
  3. iterate (modify, re-score) until the budget; “discretion” constraints: add only plausible edges (degrees, common features).

Historic result: a few flips suffice to flip the prediction — and the attacks transfer to the true GCN (the surrogate suffices). The LLGA lesson: in fraud detection, the attacker knows the model (or its shadow); robustness is a design requirement, not an option.

7.2 — Poisoning: Meta-Self (Zügner & Günnemann, 2019) An attack at training time (poisoning): the graph is corrupted before learning. Bilevel: \min_{\delta}\ \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(f_{\theta^*}(A + \delta)\big) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{train}\big(f_\theta(A + \delta)\big), solved by meta-gradient: a few inner gradient steps are unrolled (differentiable), the outer gradient traces back to \delta — the same machinery as MAML (model-agnostic meta-learning, Finn et al., 2017), which learns an initialization from which a few gradient steps solve a new task, by differentiating through those steps. A “self-labeled” variant (Meta-Self): the attacker also controls a few labels.

Structural defenses:

  • pruning (attack edges are often “suspicious” — large feature distances);
  • purification (Pro-GNN: learn a clean graph A' regularized close to A while training the GNN);
  • robust GCN (RGCN, Zhu et al., 2019): Gaussian layers whose variance absorbs the attack + attention over reliable neighbors.

7.3 — Certification: sketch Guarantee that no attack of budget \Delta changes the prediction: by randomized smoothing applied to graphs (majority over graphs perturbed by random flips) or by spectral bounds (certificates tied to \lambda_{\max} of ch. 3 — same currency); the state of the art certifies small budgets on moderate GCNs: in practice, one combines partial certification + the defenses above + monitoring (detecting edge-addition anomalies).

Recap — differential privacy (Emerging ML ch. 5, Privacy & UQ ch. 1) Differential privacy is taught in full in Emerging ML ch. 5 (M1 P1 elective) and Privacy and Uncertainty ch. 1 (announced M2 P1 module); here is what 7.4 uses.

A randomized mechanism \mathcal{M} is (\varepsilon, \delta)-differentially private if, for all neighbouring datasets D \sim D' and every set of outputs S, \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(D) \in S) \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(D') \in S) + \delta . On graphs, “neighbouring” means one edge apart (edge-DP) or one node and its edges apart (node-DP).

The Laplace mechanism releases f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta_1 / \varepsilon), where \Delta_1 is the largest change of f in \ell_1 norm between neighbours: it is (\varepsilon, 0)-DP. The Gaussian mechanism adds \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) noise with \sigma \ge \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}\,\Delta_2 / \varepsilon \qquad (0 < \varepsilon < 1), where \Delta_2 is the sensitivity in \ell_2 norm.

Two rules make DP usable: composition (mechanisms with budgets \varepsilon_1 and \varepsilon_2 cost at most \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 together) and post-processing (any function of a DP output stays DP).

7.4 — Privacy: node-DP, edge-DP, reidentification

  • edge-DP: the basic mechanism is randomized response — flip each edge with probability p, calibrated for (\varepsilon, 0)-DP; counting queries (how many edges between two groups?) receive Laplace noise of sensitivity 1;
  • node-DP: much harder — sensitivity explodes with the degree (removing a node removes d edges); recipe: project the graph onto a graph of bounded degree k (keep the k “most important” neighbors), then apply the edge mechanisms; the price: bias on hubs;
  • reidentification: graph embeddings (M1, ch. 3) are fingerprints — re-routing an “anonymized” node into a public graph by comparing neighborhoods (attacks by degrees, by subgraphs — the Narayanan-Shmatikov attack transposed); GraphRAG (M1, ch. 10) aggregates scattered facts: the reidentification risk grows with the quality of the graph;
  • evaluation: measure the utility-privacy trade-off (AUC vs \varepsilon); cross-reference with the M2 security module for LLMs (prompt injection over the KG, leakage by generation).

7.5 — Structural fairness Degree bias: low-degree nodes (minorities in social networks, new customers) are predicted less well — fewer messages = less signal; remedies: structural features (RWSE, ch. 4), virtual edges, degree-based resampling, reweighted losses; open question: structural fairness (communities) vs individual fairness (nodes) can conflict — the exact counterpart of the fairness tensions of the Optimal Transport module (transporting node distributions; homophily bias: a homophilous GNN propagates label biases).

8. LLGA bridges: 3D, algorithmic reasoning, GNN × LLM

This chapter connects the module to the other axes of the master: geometric graphs (3D molecules), GNNs that learn to execute algorithms, and the combination of GNNs with LLMs.

8.1 — 3D molecules: EGNN and E(n) equivariance (Satorras, Hoogeboom & Welling, 2021) The positions x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3 make the molecular graph geometric; physics is invariant under rotations/translations (E(3)); a GNN that respects this symmetry by construction learns better with less data (Bronstein et al., the Geometric DL “charter”). EGNN: m_{ij} = \phi_e\big(h_i, h_j, \|x_i - x_j\|^2, e_{ij}\big), x_i^{(\ell+1)} = x_i^{(\ell)} + C \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} (x_i^{(\ell)} - x_j^{(\ell)})\, \phi_x(m_{ij}), h_i^{(\ell+1)} = \phi_h\big(h_i^{(\ell)}, \textstyle\sum_{j} m_{ij}\big). Equivariance: proved in Graph ML, Proposition 8.3 — the distances, hence the messages, are invariant, and R factors out of the position update; the factor C bounds the step. The invariant and equivariant families used for molecules (SchNet, DimeNet, MACE) and the forces as -\nabla of an invariant energy are compared in Geometric Data ch. 7, a mandatory module of the same period.

Applications: 3D property prediction, learned molecular dynamics, 3D generation (EDM — equivariant diffusion; the M2 Geometric DL module generalizes to groups; M1 ch. 9 covers the 2D version).

8.2 — Neural algorithmic reasoning (Veličković et al.) Learning to execute algorithms with GNNs: encode inputs as a graph (sorting: a list = a comparison graph; shortest paths: the graph itself), execute via message passing layers, decode outputs — the black-box replaces the code.

The CLRS benchmark (Veličković et al., ICML 2023) tests faithful execution of classical algorithms (CLRS = Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein). Results: GNN processors generalize to unseen sizes better than transformers on certain families (structural induction).

The LLGA lesson: a learned algorithm is differentiable (it can be inserted into an end-to-end trained pipeline: routing, scheduling, matching) and approximately robust (noisy inputs); at the price of exactness — the trade-off to master.

Loop with Refresher CS: what more can a “learned algorithm” do than an algorithm? Answer: train on data, slot into gradient descent — and the pseudo-code prompting of ch. 9.4 is the dual path (the LLM simulates the execution; NAR embodies it).

8.3 — GNN × LLM: injected encoders and benchmarks Three injection architectures (M1, ch. 10.2, deepened):

  • structural tokens: the outputs of a GNN (node/graph embeddings) are projected into the LLM’s embedding space as soft tokens; the LLM reads structure as a language;
  • structural recall: the GNN (or the graph traversal) retrieves the relevant facts, the LLM writes — RAG becomes “graph-RAG”; in both cases, the question of the alignment of the spaces arises (contrastive: bring the GNN embedding closer to the text describing the same node);
  • LLM annotator: the LLM enriches node features (summaries, types) for the GNN — the graph becomes text-attributed (the “TAG” regime); benchmarks: NLGraph (serialized reasoning — M1, ch. 10.2), Talk like a Graph (encodings: none dominates everywhere), and the industrial GraphRAG suites.

Industrial GraphRAG (summary; details in M1, ch. 10): LLM extraction → entity graph → Leiden communities + summaries → local/global queries; the M2’s role: the quality of the graph (robust communities — ch. 7; KGC completion — M1 ch. 6) is the system’s bottleneck; the rare specialty the master builds: both GNN theory and the LLM pipeline.

9. Research at DaSciM (Vazirgiannis, Lutzeyer)

The DaSciM laboratory (LIX, École Polytechnique — M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer) is the master’s host laboratory: this chapter studies its papers in depth, positioning them relative to the program. The reference presentation of these papers is Graph ML ch. 11 (M1 P2); this chapter keeps its own angle, the machinery: proofs of expressive power, complexity and positioning against chapters 2–5.

9.1 — Path Neural Networks, in depth (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Starting point: the canonical GNN layer aggregates 1-hop neighbors (M1, ch. 5); stacking L layers amounts — when all goes well — to aggregating walks of length L. But

  1. walks repeat nodes (backtracking — redundant information);
  2. composing layers degrades (over-smoothing/squashing, ch. 3);
  3. 1-WL — hence the GNN (th. 2.2) — aggregates only multisets of colors, without the sequence of the route.

The path operator: for each node v, enumerate simple paths (without repetition) starting from v, of length \ell; encode each path with a GRU, aggregate: m_p = \mathrm{GRU}\big(h_{u_\ell}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{GRU}(\dots \mathrm{GRU}(h_{u_1}^{(0)}, h_v^{(0)}) \dots )\big), \qquad h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\big\{ m_p : p \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} \big\}. Three variants bound the enumeration cost:

  • PathNN-s (shortest paths): \mathcal{P}_v = shortest paths to all destinations (at most n - 1 paths, \mathcal{O}(n) GRUs per node); captures distances — two nodes “close to everyone” versus “peripheral” separate;
  • PathNN-r (random walks): \mathcal{P}_v = sampled walks — the bridge with DeepWalk/NetMF (M1, ch. 3);
  • PathNN-n (neighborhood sampling): locally sampled simple paths — the practical compromise for large graphs.

Complexity: \mathcal{O}(P \cdot \ell \cdot d^2) per node (P sampled paths) — of the same order as a GAT if P \sim \bar d.

Results: on graph classification (standard benchmarks including molecular ones — ZINC and TU datasets), PathNN matches or exceeds GIN/GCN; on tasks where the geometry of routes carries the signal (molecules: paths are chemical chains!), the gain is clear.

Theoretical positioning: simple paths carry information that walk multisets ignore (recall: C6 vs 2C3, Example 2.4 — triangles are read in paths of length 3); ordered GRU aggregation is a special case of GOAT’s “order + sequence” family (below); and PathNN is the subject of the “paper” exercise (exercise 11).

9.2 — GOAT (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) Seen in M1 (ch. 11) on the generation side; on the expressive power side: the invariant aggregator \mathrm{AGG} (th. 2.2–2.3) bounds the GNN to 1-WL precisely because it is invariant.

GOAT learns a local order by attention (scoring neighbors by (h_v, h_u), sorting), then processes the sequence with an RNN — a sequence model inside the graph. It is strictly more expressive than invariant aggregation (it distinguishes identical distributions of a multiset as soon as the order carries signal), while remaining globally equivariant (the order is a learned function of the representations, not an external numbering).

Positioning in M2: an architectural answer to the question of ch. 2 — gain expressive power without paying \mathcal{O}(n^k); and an answer to ch. 4 — the local order is a learned positional encoding.

9.3 — NGG (Evdaimon et al., 2024): conditioned latent generation The bridge M1-ch. 9 ↔︎ diffusion: a GNN encoder projects graphs into a compact latent space; a diffusion model (M1, ch. 9.5 — Multimodal GenAI) learns the distribution there conditioned on structural features (spectrum, degrees, counts); the decoder regenerates graphs exhibiting the requested features.

The interest for M2: latent diffusion bypasses discretization (continuous diffusion in \mathbb{R}^d rather than over DiGress’s categories), and the spectral conditioning connects generation to ch. 3 (the spectrum governs communication — here, it governs the shape of the generated graphs). LLGA applications: faithful synthetic data (pre-training!), distributional anonymization, anomaly detection (a real graph far from the latent support).

9.4 — Pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024) Presented in M1 (ch. 10) as a GraphRAG tool; the M2 reading: a benchmark of algorithmic reasoning — LLMs execute graphs described in pseudo-code better than in natural language (systematic gains); to be connected with neural algorithmic reasoning (ch. 8): two ways of making algorithms run on graphs — by a prompted LLM, or by a trained GNN.

  10. ★★ Edge-DP. Prove that randomized response (flipping each edge with probability p) is (\varepsilon, 0)-DP with \varepsilon = \log \frac{1-p}{p} for p < \tfrac12 (analyze the two output mechanisms per edge); deduce the equivalent Laplace noise for an edge count.

    Solution: for two graphs neighboring in one edge, the likelihood ratio of any output is bounded by \frac{1-p}{p} (the only edge where the two graphs differ is reported as present with probability 1-p in one and p in the other; all other edges have the same law); hence the \varepsilon.

    Counting: randomized response is applied to every one of the N = n(n-1)/2 node pairs, edge or not. An edge is reported present with probability 1-p, a non-edge with probability p.

    The reported count \tilde m therefore has mean m(1-2p) + Np and variance N\,p(1-p), whatever m.

    The unbiased estimate \hat m = (\tilde m - Np)/(1-2p) has variance N\,p(1-p)/(1-2p)^2. This is comparable to a Laplace mechanism of scale \sqrt{N p(1-p)/2}\,/(1-2p) (a Laplace variable of scale b has variance 2b^2).

    The noise grows with the number of pairs, not of edges: on a sparse graph, randomized response is far noisier than releasing the true count plus Laplace noise of scale 1/\varepsilon (the count has sensitivity 1 under edge-DP).

Sample questions. Five questions of 4 points each (20 points) make a 2-hour paper on the in-class chapters.

Q1 (4 pts) — 1-WL by hand. G_1 is a triangle on the nodes 1, 2, 3 with a tail 3–4–5; G_2 is a square 1–2–3–4 with a pendant node 5 attached to node 1. Both have the degree sequence (3, 2, 2, 2, 1).

  1. Run 1-WL from uniform initial colours and say at which round the two graphs are separated. (b) Explain why 1-WL never separates the 6-cycle from two disjoint triangles. (c) Give a feature of ch. 4 that separates them, with its value on each graph.

Proof. Marking points

  • Round 1 recovers the degrees, and the two colour histograms coincide (1 pt).
  • Round 2: the degree-3 node of G_1 sees neighbour degrees \{2, 2, 2\}, that of G_2 sees \{2, 2, 1\}, so the histograms differ (1 pt).
  • Both graphs of (b) are 2-regular on 6 nodes: every node keeps the same colour and the same neighbour multiset at every round; by Theorem 2.2 no message-passing GNN separates them either (1 pt).
  • The random-walk encoding: the return probability after 3 steps is \frac14 on a triangle and 0 on the 6-cycle (Example 4.2) (1 pt). Common error: stopping at round 1 and concluding that G_1 and G_2 are equivalent.

Q2 (4 pts) — Aggregators. (a) Show that mean and max return the same output on the multisets \{a, a, b, b\} and \{a, b\}, and on \{a, a\} and \{a\}. (b) With the encoding a \mapsto \log 2 and b \mapsto \log 3, show that the sum separates both pairs, and explain why sums of logarithms of primes are injective on finite multisets. (c) Hydrogen atoms are nodes with identical features: which aggregator tells a carbon with four hydrogen neighbours from a carbon with two?

Proof. Marking points

    1. Both means equal (a + b)/2 and both maxima \max(a, b); on the second pair, mean and max both return a (1 pt).
    1. The sums are \log 36 against \log 6, and \log 4 against \log 2 (1 pt).
  • Injectivity: \sum_c m_c \log p_c = \log \prod_c p_c^{m_c}, and unique factorization recovers every multiplicity m_c (1 pt).
    1. Only the sum, which returns 4 or 2 times the hydrogen feature; mean and max return the hydrogen feature in both cases. This is why GIN uses a sum followed by an MLP to reach 1-WL (Theorem 2.3) (1 pt). Common error: taking the MLP after a mean to restore the counts, which no function of the mean can do.

Q3 (4 pts) — Ollivier–Ricci curvature. Use the uniform law on the neighbours of each node. (a) Compute the curvature of an edge of a triangle.

  1. Two triangles u, a, b and v, c, d are joined by the edge (u, v): compute the Wasserstein distance between the neighbour laws of u and v, then the curvature of (u, v). (c) SDRF adds the edge (a, c): compute the new curvature of (u, v) and say why it matters.

Proof. Marking points

    1. Keep the shared neighbour and move \frac12 over one edge: W_1 = \frac12, so \kappa = \frac12 (1 pt).
    1. The law of u puts 1/3 on a, b, v; the law of v puts 1/3 on c, d, u. Only one of a, b can be sent to u (cost 1); the other must reach c or d (cost 3), and v goes to the remaining one (cost 1). So W_1 = \frac{1 + 3 + 1}{3} = \frac53 and \kappa = -\frac23 (1.5 pts).
    1. Now a → c, b → u and v → d all cost 1, and no plan can do better since the two supports are disjoint: W_1 = 1 and \kappa = 0 (1 pt).
  • The negative curvature marked the bottleneck; the new edge opens a second route between the communities, which improves the sensitivity bound of Definition 3.1 (0.5 pt). Common error: keeping some mass on the node itself (the lazy walk) without saying so, which changes every value.

Q4 (4 pts) — Edge-level randomized response. Each of the N node pairs of a graph is released with its status flipped with probability p < \frac12, independently.

  1. Show that this is (\varepsilon, 0)-edge-DP with \varepsilon = \ln\frac{1-p}{p}; give \varepsilon for p = 0.1 and the value of p that gives \varepsilon = 1.

  2. The released graph has \tilde m edges: give an unbiased estimator of the true edge count m and its variance.

  3. For 1,000 nodes, 5,000 true edges and p = 0.1, how many false edges does the release contain, and what does this imply for training a GNN on it?

Proof. Marking points

    1. Neighbouring graphs differ in one pair, so the likelihood ratio of any release is that of this pair, at most (1 - p)/p (1 pt); \varepsilon = \ln 9 \approx 2.20, and \varepsilon = 1 needs p = 1/(1 + e) \approx 0.269 (0.5 pt).
    1. From \mathbb{E}[\tilde m] = m(1 - 2p) + Np, the estimator is \hat m = (\tilde m - Np)/(1 - 2p), with variance Np(1 - p)/(1 - 2p)^2 (1 pt).
    1. N = 499{,}500 pairs: about 49,450 false edges against 4,500 surviving true ones, so 92 % of the released edges are noise (0.5 pt).
  • The count stays well estimated (standard deviation about 265), but message passing would aggregate mostly noise: release DP statistics (Laplace on counts) or train with a DP optimizer instead (1 pt). Common error: forgetting the non-edges, which dominate in a sparse graph.

Q5 (4 pts) — Diagnosis and design. A 6-layer GCN classifies the nodes of two dense communities joined by a single bridge node. Its accuracy falls with the distance to the information a node needs on the other side, and more layers make it worse. (a) Over-smoothing or over-squashing? Name a measurement that tells them apart. (b) Propose two remedies from different families and the quantity each acts on. (c) Choose between a 2-layer GAT, GPS with random-walk encodings and Exphormer for Cora, Peptides-func and a 2-million-node product graph.

Proof. Marking points

    1. Over-squashing if the sensitivity of Definition 3.1 collapses with the distance across the bridge while the representations stay distinct; over-smoothing if all representations converge to one vector as depth grows, bridge or not (1.5 pts).
    1. Rewiring (SDRF on the most negative edges, or DIGL) changes the channel, measured by curvature or \lambda_{\max}; a global channel (GPS, a virtual node) bypasses it; APPNP or GCNII act on the propagation against over-smoothing (1 pt).
    1. Cora is homophilous and local: a 2-layer GAT is enough. Peptides-func has long-range dependencies: GPS with random-walk encodings. Two million nodes rule out quadratic attention: Exphormer, or an MPNN with sampling (1.5 pts, 0.5 each). Common error: adding depth to reach long-range information.

Projects

All projects use public data, run on a laptop CPU or one free Colab T4 GPU, and rely only on this module, its prerequisite Graph ML and, where stated, the mandatory M2 P1 course Large Language Models. Levels ★ to ★★★ are those of the exercises, and the effort is per student: at most 20 h, the cap for a 24 h M2 course (the volume the session plan assumes; the official one is not communicated).
