0. Refresher: graph theory

Every later chapter manipulates the same few objects — the adjacency matrix, the degrees, the Laplacian, random walks — and uses their properties without stopping to prove them. This refresher states and proves those properties once, so that the walks of ch. 3 and the spectral arguments of ch. 4 rest on solid ground.

L3 prerequisites — the “graph” object (cf. Refresher CS)

  • Definitions: G = (V, E); directed/undirected, weighted; degree d_v, neighbors \mathcal{N}(v), adjacency matrix A, degree matrix D = \mathrm{diag}(d_v). Paths, connectivity, components, cycles, DAGs.
  • Euler and Königsberg (1736): crossing the 7 bridges is impossible because more than two vertices have odd degree — the first theorem of graph theory; the abstraction (nodes + edges) founded the discipline.
  • Linear algebra: (A^k)_{ij} counts the paths of length k; eigenvalues of A = spectrum of the graph; symmetric matrices diagonalizable in an orthonormal basis (algebra reminder).
  • Stochastic matrices: P = D^{-1}A = random walk; stationary distribution, PageRank with teleport probability \alpha, \pi \propto (I - (1-\alpha) P)^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

Definition 0.1 — Isomorphism, automorphism, subgraph Two graphs G = (V, E) and G' = (V', E') are isomorphic if there exists a bijection \pi : V \to V' such that (u, v) \in E \iff (\pi(u), \pi(v)) \in E'.

An automorphism is an isomorphism of G onto itself; their set forms a group \mathrm{Aut}(G). A subgraph of G is formed of S \subseteq V and a subset of E \cap (S \times S); the induced subgraph keeps all the internal edges.

Deciding isomorphism is a problem known neither NP-complete nor polynomial; the Weisfeiler-Lehman test (M2 module, ch. 2) is its fundamental heuristic — and the expressivity bound of GNNs.

Proposition 0.2 — Connectivity and shortest paths The relation “u \sim v if there exists a path from u to v” is an equivalence relation; its classes are the connected components. Between two vertices of the same component, every path of minimal length (shortest path) is a simple path (without repeated vertex).

Proof. Reflexivity (path of length 0), symmetry (undirected graph: reverse the path) and transitivity (concatenate) are immediate. If a path from u to v visits a vertex w twice, we delete the cycle between the two visits: the path strictly shortens. A path of minimal length therefore cannot contain a cycle, so every vertex appears there only once: it is simple, of length \leq n - 1. ∎

Theorem 0.3 (spectral theorem) and counting by the trace Every real symmetric matrix S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} writes S = U \Lambda U^\top with U orthogonal and \Lambda real diagonal.

For the adjacency of a simple graph, three counting identities follow: \mathrm{tr}(A) = 0, \qquad \mathrm{tr}(A^2) = 2|E|, \qquad \mathrm{tr}(A^3) = 6\,|\{\text{triangles}\}|.

Proof. \mathrm{tr}(A^2) = \sum_i (A^2)_{ii} = \sum_i d_i, each edge contributing to two degrees.

(A^3)_{ii} counts the closed paths of length 3 through i: these are exactly the triangles containing i, each traversed in both directions from i. Each triangle \{i, j, k\} thus produces 2 closed paths for each of its 3 vertices, i.e. 6 countings.

Conversely a closed path of length 3 i \to j \to k \to i visits three pairwise distinct, pairwise adjacent vertices. ∎

Immediate application in learning: counting triangles (motifs) by matrix products — the basic ingredient of the structural features of ch. 9 and of the expressivity of the M2 module (ch. 2).

Definition 0.4 — The Laplacian of a graph L = D - A, \qquad \text{or normalized: } L_{\mathrm{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2} A\, D^{-1/2}. For every vector x \in \mathbb{R}^n (n = |V|):

Key result — the Laplacian quadratic form x^\top L x = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (x_u - x_v)^2 \ \geq\ 0

— L is positive semi-definite; its eigenvalues 0 = \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_n encode connectivity.

\lambda_2 (the spectral gap, Fiedler 1973) measures the difficulty of cutting the graph, and the number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components.

Proof. x^\top D x = \sum_v d_v x_v^2 and x^\top A x = 2 \sum_{(u,v) \in E} x_u x_v (symmetric matrix); hence x^\top L x = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (x_u^2 + x_v^2 - 2 x_u x_v).

For the multiplicity of 0: reordering the vertices by components makes A (and L) block-diagonal; each block L_C has the indicator \mathbf{1}_C as kernel, since x^\top L_C x = 0 forces x constant on each connected component (the sum of squares cancels edge by edge, and connectivity propagates the constant — Prop. 0.2). ∎

Example 0.5 — The spectrum tells the story

  • Path of n vertices: \lambda_k = 2(1 - \cos\frac{k\pi}{n}), k = 0, \dots, n-1;
  • complete graph: 0 then n (multiplicity n-1);
  • cycle of n vertices: \lambda_k = 2(1 - \cos\frac{2k\pi}{n}), k = 0, \dots, n-1.

A “two-community” graph has a small \lambda_2; the Fiedler eigenvector separates the communities — this is the heart of spectral clustering, developed in ch. 4 (direct bridge with the PCA of the Machine Learning module).

Proposition 0.6 — Random walks: stationarity and ergodicity Let G be connected undirected, P = D^{-1}A, m = |E|. The distribution \pi_v = d_v / (2m) is stationary: \pi^\top P = \pi^\top.

If moreover G is not bipartite, the chain is irreducible and aperiodic, hence ergodic: P^t(x, \cdot) \xrightarrow[t \to \infty]{} \pi \qquad \text{whatever the starting point } x.

Proof. Detailed balance: \pi_u P_{uv} = \frac{d_u}{2m} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{1}[(u,v) \in E]}{d_u} = \frac{\mathbb{1}[(u,v) \in E]}{2m}, a quantity symmetric in u, v; summing over u, (\pi^\top P)_v = \pi_v.

Irreducibility: connectivity makes every vertex reachable.

Periodicity: on a bipartite graph, every closed walk has even length (P^t(x, x) = 0 for odd t), so the chain has period 2.

Conversely, a connected non-bipartite graph contains a cycle of odd length \ell. From any vertex x the walk can come back in 2 steps (along an edge and back) or in an odd number of steps (walk k steps to the cycle, go around it, walk back: 2k + \ell steps), and \gcd(2, 2k + \ell) = 1 — period 1.

The convergence theorem for Markov chains (cf. Refresher Statistics) concludes. ∎

Practical consequence: the walks of DeepWalk (ch. 3) “mix” the distribution toward \pi \propto degrees — hence the bias of embeddings toward hubs, partly corrected by node2vec and by NetMF.

Definition 0.7 — Modularity (Newman & Girvan, 2004) For a partition \mathcal{C} = \{C_1, \dots, C_k\} of the vertices: Q(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \Big[ A_{ij} - \frac{d_i\, d_j}{2m} \Big]\, \delta(c_i, c_j), where c_i is the community of i and \delta the indicator.

Q compares the observed density of intra-community edges to the expected one under the configuration model (edges redistributed while preserving degrees, term d_i d_j / 2m).

Maximization sketch: maximizing Q exactly is NP-hard; Louvain does it greedily. Each node i in turn moves to the neighboring community C that most increases the modularity — the gain of moving an isolated node i into C is \Delta Q = \frac{k_{i,C}}{m} - \frac{\Sigma_C\, d_i}{2m^2}, with k_{i,C} the number of edges from i to C and \Sigma_C the sum of the degrees in C. Then each community is contracted into a single node and the procedure restarts; hence hierarchies of communities used as-is by GraphRAG (ch. 10).

Known limit: resolution — Q cannot “see” communities smaller than ~\sqrt{2m} edges; SBM (ch. 9) offers a better-founded statistical alternative.

Exploring and describing a graph

The second lecture of the official programme covers graph exploration and traditional graph algorithms, and the course description opens with the fundamental descriptive statistics of graphs. Before any learning, these are the first things computed on a new graph: they reveal its scale, its hubs and its local density, and they remain strong hand-crafted features for classical models.

Algorithm 0.8 — Breadth-first search and unweighted shortest paths From a source s:

  1. set \mathrm{dist}(s) = 0, put s in a FIFO queue;
  2. repeatedly pop a vertex v and, for every neighbor u not yet discovered, set \mathrm{dist}(u) = \mathrm{dist}(v) + 1, \mathrm{parent}(u) = v, and push u.

Cost \mathcal{O}(|V| + |E|): each vertex is pushed once, each edge examined twice.

Claim: \mathrm{dist}(u) is the length of a shortest path from s to u, and the parent pointers form a shortest-path tree.

Proof. The queue always holds vertices whose \mathrm{dist} values are non-decreasing and differ by at most 1 (true at the start; popping v and pushing vertices with \mathrm{dist}(v) + 1 preserves it). Hence vertices are popped in non-decreasing order of \mathrm{dist}.

Suppose some vertex u gets \mathrm{dist}(u) > \delta(u), the true distance, and take such a u with smallest \delta(u).

Let w be its predecessor on a shortest path, so \delta(w) = \delta(u) - 1 and \mathrm{dist}(w) = \delta(w) by minimality.

When w was popped, u was either undiscovered — then it received \mathrm{dist}(w) + 1 = \delta(u), a contradiction.

Or it was already discovered from a vertex popped before w, whose \mathrm{dist} is \leq \mathrm{dist}(w), giving \mathrm{dist}(u) \leq \delta(u), again a contradiction.

Conversely \mathrm{dist}(u) \geq \delta(u) always, since the parent pointers trace an actual path of that length. ∎

Replacing the queue by a stack gives depth-first search, used for connected components, cycle detection and topological order (DAGs); with non-negative edge weights, Dijkstra’s algorithm replaces the queue by a priority queue on tentative distances, in \mathcal{O}((|V| + |E|)\log |V|). All three are recalled in the Refresher in Computer Science; here they are the primitives of the statistics below and of the neighborhood sampling of ch. 7.

Definition 0.9 — Descriptive statistics and centralities With n = |V|, m = |E|:

  • degrees: mean degree 2m/n, density 2m / (n(n-1)), and the degree distribution p_k — often heavy-tailed in real networks (p_k \propto k^{-\gamma} over a range of k), which produces hubs;
  • clustering: the local coefficient C_v = \frac{|\{\text{edges among the neighbors of } v\}|}{\binom{d_v}{2}} = \frac{(A^3)_{vv}}{d_v(d_v - 1)} (Theorem 0.3), and the global transitivity 3 \times \#\text{triangles} / \#\text{connected triples};
  • distances: eccentricity of v (largest BFS distance), diameter (largest eccentricity), average shortest-path length; a small world combines short paths with high clustering (Watts & Strogatz, 1998);
  • centralities:
    • degree;
    • closeness c(v) = (n-1) / \sum_{u} \mathrm{dist}(v, u);
    • betweenness b(v) = \sum_{s \neq v \neq t} \sigma_{st}(v)/\sigma_{st}, where \sigma_{st} counts the shortest s–t paths and \sigma_{st}(v) those through v (Brandes’ algorithm computes all of them in \mathcal{O}(nm));
    • eigenvector centrality Ax = \lambda_1 x;
    • PageRank, the stationary law of the walk of Proposition 0.6, where \alpha is the teleport probability: \pi^\top = (1 - \alpha)\, \pi^\top P + \alpha\,\mathbf{1}^\top / n;
  • assortativity: the correlation between the degrees at the two ends of an edge (social networks: positive; technological and biological networks: often negative).

These numbers are the classical features of node and graph classification, and the statistics compared by the MMD evaluation of generated graphs (9.6).

Example 0.10 — The statistics of a small graph, computed Take the “kite” with vertices a, b, c, d, e and edges ab, ac, bc, cd, de (a triangle abc with a tail c - d - e).

Here n = m = 5, density 5/10 = 0.5, degrees (2, 2, 3, 2, 1).

  • BFS from a: layers \{a\}, \{b, c\}, \{d\}, \{e\}, so the eccentricity of a is 3, which is also the diameter.

  • Clustering: C_a = C_b = 1; C_c = 1/3 (among its neighbors a, b, d only ab is an edge); C_d = 0; C_e = 0 by convention.

    One triangle and \sum_v \binom{d_v}{2} = 1 + 1 + 3 + 1 + 0 = 6 connected triples give a transitivity 3/6 = 0.5.

  • Closeness: the distances from c sum to 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 5, so c(c) = 4/5 = 0.8.

    From d: 6, so c(d) \approx 0.67; from a: 7, so c(a) \approx 0.57.

  • Betweenness (unordered pairs): every shortest path from \{a, b\} to \{d, e\} passes through c, so b(c) = 4.

    The paths from \{a, b, c\} to e pass through d, so b(d) = 3; b(a) = b(b) = b(e) = 0.

Vertex c has only one more edge than the others, but it controls all traffic between the two halves — what degree alone does not show.

1. Why graphs?

Concrete scene: fraud detection on a transaction graph (nodes = accounts, edges = transfers). A tabular classifier on account features hits 0.71 AUC; the same model enriched with a 2-hop neighborhood reaches 0.89 — until a random split leaks train↔︎test edges and inflates the score. This chapter states why structure matters, before writing a GNN.

What breaks Treating nodes as i.i.d. (signal lost); splitting without blocking edges (leakage); stacking GCN layers until every embedding looks the same (over-smoothing). All three sabotage a GraphRAG as much as a toy Karate Club.

Real-world data is relational: the structure carries information that i.i.d. methods ignore.

Social networks (friendships), molecules (bonds), transaction networks (fraud!), enterprise knowledge graphs (customers, products, processes), computer networks, citation clusters, legal ontologies… All are naturally graphs, often heterogeneous (typed nodes/edges: “buys”, “owns”, “works with”) and dynamic (timestamped edges: transactions second by second).

Also typically: few labels (a few dozen annotated nodes out of millions) — the semi-supervised regime of ch. 5 — and distributions biased by sampling (the observed edges are not a uniform sample of the interactions).

independent note LLMs excel at text but struggle with: structured relations, factual fidelity (hallucinations), and non-textual data. Graphs fill exactly these gaps:

  • GraphRAG: connecting the LLM to structured proprietary data — today the most effective approach for making an LLM reliable in the enterprise (ch. 10);
  • GNN + LLM: generative AI on structured data (generating molecules, plans, recommendations);
  • Industrial applications: pharma/biotech (molecules = graphs), finance (transaction networks, systemic risk), recommendation, supply chain, multi-agent systems (agent workflows are DAGs — RL module).

Common thread of the master’s program Three places of the graph in generative AI: inside the model (the attention of transformers is a complete dynamic graph — Deep Learning module), for the model (GraphRAG, structural encoding — ch. 10), between models (agent orchestration = DAG programming). This module lays the foundations of all three; the M2 module “Advanced GNN” extends each one.

2. Learning tasks on graphs

Before choosing a model, one must say what is predicted: a label per node, a score per pair of nodes, or a property of the whole graph. This chapter fixes that taxonomy, then the two constraints that every later model must respect: the symmetry under renumbering of the nodes, and the distinction between predicting on the training graph and on new graphs.

Definition 2.1 — Permutation invariance and equivariance A graph has neither a fixed size nor a canonical order of the nodes: the model must be permutation-invariant (graph output) or permutation-equivariant (per-node output): for every permutation matrix P, f(P X, P A P^\top) = f(X, A) \quad \text{(invariant)}, \qquad f(PX, PAP^\top) = P f(X, A) \quad \text{(equivariant)}.

CNNs (grid), RNNs (sequence) violate these constraints — hence dedicated architectures (ch. 5) and the whole expressivity theory of the M2 module (ch. 2).

Transductive vs inductive — a distinction that structures the whole module

  • Transductive: training and testing on the same graph (embeddings ch. 3); impossible to predict for a node arriving after training;
  • Inductive: the model (its weights) reapplies to new graphs (GraphSAGE, GNNs ch. 5) — indispensable in production;
  • Structural semi-supervised: on Cora (2,708 nodes), 140 labels suffice — the structure relays the information (homophily hypothesis: neighbors share the label; when it fails, one needs “virtual” edges or heterophilous models — ch. 5, “Heterophily”).

Similarity measures and graph kernels

The third lecture of the official programme is devoted to graph kernels and similarity measures — the way graphs were learned on before neural networks, and still the strongest baselines on many small benchmarks. Two questions are asked: how similar are two nodes of one graph (the basis of link prediction, ch. 6), and how similar are two whole graphs of different sizes, without any correspondence between their nodes (the basis of graph classification with kernel machines).

Definition 2.2 — Node similarity measures For nodes u, v with neighborhoods \mathcal{N}(u), \mathcal{N}(v):

  • common neighbors |\mathcal{N}(u) \cap \mathcal{N}(v)| = (A^2)_{uv};
  • Jaccard \frac{|\mathcal{N}(u) \cap \mathcal{N}(v)|}{|\mathcal{N}(u) \cup \mathcal{N}(v)|};
  • Adamic-Adar (a shared neighbor counts more if it is not a hub) \sum_{w \in \mathcal{N}(u) \cap \mathcal{N}(v)} \frac{1}{\log d_w};
  • Katz S = \sum_{k \geq 1} \beta^k A^k = (I - \beta A)^{-1} - I, which counts all paths with a weight decaying with length and converges for \beta < 1/\lambda_{\max}(A);
  • personalized PageRank from u, evaluated at v.

The first three are local (two hops), the last two global.

In the kite of Example 0.10, a and d share the neighbor c (\mathcal{N}(a) = \{b, c\}, \mathcal{N}(d) = \{c, e\}).

Common neighbors 1, Jaccard 1/3, Adamic-Adar 1/\log 3 \approx 0.91.

These heuristics are the baselines any learned link predictor (ch. 6) must beat (Liben-Nowell & Kleinberg, 2007).

Definition 2.3 — Graph kernel A graph kernel is a function k(G, G') = \langle \varphi(G), \varphi(G') \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} for some feature map \varphi into a Hilbert space — equivalently, a symmetric function whose Gram matrix on any finite set of graphs is positive semi-definite — and which is invariant under isomorphism.

Any kernel method of the Machine Learning module (SVM, kernel ridge regression, kernel PCA) then applies to a dataset of graphs of different sizes.

A complete kernel (\varphi injective on isomorphism classes) would decide graph isomorphism, so computing one is at least as hard as that problem (Gärtner et al., 2003); practical kernels therefore compare substructures (R-convolution, Haussler, 1999): k(G, G') = \sum_{s \in S(G)} \sum_{s' \in S(G')} \kappa(s, s').

Main families: graphlet kernels (histograms of the induced subgraphs on 3–5 vertices), shortest-path kernels (compare the multisets of shortest-path lengths, with end labels), random-walk kernels and Weisfeiler-Lehman kernels, detailed below.

Proposition 2.4 — The random-walk kernel through the product graph The direct product graph G_\times of G and G' has the vertex pairs (v, v') as nodes.

It has an edge between (u, u') and (v, v') iff (u, v) \in E and (u', v') \in E'; for unlabeled graphs its adjacency is the Kronecker product A_\times = A \otimes A'.

The geometric random-walk kernel counts pairs of walks of equal length, with weight \lambda^k for length k: k_{\mathrm{RW}}(G, G') = \sum_{k \geq 0} \lambda^k\, \mathbf{1}^\top A_\times^k\, \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}^\top (I - \lambda A_\times)^{-1}\, \mathbf{1}, \qquad \lambda < 1/\lambda_{\max}(A_\times). It is a valid kernel; for unlabeled graphs it reduces to \sum_k \lambda^k\, w_k(G)\, w_k(G') with w_k(G) = \mathbf{1}^\top A^k \mathbf{1} the number of walks of length k.

Proof. By the mixed-product rule of Kronecker products, (A \otimes A')^k = A^k \otimes A'^k, so (A_\times^k)_{(u,u'),(v,v')} = (A^k)_{uv}\,(A'^k)_{u'v'}: a walk of length k in G_\times is exactly a pair of walks of length k, one in each graph.

Summing over all start and end pairs, \mathbf{1}^\top A_\times^k \mathbf{1} = (\mathbf{1}^\top A^k \mathbf{1})(\mathbf{1}^\top A'^k \mathbf{1}) = w_k(G)\, w_k(G'). Hence k_{\mathrm{RW}} = \sum_k \lambda^k w_k(G) w_k(G') = \langle \varphi(G), \varphi(G') \rangle, \qquad \varphi(G) = (\lambda^{k/2} w_k(G))_{k \geq 0} — an inner product, so positive semi-definite.

The geometric series converges when \lambda\, \lambda_{\max}(A_\times) < 1, and then equals the matrix inverse. ∎

Cost: a naive inversion of the nn' \times nn' system takes \mathcal{O}(n^6); Sylvester-equation and conjugate-gradient methods bring it to about \mathcal{O}(n^3) (Vishwanathan et al., 2010). Weakness: walks may totter back and forth along one edge, which inflates similarity between unrelated graphs.

Algorithm 2.5 — Weisfeiler-Lehman subtree kernel (Shervashidze et al., 2011) Start from labels \ell_0(v) (node labels, or a constant). At iteration h = 1, \dots, H, give each node the new label \ell_h(v) = \mathrm{relabel}\Big(\ell_{h-1}(v),\ \{\!\{\ell_{h-1}(u) : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\!\}\Big), where \{\!\{\cdot\}\!\} is a multiset and \mathrm{relabel} is an injective dictionary shared by all the graphs of the dataset.

Let \varphi_h(G) count how many nodes carry each label at iteration h; the kernel is k_{\mathrm{WL}}(G, G') = \sum_{h=0}^{H} \langle \varphi_h(G), \varphi_h(G') \rangle .

The label \ell_h(v) encodes the rooted subtree of depth h obtained by unfolding the neighborhood of v; computing all features costs \mathcal{O}(H\,m) per graph — linear, which makes the WL kernel usable on thousands of graphs.

It is the 1-WL isomorphism test (Definition 0.1) turned into features; message passing (ch. 5) is the same iteration with a learned, continuous relabeling, which is why GNNs are at most as discriminating as 1-WL (M2, ch. 2).

Example 2.6 — Two WL iterations, computed Compare the path G_1 = 1{-}2{-}3{-}4 and the star G_2 (center 0, leaves 1, 2, 3): both have 4 nodes and 3 edges. Start with a constant label \circ.

  1. h = 0: both histograms are \{\circ : 4\}, contribution 16.

  2. h = 1: a node with one neighbor gets A = (\circ, \{\circ\}), two neighbors B = (\circ, \{\circ, \circ\}), three C.

    G_1: \{A : 2, B : 2\}; G_2: \{A : 3, C : 1\}; contribution 2 \cdot 3 = 6.

  3. h = 2: in G_1 the ends become D = (A, \{B\}) and the inner nodes E = (B, \{A, B\}).

    In G_2 the center becomes F = (C, \{A, A, A\}) and the leaves G = (A, \{C\}); no label is shared, contribution 0.

So k_{\mathrm{WL}}(G_1, G_2) = 22, while k(G_1, G_1) = 16 + 8 + 8 = 32, \qquad k(G_2, G_2) = 16 + 10 + 10 = 36; the normalized kernel is 22/\sqrt{32 \cdot 36} \approx 0.65 .

Limit: on regular graphs all labels stay equal — two disjoint triangles and the 6-cycle get identical WL features, although they are not isomorphic.

Kernels or GNNs? A kernel fixes the features (\varphi) and learns only the classifier; a GNN learns the features too, and uses continuous node attributes naturally.

On small graph-classification datasets (MUTAG, PROTEINS, NCI1), the WL kernel with an SVM remains a strong baseline (survey by Kriege, Johansson & Morris, 2020), and under a fair protocol even structure-agnostic baselines are hard for GNNs to beat (Errica et al., 2020); kernels lose ground on large datasets, continuous features and node-level tasks.

The node similarities above also reappear inside learned models: the shared-neighbor counts of A^2 are the structural features added to GNN link predictors (for example SEAL, Zhang & Chen, 2018).

3. Node embeddings

A classical model (logistic regression, k-means) needs one vector per node, but a graph only gives relations. Node embeddings solve this by learning a vector z_v \in \mathbb{R}^d for each node from the structure alone, so that nodes close in the graph get close vectors.

Objective Learn z_v \in \mathbb{R}^d such that the similarity of the embeddings reflects proximity in the graph: \mathrm{sim}(z_u, z_v) \approx P(u \text{ neighbor of } v) — an implicit factorization of the adjacency matrix (or of a matrix of derived proximities), in contrast with the GNNs of ch. 5 which condition the embedding on the features X.

DeepWalk (Perozzi et al., 2014) and node2vec (Grover & Leskovec, 2016)

  1. generate random walks from each node (length \ell, r walks per node); node2vec biases the walk with (p, q) — developed below;
  2. apply word2vec skip-gram to the walks: the “sentences” are sequences of nodes, the window b defines the (center, context) pairs;
  3. afterwards: any vector method (k-means, classification…).

The random walk defines proximity: two often co-visited nodes get close embeddings — the NetMF theory (Qiu et al., 2018) shows retrospectively that DeepWalk factorizes a deterministic matrix of walks (Theorem 3.3).

Derivation 3.1 — Skip-gram with negative sampling: the loss and its gradient Take a center vector v_w and a context vector u_c. The negatives are drawn from the noise law P_n (unigram frequencies to the power 3/4, as in NLP theorem 4.1). \mathcal{L}(w, c) = -\log \sigma(u_c^\top v_w) \; - \; \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-u_{n_i}^\top v_w), with \sigma(s) = 1/(1 + e^{-s}).

Writing s_i = u_{n_i}^\top v_w and differentiating \frac{d}{ds}[-\log\sigma(s)] = \sigma(s) - 1: \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial v_w} = \big(\sigma(u_c^\top v_w) - 1\big)\, u_c \; + \; \sum_{i=1}^K \sigma(s_i)\, u_{n_i}, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_c} = \big(\sigma(u_c^\top v_w) - 1\big)\, v_w.

Reading: a descent step moves the center v_w toward the context u_c, because \sigma - 1 is negative, and moves it away from each negative. The weight \sigma(s_i) is large when that negative is already too close.

Stochastic gradient descent: cost per pair \mathcal{O}(K d), independent of n — this is what made word2vec (and hence DeepWalk) scalable; the hierarchical softmax trick replaces the \mathcal{O}(n) of the full softmax by \mathcal{O}(\log n).

Derivation 3.2 — node2vec: the biases p and q via transition probabilities At step i, the walk comes from t, is at v; for the candidate x \in \mathcal{N}(v): \pi_{tx} = \frac{w_{tx}}{Z}\, \alpha_{pq}(t, x), \qquad \alpha_{pq}(t, x) = \begin{cases} 1/p & \text{if } d_{tx} = 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } d_{tx} = 1 \\ 1/q & \text{if } d_{tx} = 2 \end{cases} (d_{tx} = distance in the graph; Z normalizes over \mathcal{N}(v).)

In a simple graph, d_{tx} \leq 2 for every x \in \mathcal{N}(v) \cup \{t\} if t and v are adjacent.

Three regimes:

  • p \lt 1: return toward t favored — the walk stays in the close neighborhood (BFS): two nodes with analogous structural roles (hubs, bridges) get close embeddings — structural equivalence;
  • q \lt 1: vertices at d_{tx} = 2 are favored — the walk moves away (DFS): two nodes of the same community/cluster are close — homophily;
  • p = q = 1: uniform random walk — DeepWalk is a special case of node2vec.

The qualitative proof: with p \to 0, the probability of returning to t dominates and the walk samples the 2-hop neighborhood of t almost uniformly; with q \to 0, the chain avoids t and behaves like a large-scale exploratory walk — find these behaviors again in exercise 2.

Theorem 3.3 — NetMF: DeepWalk as matrix factorization (Qiu et al., 2018) As the window b and the number of walks grow, DeepWalk converges to the (truncated, element-wise) factorization of the matrix: M = \log\Big( \frac{\mathrm{vol}(G)}{b\, T} \Big( \sum_{r=1}^{T} P^r \Big) D^{-1} \Big), \qquad \mathrm{vol}(G) = \sum_v d_v = 2m, that is, we look for Z Z^\top \approx M; T plays the role of the effective horizon of the walks.

Consequences:

(i) a theoretical bridge — DeepWalk, LINE, PTE and node2vec are all factorizations of variants of M (hence the name “NetMF”, Network Embedding as Matrix Factorization);

(ii) a deterministic algorithm — factorizing M directly (truncated SVD) matches or beats DeepWalk without drawing a single walk;

(iii) a diagnostic — the target matrix is built on P = D^{-1}A: the embeddings encode the walks, not the features, and remain transductive (a new node requires recomputing everything — direct motivation for the inductive GNNs of ch. 5).

The “word2vec on the graph” intuition — and its converse This is the historical NLP ↔︎ graphs bridge: a corpus of walks ≈ a corpus of sentences. DaSciM pushed the reverse idea — Graph of Words (ch. 11): representing a document by a graph of co-occurring terms and classifying it with graph tools. Both directions converge today in LLMs on graphs (ch. 10). To go beyond walks (with repetitions) toward paths (simple), see the Path Neural Networks (ch. 11; M2, ch. 2 and 9).

Example 3.4 — Karate Club: complete code Zachary’s graph (34 nodes, 78 edges, 2 factions at the club split) is the “MNIST of graphs”.

import numpy as np, networkx as nx
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import normalized_mutual_info_score

G = nx.karate_club_graph()
y = np.array([0 if G.nodes[v]["club"] == "Mr. Hi" else 1 for v in G])

def biased_walk(G, v, p, q, length):
    walk, prev = [v], None
    for _ in range(length):
        cand = list(G.neighbors(walk[-1]))
        probs = []
        for x in cand:
            d = 0 if x == prev else nx.shortest_path_length(G, prev, x) if prev is not None else 1
            probs.append({0: 1/p, 1: 1.0, 2: 1/q}[d])
        probs = np.array(probs) / sum(probs)
        prev, nxt = walk[-1], np.random.choice(cand, p=probs)
        walk.append(nxt)
    return walk

walks = [ [str(u) for u in biased_walk(G, v, p=1, q=0.25, length=30)]
          for _ in range(80) for v in G.nodes() ]
model = Word2Vec(walks, vector_size=64, window=5, min_count=0, sg=1, epochs=10)
Z = np.array([model.wv[str(v)] for v in sorted(G.nodes())])
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Z)
print("NMI :", normalized_mutual_info_score(y, km.labels_))   # ~0.5-0.8 depending on p, q, seed

4. Spectral filters and spectral clustering

To filter a signal carried by the nodes (smooth it, detect its sharp variations) or to cut a graph into communities, one needs a notion of frequency on a graph. The eigenvectors of the Laplacian (Definition 0.4) provide it; this chapter builds the graph Fourier transform, uses it for spectral clustering, then turns it into the first graph convolutions.

Definition 4.1 — Graph Fourier transform Let L = U \Lambda U^\top (spectral decomposition, U orthogonal, columns u_1, \dots, u_n). For a signal x \in \mathbb{R}^n carried by the nodes:

Key result — graph Fourier transform \hat x = U^\top x \quad (\text{analysis}), \qquad x = U \hat x \quad (\text{synthesis}).

The “frequencies” are the eigenvalues \lambda_k — small = slow variations over the graph (low frequencies), large = local alternations (high frequencies). This is the convolution of the Signal Processing module, with the Laplacian in place of -i\frac{d}{dt}: L plays the role of the differential operator there.

Theorem 4.2 — Variational characterization of \lambda_2 (Fiedler) For a connected graph: \lambda_2 \;=\; \min_{x \neq 0,\ x \perp \mathbf{1}} \frac{x^\top L x}{x^\top x}, attained at the Fiedler vector u_2. The smaller \lambda_2, the more there exists a quasi-constant signal “almost orthogonal” to \mathbf{1} — that is, two large blocks of weakly connected opposite values.

Proof. Write x = \sum_i c_i u_i (orthonormal basis); x \perp \mathbf{1} forces c_1 = 0 since u_1 = \mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n} (for a connected graph the kernel of L is exactly spanned by \mathbf{1} — the multiplicity computation of Definition 0.4).

Then x^\top L x = \sum_{i \geq 2} \lambda_i c_i^2 and x^\top x = \sum_{i \geq 2} c_i^2; the quotient is a weighted average of the \lambda_i, i \geq 2, hence lower-bounded by \lambda_2, with equality for x = u_2. ∎

Example 4.3 — Fiedler vector on two linked cliques (exact computation) Two K_5s (\{u, x_1, \dots, x_4\} and \{v, y_1, \dots, y_4\}) linked by the bridge (u, v). By symmetry, look for an antisymmetric eigenvector: x_i = a on the 4 non-bridge nodes of the left, u \mapsto b, and opposite values on the right.

The equations L x = \lambda x at a non-bridge node (degree 4) and at u (degree 5) give: \lambda a = a - b \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lambda b = 6b - 4a \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 2 = 0. Hence \lambda_2 = \frac{7 - \sqrt{41}}{2} \approx 0.298, \qquad b = (1 - \lambda_2)\, a \approx 0.70\, a; the Fiedler vector equals, up to normalization: (\underbrace{1, 1, 1, 1}_{x_i}, \underbrace{0.70}_{u}, \underbrace{-0.70}_{v}, \underbrace{-1, -1, -1, -1}_{y_j}) — the sign exactly separates the two communities, and the bridge node takes an intermediate value. This is the picture to remember about spectral clustering.

Theorem 4.4 — Spectral clustering: complete algorithm and justification (RatioCut relaxation) Algorithm (for k communities):

  1. compute the first k eigenvectors u_1, \dots, u_k of L; form F = [u_1 \| \dots \| u_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k};
  2. normalize each row of F;
  3. apply k-means to the n rows of F (spectral descriptor).

Justification: the RatioCut of a partition \{C_1, \dots, C_k\} is \mathrm{RatioCut} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\mathrm{cut}(C_j, \bar{C}_j)}{|C_j|} (cutting a small community is costly — unlike the plain minimization of the cut). Let H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} denote the renormalized indicators H_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[v_i \in C_j] / \sqrt{|C_j|}.

Proof.

  1. Exactness on the discrete class. H^\top H = I (orthogonal columns of norm 1). Moreover h_j^\top L h_j = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (h_j(u) - h_j(v))^2 = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \times |\{(u,v) \in E : |\{u, v\} \cap C_j| = 1\}| = \frac{\mathrm{cut}(C_j, \bar{C_j})}{|C_j|}. So \mathrm{tr}(H^\top L H) = \mathrm{RatioCut}: minimizing RatioCut = minimizing \mathrm{tr}(H^\top L H) over orthonormal indicators.
  2. Relaxation. We allow any F \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} with F^\top F = I; the relaxed problem \min_{F^\top F = I} \mathrm{tr}(F^\top L F) has as solution the first k eigenvectors (Ky Fan’s theorem: shown by writing F^\top L F = \sum_{ij} (F^\top u_i)\,\lambda_i\,(u_j^\top F) and exchanging the order of summation, then minimizing the diagonalized coefficients).
  3. Back to the discrete. The rows of F remain close to the rows of renormalized indicators; k-means groups them. ∎

Normalized Cut variant (Shi & Malik): weighting by the volumes d(C_j) leads to the generalized problem L y = \lambda D y, solved by the eigenvectors of L_{\mathrm{sym}} — this is the version used in practice (and by scikit-learn).

Links: PCA (Machine Learning module — the matrix F is the spectral counterpart of the projection); SBM (ch. 9) for the statistical approach; sign ambiguities of the eigenvectors — a real problem for GNNs, solved by SignNet (M2, ch. 4).

Theorem 4.5 — ChebNet: Chebyshev polynomials (Defferrard et al., 2016) Filtering exactly costs y = U\, g_\theta(\Lambda)\, U^\top x — a full decomposition (\mathcal{O}(n^3)) specific to each graph.

Trick: take g_\theta a polynomial of degree K in the Chebyshev basis. Set T_0(x) = 1, \qquad T_1(x) = x, \qquad T_{k+1}(x) = 2x T_k(x) - T_{k-1}(x), and \tilde\Lambda = \frac{2\Lambda}{\lambda_{\max}} - I (rescaling to [-1, 1]); then y = \sum_{k=0}^K \theta_k\, T_k(\tilde L)\, x, \qquad \tilde L = \frac{2L}{\lambda_{\max}} - I, without eigenvectors: K-localized filter, weights \theta shared by all graphs.

Proof.

  1. Chebyshev recursion. For x = \cos\theta, T_k(x) = \cos(k\theta); the addition formula \cos((k{+}1)\theta) = 2\cos\theta \cos(k\theta) - \cos((k{-}1)\theta) gives the recursion (it propagates to all x \in \mathbb{R} by polynomial identity).

  2. Functional calculus. Let us show T_k(\tilde L) = U T_k(\tilde\Lambda) U^\top: this is true for the monomials by recursion, \tilde L^m = (U \tilde\Lambda U^\top)^m = U \tilde\Lambda^m U^\top, the inner products U^\top U canceling — hence for every polynomial. Thus y = U\, g_\theta(\tilde\Lambda)\, U^\top x = g_\theta(\tilde L)\, x: the spectral filter is expressed as a polynomial of L applied directly to x.

  3. Locality. \tilde L x at a node only combines x_v and \{x_u : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}.

    By recursion on the Chebyshev recursion (linear combination of \tilde L^k x, k \leq K), the output at v depends only on the K-neighborhood. ∎

Cost: \mathcal{O}(K |E|) per application — linear in the size of the graph.

Proposition 4.6 — From spectral to spatial: the equality demonstrated Every filter that is a polynomial of degree K in L is exactly a K-localized operator, and conversely every message passing operator with K layers whose propagation is a polynomial of A realizes a polynomial spectral filter.

Proof. Forward direction: developed in (iii) above.

Conversely: a local layer y_v = \sum_{u \in \{v\} \cup \mathcal{N}(v)} c_{uv} x_u with uniform weights by symmetry writes in matrix form y = \sum_{k \leq 1} c_k\, \Pi_k(A) x for polynomials \Pi_k of degree \leq 1 (the diagonal term is \Pi_0, the edges \Pi_1).

Composing K layers without non-linearity, y = \Pi(A) x with \deg \Pi \leq K.

Since L = D - A is itself a polynomial in A (with D fixed by the graph), \Pi(A) = g(L) for a polynomial g of the same degree.

The non-linearities between layers break the exact equality — this is precisely the gap between the spectral “CNN on graph” and the spatial GNN: the GNN generalizes the filter by inserting point-wise non-linearities, like a 1D CNN. ∎

5. GNNs and message passing

Spectral filters are tied to the eigenvectors of one fixed graph. We want instead a layer that applies to any graph, costs time linear in the number of edges and trains like an ordinary network layer; message passing is that layer — each node updates its vector from the vectors of its neighbors.

Definition 5.1 — The message passing layer (MPNN, Gilmer et al., 2017) h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \phi\Big(h_v^{(\ell)},\ \underset{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}{\mathrm{AGG}}\ \big\{\psi\big(h_v^{(\ell)}, h_u^{(\ell)}\big)\big\}\Big) message \psi (computed by the neighbor), aggregation \mathrm{AGG} (permutation-invariant: sum, mean, max, attention), update \phi.

One layer = one round of message passing = one convolution = one diffusion step on the graph. The choice of \mathrm{AGG} is not innocent: mean/max lose cardinality ({a, a} and {a} have the same mean and the same max); this is the subject of the GIN theorem (M2, ch. 2).

Derivation 5.2 — GCN from spectral convolution (Kipf & Welling, 2017) Start from the Chebyshev filter of Theorem 4.5 truncated at K = 1, on the normalized Laplacian L_{\mathrm{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}, whose eigenvalues lie in [0, 2].

  1. Approximating \lambda_{\max} \approx 2 gives \tilde L = L_{\mathrm{sym}} - I, hence g_\theta(L)\, x \approx \theta_0\, x + \theta_1 (L_{\mathrm{sym}} - I)\, x = \theta_0\, x - \theta_1\, D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}\, x.
  2. Keeping a single parameter \theta = \theta_0 = -\theta_1 (fewer parameters, less overfitting on small graphs): g_\theta(L)\, x \approx \theta\,\big(I + D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}\big)\, x.
  3. The operator I + D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2} has its eigenvalues in [0, 2]: applied \ell times it can multiply some components by up to 2^\ell, and deep stacks become numerically unstable.
  4. The renormalization trick replaces it by \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}, where \tilde A = A + I adds a self-loop to each node and \tilde D is the degree matrix of \tilde A; its eigenvalues lie in (-1, 1].

Adding the features X, a weight matrix and a non-linearity, we obtain the GCN layer:

Key result — the GCN layer H^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big(\tilde D^{-1/2}\, \tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2}\, H^{(\ell)}\, W^{(\ell)}\Big), \qquad H^{(0)} = X,

a degree-normalized mean of the transformed representations of the node and of its neighbors; L layers give a receptive field of L hops.

Because the gains of the propagator stay in (-1, 1], depth no longer makes the signal explode — but repeated averaging makes it collapse instead: this is the over-smoothing of Theorem 5.3.

GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) — algorithm and pseudo-code For each node, aggregate a sample of neighbors then concatenate with the current representation:

input : G, features X, depth K, sample sizes s_1..s_K
for v in V : h_v^(0) = x_v
for l = 1 .. K :
    for v in V :
        S_v = uniform sample of N(v) of size s_l               # K neighbors in the paper
        a_v^(l) = AGG({ h_u^(l-1) : u in S_v })                 # mean / LSTM / pool
        h_v^(l) = sigma( W_l . [ h_v^(l-1) || a_v^(l) ] )       # concatenation!
        h_v^(l) = h_v^(l) / ||h_v^(l)||                          # L2 normalization
output : z_v = h_v^(K)

The concatenation preserves the information specific to the node (otherwise the mean of the neighbors crushes the local signal); the fixed per-layer sampling bounds the minibatch (ch. 7); the whole scheme is inductive: the weights W_l are re-applied to unseen nodes/graphs.

Special cases: AGG = mean ≈ GCN without renormalization; AGG = pool (MLP + max); AGG = LSTM over neighbors randomly ordered (the ordering breaks invariance… an accepted approximation; the ordering problem is addressed by GOAT, ch. 11).

GAT (Veličković et al., 2018) — equations and complexity Edge weights learned by attention: e_{uv} = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(a^\top [\,W h_u \| W h_v\,]\big), \qquad \alpha_{uv} = \frac{\exp(e_{uv})}{\sum_{u' \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \exp(e_{u'v})}, \qquad h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \alpha_{uv}\, W h_u^{(\ell)}\Big) with T parallel attention heads (T projections W^{(t)}) concatenated or averaged.

Per-layer complexity, with F and F' the input and output dimensions: \mathcal{O}(|V| F F' + |E| F') — linear in edges, comparable to GCN, but with weights tailored to each edge: useful when the graph is noisy or some relations matter more (suspicious transactions!).

Structurally identical to the attention of transformers (Deep Learning module): here the attention mask is given by the graph, there it is computed — the graph transformers of M2 (ch. 5) close the loop by learning the attention and biasing it with structure.

Beyond the homogeneous graph: R-GCN, edge features

  • R-GCN (Schlichtkrull et al., 2018): relational graph (edge types r in \mathcal{R}); h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\big(W_0 h_v^{(\ell)} + \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}_r(v)} \frac{1}{c_{v,r}} W_r\, h_u^{(\ell)}\big). To avoid the parameter explosion (|\mathcal{R}| \times F \times F'): basis decomposition W_r = \sum_b a_{rb} V_b or block-diagonal. Typical use: encoding knowledge graphs for link prediction (ch. 6).
  • Edge features: (a) line graph (each edge becomes a node); (b) convolution over edges (NNConv): \psi(h_u, e_{uv}) instead of \psi(h_u) — the chemical bond carries a type; (c) conditioned attention (GAT can inject e_{uv} into the e_{uv}-score).
  • Hierarchical pooling: DiffPool (Ying et al., 2018) learns a soft assignment matrix S^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{GNN}_A(H^{(\ell)})) and contracts the graph: H^{(\ell+1)} = S^{(\ell)\top} H^{(\ell)}, A^{(\ell+1)} = S^{(\ell)\top} A^{(\ell)} S^{(\ell)} — neural coarse-graining, costly but hierarchical; alternatives: sort/TopK pooling, pyramidal readout.

Theorem 5.3 — Over-smoothing: proof on a regular graph On a connected d-regular graph, GCN propagation without weights/non-linearity satisfies H^{(\ell)} = P^\ell X with P = \frac{A + I}{d + 1}. Then H^{(\ell)} \xrightarrow[\ell \to \infty]{} \frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top}{n}\, X, the harmonic solution (global mean), at geometric rate \rho^\ell with \rho = \max_{i \geq 2} \frac{|\lambda_i(A) + 1|}{d + 1} < 1.

Proof. On a d-regular graph, \tilde D = (d+1)I, hence \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2} = (A + I)/(d+1) =: P.

Since A is real symmetric, write X in its eigenbasis: A = \sum_i \lambda_i u_i u_i^\top, with u_1 = \mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}, \lambda_1 = d > \lambda_2 \geq \dots, and \lambda_i < d for i \geq 2 (equality only if there is a distinct connected component). Then P^\ell = \sum_i \big(\frac{\lambda_i + 1}{d+1}\big)^\ell u_i u_i^\top .

The i = 1 term equals exactly \frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top}{n}; all the others have a ratio \frac{\lambda_i + 1}{d + 1} in \big[\frac{1-d}{d+1}, 1\big) (since -d \leq \lambda_i < d), hence of absolute value < 1, and decay geometrically.

All the information of the non-constant components is erased: every node converges to the same mean row vector — the embeddings collapse. ∎

With weights and non-linearities, the phenomenon persists (rank collapse; Oono & Suzuki, ICLR 2020): this is a structural limit of GCN depth. Remedies:

  • Jumping Knowledge (Xu et al., 2018): the readout aggregates all the depths h_v = \mathrm{JK}(h_v^{(1)}, \dots, h_v^{(L)}) (concat / max / LSTM-attention): each node chooses its scale;
  • residuals and normalizations (GCNII, APPNP — developed in M2, ch. 3);
  • in practice, 2-4 layers usually suffice: the useful depth is the range of the statistical dependence of the task.

A second structural limit — over-squashing (topological bottlenecks) — is different: it concerns the channel rather than the representation, and is addressed by rewiring; the whole topic is in M2, ch. 3. A third limit: expressivity bounded by 1-WL (M2, ch. 2).

Heterophily: when neighbors differ

The seventh lecture of the official programme treats heterophily and temporal graphs. GCN-style averaging implicitly assumes homophily — linked nodes tend to share their label. Fraud rings (fraudsters link to honest accounts they exploit), dating networks, protein interactions or the “is a student of” relation violate it.

Definition 5.4 — Homophily ratio For a labeled graph, the edge homophily is h = \frac{|\{(u,v) \in E : y_u = y_v\}|}{|E|} \in [0, 1].

Citation graphs are strongly homophilous (Cora h \approx 0.81, Citeseer \approx 0.74). The standard heterophilous benchmarks are far lower (Texas \approx 0.11, Wisconsin \approx 0.21, Chameleon \approx 0.23, Squirrel \approx 0.22; Pei et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2020).

A low h is not harmful in itself: what matters is whether the label distribution of a node’s neighbors is informative about its own label (Ma et al., 2022).

Proposition 5.5 — Mean aggregation on a perfectly heterophilous graph, computed Take a connected d-regular bipartite graph whose two sides are the two classes, with a scalar feature x_v = +1 on one side and -1 on the other (h = 0). One GCN propagation (Theorem 5.3, P = (A + I)/(d+1)) gives (Px)_v = \frac{x_v + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} x_u}{d + 1} = \frac{1 - d}{d + 1}\, x_v.

For d = 1 the signal is erased; for d = 3 it becomes -0.5\,x_v: sign flipped, magnitude halved; with L layers it shrinks as \big(\frac{d-1}{d+1}\big)^L.

Spectral reading (Definition 0.4): this label signal has x^\top L x = \sum_{(u,v) \in E}(x_u - x_v)^2 = 4m, the largest possible value for \pm 1 signals — it is a high-frequency signal, and GCN propagation is a low-pass filter (ch. 4) that attenuates exactly such components.

Mixing the node with its neighbors inside one average makes the two cancel; keeping them separate, h_v = W_1 x_v \,\|\, W_2\, \mathrm{mean}_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} x_u, \quad \text{here } (x_v, -x_v), preserves the information fully.

Architectures for heterophily H2GCN (Zhu et al., 2020) combines three designs: ego/neighbor separation (as above, and as in GraphSAGE’s concatenation), aggregation over 2-hop neighborhoods (in a bipartite graph the same class sits two hops away) and combination of all intermediate layers (as Jumping Knowledge).

GPR-GNN (Chien et al., 2021) learns the coefficients \gamma_k of a polynomial filter \sum_k \gamma_k P^k X; negative coefficients produce high-pass filters (Proposition 4.6 makes this a spectral design).

FAGCN uses signed attention weights, letting a node subtract the messages of dissimilar neighbors. The interaction of heterophily with over-smoothing and over-squashing is developed in M2, ch. 3.

Temporal graphs

Definition 5.6 — Discrete- and continuous-time dynamic graphs A discrete-time dynamic graph is a sequence of snapshots G_1, \dots, G_T (one per day, per week); a continuous-time dynamic graph is a stream of timestamped events (u, v, t, e_{uv}) — transactions, messages, citations.

Tasks: temporal link prediction (will u interact with v at time t?), dynamic node classification (is this account fraudulent now?), and forecasting node signals on a fixed graph (traffic: spatio-temporal graphs).

The causality rule: a prediction at time t may use only events with timestamps < t.

Models 5.7 — Snapshot models, TGAT and TGN Snapshot models run a GNN on each G_\tau and an RNN over time, either on the node embeddings (GCRN) or on the GNN weights themselves (EvolveGCN, Pareja et al., 2020).

TGAT (Xu et al., 2020) handles continuous time with a GAT-like attention over a node’s past neighbors, each message concatenated with a functional time encoding of the elapsed time \Delta t: \Phi(\Delta t) = \big[\cos(\omega_1 \Delta t + b_1), \dots, \cos(\omega_k \Delta t + b_k)\big], with learned frequencies — a random-feature form of a translation-invariant kernel in time (Bochner’s theorem).

TGN (Rossi et al., 2020) adds a memory s_v(t) per node, updated at each event involving v by a recurrent cell, s_v(t) \leftarrow \mathrm{GRU}\big(s_v(t^-),\ \mathrm{msg}(s_v(t^-), s_u(t^-), \Delta t, e_{uv})\big); embeddings are computed by temporal attention over recent neighbors and their memories, and an MLP decodes link probabilities.

Events are processed in chronological batches, and the memory is updated after the batch has been predicted, so that no event informs its own prediction.

Example 5.8 — Temporal neighborhoods and leakage, computed Events: (a, b, 1), \quad (b, c, 2), \quad (a, c, 5), \quad (c, d, 7). Query at t = 6: will c interact with d?

The admissible history is the first three events. The temporal neighbors of c are b (elapsed \Delta t = 4) and a (\Delta t = 1).

Their time encodings \Phi(4) and \Phi(1) differ, so the attention can favour the recent contact.

Node d has no past event: the prediction is inductive and rests on its features and on the time encoding only.

A static GNN trained on the aggregated graph would contain the edge (c, d) — the very event to be predicted — and report an inflated score; the honest protocol splits events chronologically (train on t \leq 5, test on t > 5), as in Example 8.1. Memory-based models and temporal benchmarks are developed in M2, ch. 6.

import torch
from torch_geometric.nn import GCNConv, GATConv
from torch_geometric.datasets import Planetoid

dataset = Planetoid(root="/tmp/Cora", name="Cora")   # 2708 nodes, 5429 edges, 7 classes
data = dataset[0]

class GCN(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_hid, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.c1, self.c2 = GCNConv(d_in, d_hid), GCNConv(d_hid, n_classes)
    def forward(self, x, edge_index):
        return self.c2(torch.relu(self.c1(x, edge_index)), edge_index)

model = GCN(dataset.num_features, 64, dataset.num_classes)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.01, weight_decay=5e-4)
for epoch in range(200):
    opt.zero_grad()
    out = model(data.x, data.edge_index)
    loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(out[data.train_mask], data.y[data.train_mask])
    loss.backward(); opt.step()
acc = (model(data.x, data.edge_index).argmax(1)[data.test_mask]
       == data.y[data.test_mask]).float().mean()
print(f"Cora test accuracy : {acc:.3f}")   # ~0.81 with this simple setting

6. Link prediction and graph auto-encoders

Many graphs are incomplete: a recommender wants the purchases that have not happened yet, a knowledge graph misses true facts. This chapter turns the node representations of ch. 3 and 5 into scores for pairs of nodes, and trains them to recover the missing edges.

Definition 6.1 — Link prediction Predicting the existence (or the type) of a missing edge (u, v): the most widespread task in industry — recommendation (“who will like what?”), completion of knowledge graphs (“does this drug target this protein?”), prediction of future interactions (M2 ch. 6 for the temporal case).

Protocol: mask a fraction of the edges (10%), sample as many “negatives” (non-adjacent pairs), score all the pairs, measure AUC / Hits@k.

GAE / VGAE (Kipf & Welling, 2016) — graph auto-encoders GAE: encoder Z = \mathrm{GCN}(X, A); decode by dot product — the reconstruction probability of an edge:

Key result — the GAE decoder p(A_{uv} = 1 \mid z_u, z_v) = \sigma(z_u^\top z_v).

Training: maximize the likelihood of the observed edges and minimize that of the negatives — the cross-entropy of the negative sampling of ch. 3, transposed: \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{(u,v) \in E^+} \log \sigma(z_u^\top z_v) \; - \sum_{(u,v') \in E^-} \log \big(1 - \sigma(z_u^\top z_{v'})\big).

VGAE: variational Gaussian encoder q(Z \mid X, A) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}(z_i;\ \mu_i, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_i^2)) with \mu = \mathrm{GCN}_\mu(X, A), \log\sigma = \mathrm{GCN}_\sigma(X, A), prior \mathcal{N}(0, I); one maximizes the ELBO (Deep Learning module): \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(A \mid Z)] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q(Z \mid X,A)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big), reparameterization z_i = \mu_i + \sigma_i \odot \varepsilon, \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).

Results: on Cora, AUC \approx 0.91 (GAE) and \approx 0.92 (VGAE) — the prior regularizes the dot product (cf. the connection with SBM inference, ch. 9).

Scoring pairs: the choice of the decision function

  • dot product z_u^\top z_v — rank-d decomposition, symmetric, related to NetMF (ch. 3);
  • MLP over (z_u \odot z_v, z_u - z_v) or concatenation — asymmetric (important in recommendation: “A buys B” ≠ “B buys A”);
  • relational distances (KGC, below);
  • negatives: uniform (easy) vs hard — pairs sharing a neighbor (popularity-biased); the choice of negatives changes the learned ranking (Deep Learning module, contrastive; NLP module for the word2vec analogy).

PinSage (Ying et al., 2018) — recommendation at industrial scale The Pinterest deployment of GraphSAGE: a user-pin graph of 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges, trained by minibatches. Two key differences from GraphSAGE:

  1. importance sampling: instead of uniform neighbors, the T neighbors most often visited by short random walks started at the node (a personalized PageRank score); their normalized visit counts also weight the aggregation;
  2. ranking loss (hinge margin): \max(0, m - s(u, v^+) + s(u, v^-_h)) with v^-_h a hard negative (a pin popular but not clicked); production requires candidates ranked by approximate score (vector search — NLP/Multimodal module).

LLGA lesson: at this scale, the architecture matters less than the system (sampling, cache, GPU) — this is the bridge to ch. 7.

Knowledge graph completion (KGC) Link prediction on a typed heterogeneous graph (heads h, relations r, tails t):

  • TransE (Bordes et al., 2013): embeddings in \mathbb{R}^d with the energy score f(h, r, t) = -\|h + r - t\| — the relation is a translation; margin-based training (positives vs corrupted negatives); illuminating but limited (1-N, symmetric relations);
  • R-GCN + score (ch. 5): the encoder propagates over the typed graph, a decoder (DistMult: \langle h, \mathrm{diag}(r), t \rangle) scores the triples — the “encode-decode” architecture dominates;
  • GraphRAG link: an enterprise KG completed by KGC becomes queryable by an LLM (ch. 10); the completion deficits guide the collection of data — a data/model loop specific to the LLGA trade.

7. GNNs at scale

The models of ch. 5 were trained on Cora, which fits in memory; industrial graphs have millions of nodes and billions of edges. This chapter explains why full-batch training breaks at that scale and presents the three standard ways of training on pieces of the graph.

Why full-batch does not fit The gradient of a GCN layer mobilizes the whole graph: activation memory \mathcal{O}((|V| + |E|) d) per layer, not to mention the densification of the deep layers.

On ogbn-products (2.4 million nodes, 61.9 million edges), the full-batch does not fit on a GPU; three families of solutions, all grounded on the same observation: the dependence of a node over L layers is limited to its L-neighborhood.

7.1 — Neighborhood minibatching (GraphSAGE / NeighborSampler) For a batch of target nodes \mathcal{B} and a depth L with sampling sizes (s_L, \dots, s_1) (fanouts):

N_L = B                                             # the target nodes of the batch
for l = L .. 1 :                                    # from the output layer down to the input
    N_{l-1} = N_l + (s_l sampled neighbors of each node of N_l)
compute h^(1), ..., h^(L) layer by layer, layer l only on the nodes of N_l
update the parameters with the loss on the outputs of B only

Subgraph size: |\mathcal{B}| \times (1 + s_L (1 + s_{L-1}(\dots))). With L = 2, s_1 = s_2 = 10, a batch of 1 node touches at most 1 + 10 + 100 = 111 nodes — bounded, independent of n.

Two subtleties: (i) the estimator is unbiased in expectation for the mean over neighbors, but the variance explodes at the hubs (hence decreasing fanouts); (ii) the nested samplings create redundant computation across batches (the “expansive neighborhood”; PyG’s NeighborLoader implements exactly this).

7.2 — Cluster-GCN (Chiang et al., 2019): partitioning Partition the graph into k clusters (METIS), each batch = one whole cluster: GCN training on the induced subgraph costs \mathcal{O}(|E_c| d + |V_c| d^2) and each full epoch costs \mathcal{O}(|E|) — like the full-batch, but in constant memory, and deep GCNs become trainable again.

The price: the partition bias — the edges between clusters are invisible during the step.

The correction of the paper builds each batch from several clusters drawn at random, which restores the edges between the chosen clusters and varies the batches from one epoch to the next; for deep models it also enlarges the diagonal of the normalized adjacency, \bar A = \tilde A + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(\tilde A), so that each node keeps weight on its own representation.

Even better: link the clusters by a “virtual graph” of clusters — a precursor of the GraphRAG hierarchies (ch. 10).

7.3 — GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2020): sampling subgraphs, unbiasedly Flip the perspective: first sample a subgraph \mathcal{G}_s (by nodes, by edges, or by random walks), then compute a corrected gradient on it.

The key is the normalization by inclusion probabilities. Let p_v be the probability that the sampler includes node v and p_{uv} the probability that it includes edge (u, v) (both estimated once, by pre-sampling).

Inside the subgraph, the message from u to v is divided by \alpha_{uv} = p_{uv}/p_v, which makes each aggregation an unbiased estimator of the full-graph aggregation, and the loss is reweighted as \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{batch}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{G}_s} \frac{\mathcal{L}_v}{\lambda_v}, \qquad \lambda_v = |V|\, p_v, whose expectation is exactly the mean loss over all the nodes: \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{v \in \mathcal{G}_s} \mathcal{L}_v / \lambda_v\big] = \sum_{v \in V} p_v \mathcal{L}_v / (|V| p_v). The paper proves both statements by writing the expectation over the sampling scheme.

Hidden benefit: the batches are “dense” (no expansive neighborhood), and walk-based sampling captures the community structure.

The general picture: bias-variance-memory — neighbor-sampling (high variance, zero bias); cluster-GCN (partition bias, zero variance); GraphSAINT (unbiased by reweighting, variance controlled by RW sampling).

These trade-offs are the daily bread of M2, ch. 1 (and of the Emerging ML module for the federated parallel: partitions = clients).

8. Applications and hands-on

The previous chapters gave the models; this chapter maps them onto the use cases met in industry, then gives the protocol of a first honest experiment — the step where most graph projects go wrong.

  • Recommendation: user-item graph, link prediction (ch. 6); PinSage (3B nodes, 18B edges) = GraphSAGE at industrial scale; cold-start = the inductive case par excellence;
  • Fraud detection: node classification on the transaction graph; typical signals: counter proximities, money-laundering cycles (the motifs matter!), card hubs — GNNs capture the multihop neighborhood that an iid model ignores; temporal version in M2 (ch. 6);
  • Chemistry / biotech: molecule = graph (atoms = nodes, bonds = edges); graph classification to predict properties (toxicity, solubility, HIV activity); benchmarks: ZINC, QM9, ogbg-mol*; 3D geometry and equivariance: Definition 8.2 below, developed in M2 (ch. 8);
  • Knowledge graph completion: link prediction on heterogeneous KGs (TransE, R-GCN — ch. 6); the basis of “enterprise” RAG (ch. 10);
  • Scale up: neighborhood minibatching, clustering, sampling (ch. 7), multi-node GPUs (M2, ch. 1); open benchmarks: OGB.

Architecture choice — quick decision sheet

  • Homogeneous graph, rich features: GCN (baseline);
  • noisy/heterogeneous edges (fraud): GAT / R-GCN;
  • inductive production: GraphSAGE;
  • need to distinguish local structures (cycles): GIN + motif features (M2, ch. 2);
  • long-range dependencies (molecules, QA over KGs): graph transformer (M2, ch. 5).

Always: 2-4 layers, skip connections, light dropedge as a first choice.

Example 8.1 — Hands-on: evaluating link prediction without leakage Take a graph with 10,000 edges and the task “predict the missing edges”. The tempting protocol trains a GAE (ch. 6) on all the edges, then scores 1,000 of them as “test” edges.

The encoder has already passed messages along those edges, so their endpoints have similar embeddings by construction, and the AUC is optimistic. The honest protocol has three steps.

  1. Split before training. Remove 1,000 test edges (10%) and 500 validation edges (5%) from the graph, and draw as many non-adjacent pairs as negatives for each set.
  2. Train on what remains. The encoder sees only the 8,500 training edges, both as supervision and as the graph along which messages are passed; node features computed from the structure (degrees, PageRank) are computed on this training graph too.
  3. Score the held-out pairs. Report the AUC (or Hits@k) of the test positives against the test negatives, after choosing the hyperparameters on the validation set only.

When the edges carry timestamps (transactions, citations), replace the random split by a temporal one — train on the edges before a date, test on those after it — since a random split lets the model see the future.

In PyG, RandomLinkSplit implements steps 1 and 2; the same discipline applies to node classification, where the test labels must never be used, even through the features of their neighbors.

Geometric graphs and applications in biomedicine

The eighth lecture of the official programme concerns geometric graphs — graphs whose nodes live in physical space — and their applications in biomedicine, where molecules and proteins are the central objects. The new ingredient is a second symmetry, on top of the permutations of Definition 2.1: moving or rotating a molecule does not change its chemistry.

Definition 8.2 — Geometric graph and E(3) symmetry A geometric graph has node features h_v (atom type, residue) and coordinates x_v \in \mathbb{R}^3; its edges are chemical bonds or the pairs closer than a cutoff r (radius graph).

The Euclidean group E(3) acts by x \mapsto Qx + t with Q orthogonal (Q^\top Q = I: rotations and reflections) and t a translation.

A model predicting a scalar property (energy, solubility) must be invariant; one predicting vectors (forces) must be equivariant: f(QX + t, H) = f(X, H), \qquad F(QX + t, H) = Q\,F(X, H), and for updated positions X' \mapsto QX' + t.

Feeding raw coordinates into an ordinary MPNN breaks this symmetry; training with random rotations only enforces it approximately.

Proposition 8.3 — The EGNN layer is E(n)-equivariant (Satorras, Hoogeboom & Welling, 2021) The layer m_{uv} = \phi_e\big(h_u, h_v, \|x_u - x_v\|^2, a_{uv}\big), \qquad x_v' = x_v + C \sum_{u \neq v} (x_v - x_u)\, \phi_x(m_{uv}), \qquad h_v' = \phi_h\Big(h_v, \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} m_{uv}\Big), with MLPs \phi_e, \phi_x, \phi_h and a constant C, maps (QX + t, H) to (QX' + t, H'): the features are invariant and the coordinates equivariant.

Proof. Distances are invariant since Q is orthogonal: \|(Qx_u + t) - (Qx_v + t)\|^2 = \|Q(x_u - x_v)\|^2 = \|x_u - x_v\|^2 . Hence every message m_{uv}, and therefore h_v', is unchanged.

For the coordinates, the translation cancels in the differences and Q factors out of the linear combination: (Qx_v + t) + C\sum_u Q(x_v - x_u)\,\phi_x(m_{uv}) = Q\big(x_v + C\sum_u (x_v - x_u)\,\phi_x(m_{uv})\big) + t = Q x_v' + t . ∎

The cost is that of an ordinary MPNN. Invariant models that use only distances (SchNet) or distances and angles (DimeNet), and higher-order equivariant networks built on spherical harmonics (tensor field networks, MACE), trade cost for expressivity — developed in M2, ch. 8.

Example 8.4 — What a rotation changes, and what it does not A water molecule: O at (0, 0, 0), H_1 at (0.96, 0, 0), H_2 at (-0.24, 0.93, 0) (Å).

  • Distances: O–H_1 = 0.96, O–H_2 = \sqrt{0.0576 + 0.8649} \approx 0.96, H_1–H_2 = \sqrt{1.2^2 + 0.93^2} \approx 1.52.
  • The H–O–H angle is \arccos\big(\frac{-0.2304}{0.96 \cdot 0.9605}\big) \approx 104.5° .
  • Rotate by 90° about the z-axis, (x, y, z) \mapsto (-y, x, z): H_1 \mapsto (0, 0.96, 0), H_2 \mapsto (-0.93, -0.24, 0).

Every coordinate changed, yet the three distances (H_1–H_2: \sqrt{0.93^2 + 1.2^2} \approx 1.52) and the angle are identical, so an energy model built on them returns the same value, and an EGNN’s predicted positions rotate with the molecule.

Applications in biomedicine

  • Molecular property prediction: QM9 (about 134k small molecules, 12 quantum-chemical targets) and ogbg-molhiv (ch. 7) for graph regression and classification.
  • Machine-learned force fields: forces are -\nabla_x E, automatically equivariant when the energy model E is invariant.
  • Proteins: AlphaFold 2’s structure module reasons over residue frames with invariant point attention; RFdiffusion generates protein backbones by diffusion over residue frames; DiffDock predicts how a drug binds to a protein by diffusion over ligand poses.
  • Biomedical knowledge graphs: drug repurposing as link prediction (ch. 6) on heterogeneous graphs linking drugs, genes and diseases (Hetionet); protein–protein interaction networks for node classification.

9. Graph generation

The previous chapters analyzed given graphs; this chapter learns to produce new ones — molecules with a target property, synthetic networks for testing — which raises difficulties that images and text do not have.

Problem Sample G \sim p_\theta(G) where p is learned over a family of graphs (active molecules, road networks…) — possibly conditioned on a target property (“generate a molecule with this activity”) or on text (text-to-graph). This is the “Generative AI” side of the title of the master.

Three difficulties specific to graphs: (i) variable size (n random); (ii) discrete outputs (atom types, edge types); (iii) a space quotiented by isomorphism — two permuted adjacencies are the same graph: one needs exchangeable models (theory below).

9.1 — The limiting model: graphons and exchangeability A graphon is a measurable function W : [0,1]^2 \to [0,1]. Given W and n:

  1. draw x_i \sim \mathcal{U}([0,1]);
  2. then draw each edge (i, j) independently with probability W(x_i, x_j) — a graph G_n on n vertices.

Two results justify the object. The Aldous-Hoover theorem states that every infinite exchangeable random graph (distribution invariant under permutation of the vertices) is a mixture of such graphon models; and Lovász and Szegedy showed that every convergent sequence of dense graphs has a graphon as its limit for the cut metric (Lovász, Large Networks and Graph Limits, 2012).

The learning connection:

  • estimation: estimating W from a large graph = approximating it by a step function constant per block. Partition the n vertices into k blocks of sizes n_b, and set \hat W = \frac{e_{bb'}}{n_b n_{b'}} \quad \text{on the block } (b, b') (empirical densities); the partition is the problem — and SBM/clustering solves it;
  • generation: a learned graph model implicitly targets a \hat W (large molecules: large n, structured target distribution);
  • theory of deep generative models of graphs: the exchangeable architectures (invariant to permutations of the nodes) are those that approximate graphons — this is the consistency criterion of the models below.

9.2 — Classical random models: ER and SBM (with the math)

  • Erdős-Rényi G(n, p): all the edges iid with probability p; degrees \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n-1, p), no community; threshold phenomena (at \frac{\log n}{n}) for connectivity — the “null” reference against which to test structure (Refresher Statistics module);

  • SBM (Holland et al., 1983): each node has a latent block z_i \in \{1..k\} (uniform), and P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid z_i = a, z_j = b) = B_{ab}.

    Likelihood (with n_{ab} = observed edges between blocks a, b): \log P(A \mid z, B) = \sum_{a,b} n_{ab} \log B_{ab} + (n_a n_b - n_{ab}) \log(1 - B_{ab}); inference by maximum of likelihood (EM) or Bayesian (MCMC sampling).

    Detectability: for two symmetric blocks, below the Kesten-Stigum threshold no algorithm recovers the blocks better than chance; spectral methods built on the non-backtracking matrix reach this threshold, whereas the plain Laplacian spectral clustering of ch. 4 degrades on very sparse graphs.

    Generation: sample z then A; DCSBM variant (corrected degrees).

    The SBM is used in both directions: generating realistic synthetic graphs (benefit: total control) and inferring the blocks (statistically grounded clustering, without a resolution limit unlike the modularity of ch. 0); topic modeling by SBM (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018) treats a document-term corpus as a bipartite SBM — the “statistical” generalization of the Graph of Words of ch. 11.

9.3 — Graph VAEs: GraphVAE and NeVAE GraphVAE (Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018): a GNN encoder, decoder in one block \hat A, \hat X, \hat E (full adjacency); the mismatch of the outputs to the ground truth requires a matching matrix (Hungarian matching) — the node ordering problem is paid for here (cf. GOAT, ch. 11).

NeVAE (Samanta et al., AAAI 2019): a granular VAE designed for chemistry:

  1. sparse encoder: one embedding per atom (no full adjacency — memory \mathcal{O}(n) instead of \mathcal{O}(n^2)), MPNN messages between atoms;
  2. sequential decoder constrained by valence: at each step, a new atom is added and its bonds drawn by probability heads conditioned on the remaining valence — a carbon cannot receive 5 bonds; aromaticity is handled by dedicated heads;
  3. noise correction: local resampling of the invalid bonds during training — the penalty of forbidden solutions is softened;
  4. result: chemical validity > 90% before post-processing, where unconstrained GraphVAEs produce very few.

Moral: injecting domain knowledge (valence) into the architecture beats the post-processing (RDKit sanitization) — reused by all the following methods.

9.4 — Autoregressive models: GraphRNN (You et al., 2018) Generating a graph = generating a sequence. Order the nodes 1, 2, \dots (any order: the model must be robust to this choice — in practice canonical BFS orders); the graph is then the sequence of adjacency rows S^i = (A_{i1}, \dots, A_{i,i-1}) \in \{0,1\}^{i-1}.

Two levels: \text{node level: } h_i = \mathrm{RNN}_{node}(h_{i-1}, s_i), \qquad \text{edge level: } P(S^i \mid h_i) = \prod_{j < i} \mathrm{RNN}_{edge}(s_{ij}), where the state h_i conditions the edge RNN which emits S^i bit by bit (Bernoulli categorical per position); training maximizes the sequential likelihood (teacher forcing).

Properties: exchangeability approximated by the use of BFS orders (the prefix is invariant to intra-level permutations); complexity \mathcal{O}(n) decoding steps, \mathcal{O}(n) per step → quadratic — very reasonable for n \leq 100.

Limits: sensitivity to order (aromatic rings depend on the starting point); conditioning on properties is awkward (hence the models below). This “sequentialize the graph” scheme is exactly that of the LLM on text — the text-to-graph convergence is natural (ch. 10).

9.5 — Discrete diffusion: DiGress (Vignac et al., 2023) The molecular state of the art 2023-2026 transposes the diffusion of the Multimodal GenAI module to categories (atom types, bond types). Forward process (noising): at each step, each node and edge type is re-drawn q(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(X_t;\ X_{t-1} Q^X_t\big), \qquad q(E_t \mid E_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(E_t;\ E_{t-1} Q^E_t\big), where Q^X_t, Q^E_t are transition matrices (one row = law of the new type given the old one); after T steps, the stationary law is the marginal of the datasets (distribution of the atoms/bonds).

The continuous step this construction copies is x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, with \varepsilon a standard normal vector. The derivation lives in the Multimodal course, which is optional. This page does not re-derive it.

Backward process (denoising): a GNN \theta_\phi (MPNN + attention) predicts X_0 and E_0 clean from (X_t, E_t), then a Bayesian denoising step q(X_{t-1} \mid X_t, \hat X_0) is derived in closed form; perturbations: edges are also added/removed during noising (discrete topology).

Three winning choices: extra input features recomputed at each step (cycle counts, spectral features, and valency for molecules), which compensate for the limited expressivity of message passing; guaranteed exchangeability — the model never sees a node order (unlike GraphRNN); conditioning by graph (inpainting): freeze a skeleton and generate the rest — “target molecule” conditional generation.

3D cousin: EDM/EDT (equivariant positions — M2, ch. 8). The team’s NGG paper (ch. 11) transposes this recipe into latent space to condition on graph features.

9.6 — Evaluating generated graphs: distributional fidelity A generator is not graded image by image but distribution against distribution:

  • validity / uniqueness / novelty (chemistry): % of syntactically valid, distinct molecules, absent from training — a model that memorizes has a novelty of 0;
  • structural MMDs: maximum mean discrepancy \mathrm{MMD}^2 = \big\| \mathbb{E}_{G \sim p}[\phi(G)] - \mathbb{E}_{G \sim q}[\phi(G)] \big\|^2 with \phi = degree histograms, clustering coefficients, motif/orbit counts (the triangles of ch. 0!) — the remark: these features are exactly what 1-WL does not always capture (M2, ch. 2);
  • FCD (Fréchet ChemNet Distance): Fréchet distance between the Gaussians of the ChemNet embeddings of the generated vs real molecules. FID is the Wasserstein-2 distance between Gaussians fit to real and generated features. The derivation lives in the Multimodal course, which is optional.
  • OT distances: comparing graphs without node correspondence = Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport (Optimal Transport module); the MMDs above are kernel special cases of them.

9.7 — Conditional generation: the real industrial product Rank by target property (solubility, docking), condition on text (“an antibiotic against X”), on a skeleton (R-group optimization), on a partial graph (inpainting).

Three mechanisms: (i) input conditioning (concat of the descriptor — NGG); (ii) gradient guidance through a property predictor during sampling (the classifier guidance of images); (iii) search in latent space (Bayesian optimization — the classic QSAR pipeline).

This is where the GNN × LLM encounter is most fertile (ch. 10): the LLM proposes, the GNN evaluates, the loop optimizes.

10. Graphs × LLMs: GraphRAG and the hybrid

A large language model answers fluently but does not know the private documents of a company, and it loses track of relations spread across many of them. GraphRAG addresses both problems: it builds a knowledge graph from the documents with the LLM itself, then uses the graph, its entities, relations and communities, to retrieve what the model needs in order to answer.

Retrieve, then generate Classic RAG retrieves passages and then asks the model to generate from those passages. BM25, already derived on the NLP page, is the baseline retriever. The full treatment is LLM chapter 7. This chapter uses that pipeline and does not re-derive it.

10.1 — The complete industrial GraphRAG pipeline (after Edge et al., 2024)

  1. ingestion: splitting the corpus into chunks; LLM extraction of the entities (NER) and relations (typed relation extraction, cf. NLP module) in the triplet format (subject, relation, object, source passage); entity resolution (aliases); the graph is built incrementally — each edge keeps its source or sources;
  2. communities: community detection (Leiden — a Louvain variant, ch. 0) on the entity graph; for each community (at each level of the hierarchy), an LLM summarizes the entities, relations and themes; the summaries aggregate bottom-up;
  3. hybrid index: the graph plus a vector index of the chunks and the summaries (classic RAG remains useful for local queries);
  4. global queries (“what are the major themes of the corpus?”): map-reduce over the community summaries — each community answers partially, an LLM synthesizes; local queries (“who works with whom on X?”): expanding the neighborhood of entity X (linked entities, source chunks) then a grounded answer;
  5. evaluation: comparison of vector-text RAG vs GraphRAG on the completeness and diversity of the answers; cost: LLM ingestion is expensive — amortized over thousands of queries.

LLGA reading: each stage is an object of the course — clustering (ch. 0), link prediction for the completion of the KG (ch. 6), embeddings for the vector index (ch. 3).

10.2 — Making the LLM reason over the graph: encoding and pseudo-code

  • serialization: describing the (sub)graph as text (edge lists, adjacency, incidences); the NLGraph benchmark (Wang et al., 2023) shows that LLMs fail on elementary tasks (cycles, connectivity, shortest paths) as soon as size grows, even few-shot; the “Talk like a Graph” study (Fatemi et al., 2024) compares the encodings — none dominates everywhere;

  • pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024 — the master’s team): give the LLM the algorithm in pseudo-code rather than a description in natural language;

    input : edge list E, node s
visited = {s} ; stack = [s]
while stack not empty :
    v = stack.pop()
    for u in neighbors(v) in E :
        if u not in visited : visited.add(u) ; stack.push(u)
output : visited   # connected component of s

    the LLM executes the trace — systematic gains over natural instructions, across all LLM families; the lesson: on discrete structures, the programming language is a better “prompt” than natural language;

  • GNN + LLM hybrids: the GNN encodes the structure (ch. 5) and injects: (a) soft tokens into the prompt (projecting the GNN embeddings into the LLM space); (b) structural recall — the GNN retrieves the connected passages (like the KG of 11.1, but learned); (c) cross-distillation — the annotated LLM trains the GNN, the GNN filters the LLM’s hallucinations.

10.3 — GraphRAG limits and hygiene

  • extraction hallucinates (non-existent entities): control with a double pass and anchoring on chunks;
  • the graph is an approximation — queries depend on its quality; harden with KGC (ch. 6) before querying;
  • the ingestion cost vs the gain on relational queries: for a single-document FAQ, vector RAG is enough; GraphRAG pays off when the questions are multi-hop, aggregative or global;
  • privacy: the graph aggregates scattered facts — re-identification risk (M2, ch. 7).
  • Agents on graphs: the plan of a multi-agent system is a graph (orchestration DAG); “graph engineering” (2026) = programming the topology of the agents — the master loop: the graph in the model (attention), the graph for the model (RAG), the graph between the models (agents).

11. Research at DaSciM (Lutzeyer, Vazirgiannis)

The LLGA master is taught by the DaSciM laboratory (Data Science and Mining, LIX, École Polytechnique): M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer and their PhD students. This chapter presents four research directions of the laboratory in direct connection with the program; the papers are to be read and presented as a project — this is the module’s “paper” exercise (and the daily bread of M2, ch. 9).

11.1 — Graph of Words: from document to graph (Rousseau & Vazirgiannis; Tixier, Malliaros & Vazirgiannis) The “bag-of-words” representation ignores the order of words; the graph of words partially restores it:

  1. construction: sliding window of size w (typically 2-10); nodes = unique terms (weighted by frequency); edge (u, v) if u and v co-occur in a window, weighted by the number of co-occurrences; a document = one graph;
  2. keywords: extract the “central” nodes of the graph; Rousseau & Vazirgiannis (ECIR 2015) keep the main k-core (the maximal k-core: the largest subgraph with min degree \geq k), Tixier et al. (EMNLP 2016) classify by degeneracy (the core dominates classification);
  3. document classification: a corpus = a set of graphs → graph classification (ch. 2); moving from bag-of-words → graph-of-words enriches the co-occurrence features and improves the classic text classifiers.

Positioning: this is the symmetric twin of ch. 3 — DeepWalk applies word2vec to graphs, Graph of Words applies graphs to text. Posterity: graph-of-words + graph CNNs for hierarchical classification; topic modeling by bipartite SBM (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018); and the GraphRAG indexes (ch. 10) that index entities and co-occurrences — an enterprise graph of words.

11.2 — Path Neural Networks (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Idea: the GNN layer of ch. 5 aggregates walks of length 1 (the neighbors); paths of length \ell carry more structural information.

The path operator: for each node v, consider the set \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} of simple paths of length \ell starting from v; each path p = (v, u_1, \dots, u_\ell) is encoded by an RNN (GRU): m_p = \mathrm{GRU}\big(h_{u_\ell}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{GRU}(\dots \mathrm{GRU}(h_{u_1}^{(0)}, h_v^{(0)})\dots)\big), \qquad h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\big(\{m_p : p \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)}\}\big).

Three variants depending on \mathcal{P}_v: shortest paths (PathNN-s), random walks (PathNN-r), paths sampled in the neighborhood (PathNN-n) — sampling bounds the cost, since the number of simple paths grows exponentially.

Results: accuracies greater than or equal to the canonical GNNs on the classification benchmarks (nodes and graphs, including ZINC for molecules), with controlled complexity; the expressivity analysis (what the paths see that the walks miss — cycles, distances) is developed in M2, ch. 2 and 9.

Positioning in the course: the “natural” generalization of ch. 3 (DeepWalk walks) and of ch. 5 (1-hop neighborhoods).

11.3 — Graph Ordering Attention Networks (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) — J. Lutzeyer’s work on ordering and expressivity Invariant aggregators (sum/mean/max) throw away all ordering information — but the local order of the neighbors carries signal (think of a text: the bag of words loses the syntax). GOAT learns a local order:

  1. an attention orders the neighbors of v (scores learned on (h_v, h_u), sorting);
  2. the ordered sequence is processed by an RNN — a local sequence model, hence strictly more expressive than invariant aggregation;
  3. the GOAT levels (order at increasing radius) compose layer after layer.

The double positioning: in expressivity, GOAT distinguishes graphs that 1-WL confuses (joins M2, ch. 2); in generation, the order choice is THE Gordian knot of autoregressive models (GraphRNN, ch. 9.4; GraphVAE, ch. 9.3) — learning the order rather than suffering it is a transverse contribution. It is also an elegant answer to the GraphSAGE-LSTM question of ch. 5 (the neighborhood there was ordered randomly).

11.4 — Two recent bridges to GenAI: NGG and pseudo-code prompting

  • NGG — Neural Graph Generator (Evdaimon et al., with Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024): latent diffusion conditioned on graph features (spectrum, degrees…): a GNN encoder compresses graphs into latent vectors, a diffusion model learns the distribution and the conditioning there; one then generates graphs exhibiting the requested features — ch. 9.5 transposed into latent space, with the spectrum (ch. 4) as descriptor; applications: synthetic data, anonymization, distributional anomaly detection;
  • Pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024): make the LLMs reason about graphs (connected components, shortest paths…) by giving them instructions in pseudo-code rather than in natural language — systematic gains across all the LLM sizes tested; detailed in ch. 10.

These four directions (text↔︎graph, paths, order, generation/diffusion, LLM) cover exactly the map of the master — M2, ch. 9, develops them on the theory side.

  1. Question (6 points). A message-passing layer has three maps. Use definition 5.1.

    • Give one pair of multisets that share a mean and do not share a sum.

  2. Question (8 points). Two nodes are joined by one edge. The feature column is (1, 0) and the weight is the scalar 1. The activation is the identity. Use the renormalized GCN layer.

    • Write the adjacency matrix after self-loops are added, and its degree matrix.
    • Write the normalized propagator.
    • Compute the output feature column.

    Expected:

    • The looped adjacency is the 2 by 2 matrix of ones. The degree matrix is twice the identity.
    • The propagator equals one half times the matrix of ones.
    • The output column is (1/2, 1/2).

  3. Question (6 points). For a connected graph, prove the variational formula for the Fiedler value.

\lambda_2 = \min_{x \neq 0,\ x \perp \mathbf{1}} \frac{x^\top L x}{x^\top x} - Which constraint removes the constant eigenvector? - Expand a feasible vector in the orthonormal eigenbasis. - Name the minimizer.

*Expected*:

-   Orthogonality to the all-ones vector kills the coefficient of the first eigenvector, because that eigenvector spans the kernel.
-   The numerator becomes the sum of lambda_i c_i squared for i at least 2, and the denominator is the sum of c_i squared.
-   The quotient is at least lambda_2, with equality at the Fiedler vector.
