Why integrative projects

An integrative project is a six-week team project that solves one applied problem with several Year-1 courses at once. A team of three or four students builds a small system, for example a drug-repurposing ranker or a delivery planner. In it, a graph model, a text model, a decision component and a classical baseline must each earn their place, and an ablation measures what each one adds.

Status Independent proposal of these notes. No official Year-1 unit requires these projects, credits them or grades them, and nothing on this page is a programme decision.

The projects combine the four mandatory courses: Machine Learning in Period 1, then Reinforcement Learning, Graph ML and Text Mining and NLP in Period 2. They add the Deep Learning course that every student takes once, either DL P1 or DL P2. Route electives add one optional component per project.

They fit at the end of Year 1. Period 2 runs from 4 January to 19 March 2027 (Reinforcement Learning sheet), so a team can use its last six weeks, from 8 February, or the weeks after it, before the research internship starts. How to run it says which projects need the late Period 2 sessions.

Each project ends with an internship seed: one research question that the project’s evidence makes concrete. A student can take it to a host lab as a candidate topic for the M1 research internship.

Two mandatory courses assess through projects. The course description of Reinforcement Learning announces “a course project (50%) and practical work (50%)”, and that of Graph ML “quizzes and team projects”. Such a course could accept an integrative project, or its RL or graph component, as its course or team project only if its teaching team agrees. These notes cannot decide it.

Common frame

The eight projects share one frame: team, load, rules, milestones, rubric and responsible-AI expectations. A card below only adds what is specific to its project.

  • Team. Three or four students. Each student owns one course component (baseline and evaluation, graph model, text model, decision component); integration, the ablation and the report are shared. In a team of three, the owner of the baseline also owns the evaluation script.
  • Load. About 6 weeks × 8 h, so about 50 h per student, half of it on the owned component.
  • Prerequisites. Machine Learning, one Deep Learning course (P1 or P2), Reinforcement Learning, Graph ML and NLP; a route variant also needs its elective. The refreshers in Computer Science and Statistics cover the code and the intervals.

House rules.

  • Data. Public data with a named licence, recorded in the data card. No credentialed or controlled-access data and no scraping against terms; a free click-through registration is allowed.
  • Compute. A laptop CPU (8–16 GB of RAM) or one free Colab T4 (about 15 GB of GPU memory); a single run lasts at most 3 h. Training stays at or below 1.5B parameters (LoRA beyond 0.5B), inference at or below 8B in 4-bit, and no paid API is used.
  • Content. Only what the Year-1 courses teach, plus the readings a card names. Anything else goes into a route variant or the internship seed.
  • Measurement. Every metric is compared with a baseline taught in the courses, over at least 3 seeds, with 95 % intervals (bootstrap, Refresher in Statistics 4.1).
  • Reproducibility. One repository, a pinned environment and one command that rebuilds the main results table.

Milestones

Each milestone is a short hand-in, reviewed in the weekly check-in. The evaluation script is frozen at W2, so later components cannot tune it.

Responsible AI

The W5 note is one page. It covers the points below that apply to the project; each card names the critical ones.

  • Licence and consent. What the licence allows and forbids (for example, “ND” forbids sharing adapted data: share code and metrics, not processed data).
  • Errors by group. Errors per event, time step, category or demographic group when the data allow it, with intervals, as in the subgroup evaluation of ML 4.8; otherwise the limitation is stated.
  • Misuse and limits. What the system must not be used for. No clinical, legal, investigative or financial decision is taken without a human.
  • People’s data. No re-identification attempt, aggregate reporting only, no republication of posts or records.
  • Cost. Total compute time of the final pipeline, with an optional CodeCarbon estimate.

Y1-1 · Pharma · Drug repurposing with literature evidence

Problem and users A translational-research team wants a short list of known compounds worth testing against a disease, each with literature that supports or questions the link, and a rule for spending a small lab budget. The system ranks (compound, disease) pairs, attaches PubMed evidence and allocates 30 simulated experiments.
Data Hetionet v1.0: 47,031 nodes of 11 types and 2,250,197 edges of 24 types, including 1,552 compounds, 137 diseases and 755 “treats” edges; licences are kept per source on nodes and edges (public-domain parts CC0 1.0, some sources non-commercial or without a licence), so the data card lists those of the edge types used · BC5CDR: 1,500 PubMed abstracts with chemical and disease mentions and chemical-induced-disease relations, public-domain mark on the BigBio card · PubMed abstracts through NCBI E-utilities, at most 3 requests per second without an API key
Course components Graph ML: Adamic–Adar and Katz baselines (Def. 2.2, Graph ML ch. 2); R-GCN encoder (ch. 5) with a DistMult decoder (ch. 6); leakage-free split of Example 8.1 (ch. 8) · NLP: BM25 retrieval of abstracts (NLP ch. 2); BIO tagging of chemicals and diseases with entity-level F1, then the entity-marker relation classifier of 6.1 on (chemical, disease) pairs (NLP ch. 6), fine-tuned with Protocol 7.1 (NLP ch. 7) · ML: Platt or isotonic calibration of the ranker’s scores (4.5, ML ch. 4) · RL: Thompson sampling with Beta posteriors (Algorithm 4.4, RL ch. 4) · DL: fine-tuning (DL P1 ch. 7 or the training pipeline of DL P2 ch. 0)
Milestone specifics W1: hold out 20 % of the treats edges (151) for test and 10 % of the rest for validation; remove them, and any “palliates” edge on the same pairs, from the training graph · W2: Adamic–Adar and Katz on the training graph · W3: R-GCN + DistMult · W4: tagger and relation classifier on the official BC5CDR splits, extending NLP Ex. 14; BM25 over the abstracts retrieved for each top pair · W5: budget simulation in 6 rounds of 5 experiments, where an experiment succeeds if and only if the pair is a held-out treats edge
Deliverables and metrics Filtered Hits@10 and MRR of the true disease among the 137, on the 151 test edges, against Adamic–Adar and Katz · entity F1 on the BC5CDR test set against dictionary matching · evidence precision@5 on 20 top pairs, graded blind by two team members, with their agreement · discoveries in 30 experiments with Thompson sampling over diseases, against greedy top-30 and random, with 95 % intervals over 20 simulation seeds
Responsible AI BC5CDR relations are chemical-induced diseases, that is adverse effects: the pair classifier serves as a safety flag, never as evidence of efficacy. An association is not a therapy; the report presents rankings as hypotheses for researchers and makes no clinical claim.
Route variants Graph × LLM: align the R-GCN node embeddings with text embeddings of the abstracts that mention each entity, with a CLIP-style contrastive loss (Multimodal GenAI ch. 5) · AI systems: cost model for scoring all 212,624 compound–disease pairs (FLOPs, memory, latency on CPU and on a T4) with the roofline of DL P2 ch. 6
Objectives served O4 (knowledge-graph completion) · O5 (text evidence on a graph) · O6 (pharma) · O7 (end-to-end project)
Internship seed Does literature evidence extracted from abstracts improve the calibration of knowledge-graph repurposing scores on held-out indications, or does it only make them easier to explain?

Y1-2 · Finance · Illicit-transaction detection under an investigation budget

Problem and users A compliance team must flag illicit Bitcoin transactions as each time step arrives, and can investigate only 50 transactions per step. The system scores transactions with tabular and graph models and chooses which ones to investigate.
Data Elliptic Data Set (Kaggle, free registration): 203,769 transactions, 234,355 payment-flow edges, 49 time steps, 166 anonymised features; 4,545 illicit and 42,019 licit transactions, the rest unknown; about 0.7 GB; CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 · also loadable with PyG EllipticBitcoinDataset · temporal split of Weber et al. (2019): train on steps 1–34, test on steps 35–49, with a dark-market shutdown at step 43
Course components ML: class weights, PR-AUC and a threshold chosen after calibration (4.4–4.5, ML ch. 4); gradient boosting baseline (6.3, ML ch. 6) · Graph ML: homophily ratio of the labelled edges (Def. 5.4), GCN and GAT (Graph ML ch. 5); neighbour sampling (7.1, ch. 7); fraud use case and temporal split (ch. 8) · RL: LinUCB (Algorithm 4.5, RL ch. 4) choosing 50 investigations per step, with labels revealed only for the investigated transactions · DL: training practice for the GNN (DL P1 ch. 3 or DL P2 ch. 0)
Milestone specifics W1: the data card gives the label coverage per step and flags the shift at step 43 · W2: boosting on the 94 local features and on all 166 · W3: GCN and GAT trained on the graph of steps 1–34 · W4: bandit replay over test steps 35–49 with 50 checks per step · W5: ablation (features only, graph only, both; with and without the bandit)
Deliverables and metrics Illicit F1 and PR-AUC on the test steps, per step and before and after step 43, against boosting on local features · illicit transactions found per 1,000 investigations, against greedy top-50 by score and against random choice · homophily of the labelled edges · ablation table
Responsible AI A false flag sends an innocent party to investigation: report the false-positive rate at the operating threshold and its variation across steps. No de-anonymisation attempt. The licence forbids sharing adapted data: share code and metrics only.
Route variants Graph × LLM: federated training across three simulated banks that each hold a random third of the transactions (FedAvg, Emerging ML ch. 3), then DP-SGD at two privacy budgets (ch. 5), with the utility lost · AI systems: roofline of one GCN layer on the full graph, in FLOPs, bytes and time on CPU and on a T4 (DL P2 ch. 6)
Objectives served O4 (temporal graph learning) · O6 (finance) · O7 (budgeted decisions) · O8 (cost of errors, privacy)
Internship seed Why do graph models degrade after the dark-market shutdown of step 43, and does a budgeted policy that keeps exploring recover faster than a classifier retrained on a fixed schedule?

Y1-3 · Social media · Rumour early warning from text and propagation

Problem and users Fact-checkers want to know, within 30 minutes of a breaking-news post, whether its thread is a rumour, and which five threads per hour to check first.
Data PHEME for rumour detection and veracity classification (figshare record 6392078; this API view lists the download link): 6,425 Twitter threads from 9 breaking-news events, 2,402 rumours and 4,023 non-rumours, each with its reply tree and timestamps; 46.5 MB compressed; CC BY 4.0 · the five large events (Charlie Hebdo, Ferguson, Germanwings, Ottawa shooting, Sydney siege) hold 5,802 threads and both classes, the other four are too small, or almost one-class, for leave-one-event-out
Course components NLP: tokenisation of noisy posts (NLP ch. 1); Naive Bayes and sparse logistic regression (ch. 3); a fine-tuned encoder (ch. 7); burst detection of Example 8.7 (8.6, ch. 8) · Graph ML: Weisfeiler-Lehman kernel (Algorithm 2.5) with an SVM on reply trees (Graph ML ch. 2); a message-passing classifier with sum aggregation (Def. 5.1, ch. 5) · ML: leave-one-event-out cross-validation as a group split (1.2, ML ch. 1; leakage, 4.3, ch. 4) · RL: Thompson sampling to allocate 5 checks per hour (RL ch. 4) · DL: fine-tuning (DL P1 ch. 7 or DL P2 ch. 0)
Milestone specifics W1: build per-thread views truncated at 5, 10, 15, 30 and 60 minutes after the source post · W2: TF-IDF and logistic regression, leave-one-event-out on the five large events · W3: WL kernel and GNN on the truncated reply trees · W4: encoder and burst feature · W5: fact-checker simulation in event time with 5 checks per hour
Deliverables and metrics Macro-F1 under leave-one-event-out (mean and per event) against TF-IDF with logistic regression · macro-F1 as a function of the minutes since the source post · rumours caught per 100 checks, against ranking by classifier score and against chronological order
Responsible AI Threads hold real users’ posts: report aggregates only and quote no post or handle. Report errors per event. A rumour flag helps human fact-checkers prioritise; it never removes a post automatically.
Route variants Graph × LLM: persistence of the reply tree, filtered by reply time or read as a metric space for a Rips filtration, vectorised as landscapes (TDA ch. 2, ch. 4, ch. 5) · AI systems: Welch spectrum of the per-minute reply counts as features (Signal Processing ch. 7)
Objectives served O4 (graph classification) · O5 (text and propagation) · O6 (social networks) · O7 (early-warning tool)
Internship seed How early does propagation structure add information beyond the text, and does that gain transfer to events never seen in training?

Y1-4 · Recommendation · Explore–exploit news recommender with text and graph signals

Problem and users A news site ranks today’s articles for each visitor, although most of them are new and have no clicks yet. It must explore to learn about fresh articles without losing too many clicks.
Data MIND-small (Microsoft News Dataset): 50,000 users sampled from MIND; news articles with title, abstract, category and Wikidata entities, TransE embeddings of those entities, and impression logs listing the articles shown and clicked; free for research under the Microsoft Research License Terms, accepted on the download page · warm-up: the MovieLens-100k matrix factorisation of NLP Ex. 13
Course components NLP: content-based and collaborative recommendation, matrix factorisation (Def. 8.2, Model 8.3, 8.5, NLP ch. 8); SIF title embeddings (Prop. 4.2, ch. 4); Sentence-BERT (ch. 7) · Graph ML: node2vec on the user–news click graph (Graph ML ch. 3); link prediction with a graph auto-encoder, TransE entity vectors as features (ch. 6) · ML: ridge regression for the bandit estimate (ML ch. 2); k-means user segments as contexts (ML ch. 7) · RL: LinUCB and Thompson sampling (Algorithms 4.4–4.5, RL ch. 4) · DL: a two-tower encoder trained with DL P1 ch. 3 or DL P2 ch. 0
Milestone specifics W1: the data card lists the articles of the validation week never seen in training (the cold-start set) · W2: popularity and matrix-factorisation baselines (extends NLP Ex. 13) · W3: graph features · W4: text features and the bandit · W5: replay, where at each impression the bandit picks one of the shown articles and the log gives its click label
Deliverables and metrics AUC, MRR, nDCG@5 and nDCG@10 on the MIND-small validation set (the MIND benchmark metrics), against popularity and matrix factorisation · the same metrics on cold-start impressions · replay click rate of LinUCB and Thompson sampling against greedy and random choice, with bootstrap intervals; the replay only covers the lists the production system chose, a bias the report states
Responsible AI Popularity bias and filter bubbles: report the exposure Gini and the catalogue coverage of the top-10 lists, and their category diversity. Users are hashed identifiers: no re-identification attempt. The licence is non-commercial.
Route variants Graph × LLM: federated user factors, where click histories stay on the client and FedAvg updates the item side (Emerging ML ch. 3) · AI systems: serving budget of the two-tower model, with P50 and P99 latency for 50 candidates on CPU and the memory of the item index (DL P2 ch. 5, ch. 6)
Objectives served O4 (link prediction on a click graph) · O6 (recommendation, news media) · O7 (online decisions)
Internship seed Do entity-graph features close more of the cold-start gap for fresh news than text embeddings do, and does the answer survive an evaluation that corrects for the logging policy?

Y1-5 · Logistics · Learning-augmented delivery routing on a real city graph

Problem and users A small delivery operator plans daily tours in Manhattan for 50 to 100 customers, with vehicle capacities and time windows written in free-text notes. Planners want tours within a few percent of a strong solver, computed quickly, with honest travel times.
Data Street graph of Manhattan from OpenStreetMap (ODbL) through OSMnx (MIT) · one month of NYC TLC yellow-taxi trips (NYC Open Data terms of use): pick-up and drop-off times and taxi zones, no coordinates, with the zone lookup table (265 zones) · benchmark instances with best-known solutions from CVRPLIB, loaded with vrplib (MIT) · synthetic demand, seed 2027: 50, 75 or 100 customers drawn uniformly among drivable nodes, demand uniform on 1 to 9, capacity 50, a fixed depot node, and 30 % of the customers with a note giving a time window from a bank of templates · reference solvers PyVRP (MIT) and OR-Tools (Apache 2.0)
Course components Refresher CS: CSR matrices (2.7, Refresher CS ch. 2); Dijkstra and A* (7.4, ch. 7, also in Graph ML ch. 0) · ML: travel-time regression with histogram gradient boosting (ML ch. 6), temporal split and leakage checks (ch. 4) · RL: the attention policy of the routing worked example of 5.1, with capacity and time-window masks, trained with REINFORCE and the greedy-rollout baseline (Theorem 5.1, Prop. 5.2), and the cross-entropy method (Algorithm 5.6) as a gradient-free comparison (RL ch. 5) · Graph ML: a GAT encoder restricted to the nearest customers by travel time, compared with the full self-attention encoder of that example (Graph ML ch. 5) · NLP: time-window extraction from delivery notes as BIO tagging (NLP ch. 6) · DL: training practice (DL P1 ch. 3 or DL P2 ch. 0)
Milestone specifics W1: data card of the TLC month, with its outliers (zero-length trips, trips over 3 h) · W2: travel-time baseline from the free-flow edge speeds of OSMnx · W3: attention policy and its GAT variant trained on synthetic instances, extending RL Ex. 16 from the travelling salesman problem to CVRP · W4: time-window tagger on 500 generated notes and 100 hand-written ones · W5: full pipeline with time-window feasibility checks
Deliverables and metrics Gap to the CVRPLIB best-known solutions on instances with at most 100 customers, and to PyVRP and OR-Tools with a 10 s budget on the Manhattan instances, with inference time · travel-time MAE against the free-flow baseline · time-window F1 on the hand-written notes · ablation: policy alone (full or graph-restricted attention), solver alone, solver warm-started by the policy
Responsible AI Driver workload: report the spread of tour durations across drivers and the share of tours above a shift length the team states. Synthetic customers only, no real addresses. The planner proposes; a human dispatcher approves.
Route variants Graph × LLM: cost of full against graph-restricted attention as the number of customers grows to 200, with the attention costs of 6.3 (DL P1 ch. 6) · AI systems: sub-second re-routing when one order is added, with a latency budget and batching of the policy (DL P2 ch. 5)
Objectives served O4 (graphs for logistics) · O6 (logistics) · O7 (solver-in-the-loop system)
Internship seed When does a learned constructive policy, used as a warm start, cut the solver time needed to reach a fixed gap on city graphs it was not trained on?

Y1-6 · Chemistry · Text-to-molecule search and property-guided design

Problem and users A medicinal-chemistry team types a description, such as “a soluble analogue with a carboxylic acid”, and wants candidate molecules: first retrieved from a library, then generated and steered towards a property, always chemically valid.
Data ChEBI-20 (Edwards et al., 2021) as redistributed in ChEBI-20-MM: 32,998 molecule–description pairs split 26,402 / 3,299 / 3,297; MIT on the mirror, descriptions from ChEBI (CC BY 4.0) · the ZINC 250k sample of TDC (MolGen(name='ZINC'), 249,455 molecules; free to use, no redistribution of large subsets) · properties from TDC ADME: Solubility_AqSolDB (9,982 compounds, CC BY 4.0) and BBB_Martins (1,975 compounds; licence not specified on TDC, data of Martins et al., 2012, via MoleculeNet); QED from RDKit (BSD)
Course components ML: fingerprints with logistic regression and random forests (ML ch. 3, ch. 6); a Tanimoto kernel (ch. 5); scaffold split as a group split (4.3, ch. 4) · Graph ML: message-passing property predictor with sum aggregation (Graph ML ch. 5); autoregressive generation (9.4) and validity, uniqueness, novelty and FCD (9.6, ch. 9); the node-by-node generator of Graph ML Ex. 15 is an alternative to the GRU on SMILES · NLP: TF-IDF retrieval baseline (NLP ch. 2); Sentence-BERT text encoder (ch. 7) · RL: REINFORCE fine-tuning of the generator towards QED or predicted solubility, with a KL penalty to the pretrained model, as in the trust-region view of 5.3 (RL ch. 5) · DL: GRU generator on SMILES (DL P1 ch. 5 or DL P2 ch. 1)
Milestone specifics W1: canonical SMILES with RDKit; the ChEBI-20 test molecules are removed from every training set, ZINC included · W2: fingerprint baselines on AqSolDB and BBB with a scaffold split · W3: GNN predictor · W4: text-to-molecule dual encoder (text encoder and GNN) on ChEBI-20 train; generator pretrained on ZINC · W5: RL fine-tuning at three KL weights, then the dual-use filter
Deliverables and metrics Text-to-molecule MRR and Hits@10 on the 3,297 test pairs, against the TF-IDF baseline (nearest training description, then its molecule’s fingerprint neighbours) · solubility RMSE and BBB ROC-AUC on scaffold splits, against fingerprints with a random forest · validity, uniqueness, novelty and FCD of 10,000 samples, before and after fine-tuning · property gain at a fixed KL
Responsible AI Dual use: generated molecules pass a filter of toxic substructures, and the work stops at in-silico scores, with no synthesis planning. Predicted properties are model outputs with intervals, not measurements. ZINC subsets are not redistributed.
Route variants Graph × LLM: symmetric InfoNCE loss with a temperature study for the text–molecule encoder (Multimodal GenAI ch. 5), or persistence features of the molecular graphs for the property model (TDA ch. 8) · AI systems: molecular surfaces built by marching cubes (Def. 4.4) on the distance field of the atom spheres of an RDKit 3D conformer, whose area and shape descriptors are added to the property model (Shapes ch. 4)
Objectives served O2 (molecule generation) · O4 (molecular graphs) · O5 (text–graph alignment) · O6 (chemistry and pharma)
Internship seed Does a text-aligned molecular embedding make property-guided generation more sample-efficient than an embedding trained on properties alone?

Y1-7 · Medical imaging · Chest X-ray findings with report retrieval (research prototype)

Problem and users A radiology research group wants a prototype that predicts findings on a chest X-ray and retrieves similar past reports for comparison. It is a research prototype, not a diagnostic tool.
Data Indiana University chest X-ray collection on Open-i: 3,955 reports and 7,470 frontal and lateral images (PNG archive 1.36 GB, reports 1.1 MB); CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, stated in every report’s metadata; also mirrored on Kaggle · no label file: the team extracts finding labels from the reports · split by report, so the two views of a study stay together; the collection gives no patient identifier, a stated limitation
Course components NLP: finding extraction as BIO tagging, with negation cues (“no”, “without”, “clear of”) as a tag class (NLP ch. 6); report retrieval with BM25 (ch. 2) and Sentence-BERT (ch. 7) · DL: a CNN with transfer learning (DL P1 ch. 4 and ch. 7, or DL P2 ch. 1) · ML: multi-label learning and classifier chains (Def. 9.1, Example 9.2, ML ch. 9); calibration (4.5, ch. 4) · Graph ML: a GCN over the label co-occurrence graph that shares information between findings (Graph ML ch. 5)
Milestone specifics W1: a label set of 8 to 14 findings plus “normal”, written extraction rules, 100 reports audited by hand · W2: per-label logistic regression on frozen CNN features · W3: GCN head over the label graph · W4: report retrieval · W5: ablation and error analysis on 30 images read with their reports
Deliverables and metrics Per-label AUROC and macro-F1, against one-vs-rest logistic regression on frozen ImageNet features · Brier score and reliability diagram per label · Recall@5 of report retrieval (relevant = same primary finding), against BM25 · precision and recall of the label extraction on the 100 audited reports
Responsible AI Research prototype only: no clinical claim, not a medical device. The licence forbids sharing adapted material: share code and metrics, not processed images or labels. There are no structured demographics; sex appears in the indication of about 30 % of the reports (1,169 of 3,955 by a keyword count), enough only for an indicative per-sex comparison with wide intervals (ML 4.8). The noise of automatic labels is reported.
Route variants Graph × LLM: image–report contrastive retrieval with a CLIP-style loss (Multimodal GenAI ch. 5) · AI systems: class-activation maps (Zhou et al., 2016, a short reading) of the CNN of Computer Vision ch. 9, checked against the location words of the report (left or right, upper or lower)
Objectives served O4 (label graph) · O5 (image and text) · O6 (healthcare) · O8 (calibration, limits, licence)
Internship seed Can the label noise of automatic report extraction be estimated and corrected, and how much of the AUROC differences between findings does it explain?

Y1-8 · Public institutions · Statutory-article retrieval with a citation graph and a tool-using agent

Problem and users Citizens ask legal questions in plain French. A legal-information service wants the relevant statutory articles retrieved, cross-references followed, and an answer that cites the articles: information, not advice.
Data BSARD (Louis and Spanakis, 2022): 22,633 articles from 32 Belgian codes; 1,108 questions from citizens, reformulated and labelled by jurists (886 train, 222 test), plus 113,165 synthetic training questions; French (fr-BE); CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 · the citation graph (article → cited article) is built by the team from the references in the article texts
Course components NLP: Unicode normalisation and French tokenisation (NLP ch. 1); BM25, inverted index and ranking metrics (Model 2.4, Def. 2.7, ch. 2); extraction of references such as “article 1382 du Code civil” as BIO tagging, linked to article identifiers (ch. 6); a CamemBERT bi-encoder fine-tuned on question–article pairs (ch. 7) · Graph ML: PageRank prior on the citation graph (Def. 0.9, Graph ML ch. 0); neighbourhood expansion of the retrieved articles, as in GraphRAG local queries (ch. 10) · RL: a ReAct agent with two tools, search and follow_reference, under a step budget, read as a POMDP (Def. 7.1, RL ch. 7) · Statistics: bootstrap intervals over questions (Refresher in Statistics 4.1) · DL: fine-tuning (DL P1 ch. 7 or DL P2 ch. 0)
Milestone specifics W1: the data card records that the law snapshot is outdated, as the dataset card warns · W2: BM25, checked against the BM25 row of the BSARD paper on the 222 test questions · W3: citation graph, PageRank and expansion · W4: bi-encoder and agent · W5: faithfulness grid applied to 50 answers
Deliverables and metrics Recall@100, MAP@100 and MRR@100 on the 222 test questions, the metrics of the BSARD paper, whose BM25 baseline reports 51.33 %, 16.04 % and 24.59 % and whose best bi-encoder reaches 74.78 % Recall@100 · gain of graph expansion over the same retriever · agent: recall of the cited articles, and faithfulness of 50 answers graded by two people with a written grid (each claim supported, partly supported or unsupported by a cited article)

A team that starts on 8 February has then had about five sessions of each course; a milestone must come after the session that teaches what it needs.

Running after Period 2 means the weeks before the research internship starts; their number depends on each student’s internship dates. Six projects can continue as Year-2 capstones of the transverse project, which reuse their data card, baseline and frozen evaluation script: Y1-1 as C1, Y1-2 as C2, Y1-6 as C3, Y1-4 as C4, Y1-5 as C5 and Y1-8 as C8.

Supervision

One supervisor follows two or three teams: a teacher of one of the courses involved, or a PhD student, who volunteers. The supervisor holds a 30-minute check-in each week and two reviews: at W2, when the evaluation script is frozen, and at W5, when the ablation is in. A prospective internship supervisor may attend the W6 defence of a team that wants to pursue its seed.

How the grade could be used

The rubric gives a team grade out of 100, and the defence sets each student’s factor. Outside the curriculum, this grade carries no ECTS. It could serve as formative feedback before the internship, or as preliminary evidence in an internship application. With the agreement of the Reinforcement Learning or Graph ML teaching team, the project or its RL or graph component could count as that course’s project; nothing here decides it.

If a team member missed an elective

A route variant is meant for a student who followed the elective it uses. A student who missed it has three options: take the other route’s variant if they followed that elective, read the one linked chapter in W1 (at most 4 of the 50 hours, with the supervisor’s agreement), or skip the variant. Nothing else is lost: the core of every project needs only the mandatory courses and one Deep Learning course, and every Deep Learning link names both the P1 and the P2 course.
