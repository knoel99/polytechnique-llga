Definition 0.1 — Module notation A self-attention layer with h heads over a sequence of n tokens of dimension d (heads of dimension d_k = d / h); one block = multi-head attention + MLP (expansion factor 4), each preceded by LayerNorm (pre-norm) and bypassed by a residual connection. Let L denote the number of blocks, d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 4d. The “compute budget” of a forward pass is measured in FLOPs (floating-point operations); approximate convention: a model with \theta parameters trained on D tokens costs \approx 6\,\theta D FLOPs.

1. Vision Transformers: ViT, DeiT, Swin

Concrete scene: you reproduce an SSL paper. Same code, different seed → 3-point accuracy gap. This chapter sets how to read an experimental protocol before stacking blocks.

What breaks Comparing without the same pretrain / budget; skipping ablations (the gain comes from elsewhere); scaling without watching stability (loss spikes).

1.1. ViT — “An Image is Worth 16x16 Words”

The pivotal paper (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021) transposes the transformer to images with disarming radicality: cut the image into patches and treat them as tokens.

  • Patch embedding: the image x \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W} is cut into N = HW/P^2 patches of P \times P (typically 16×16); each patch is linearly projected into a vector of dimension d — implemented as a convolution with P \times P kernel and stride P;
  • special tokens: a prepended \texttt{[class]} token (its final state serves for classification) and learned additive positional embeddings (ViT has no a priori knowledge of the 2D structure: everything must be learned);
  • classification: MLP + softmax on \texttt{[class]}.
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class ViT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, img=224, patch=16, d=768, L=12, heads=12, n_classes=1000):
        super().__init__()
        self.n = (img // patch) ** 2                     # 196 patches
        self.patch = nn.Conv2d(3, d, kernel_size=patch, stride=patch)
        self.cls = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1, 1, d))
        self.pos = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1, self.n + 1, d))
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(
            d, heads, dim_feedforward=4*d, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)
        self.encoder = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, L)
        self.head = nn.Linear(d, n_classes)

    def forward(self, x):
        p = self.patch(x).flatten(2).transpose(1, 2)     # (B, n, d)
        z = torch.cat([self.cls.expand(p.size(0), -1, -1), p], dim=1)
        z = self.encoder(z + self.pos)
        return self.head(z[:, 0])                        # [class] token

Proposition 1.1 — Complexities: attention vs convolution For an input with n tokens/spatial positions and dimension d (one layer):

Layer FLOPs Params Spatial dependence
Self-attention O(n^2 d) $ O(d^2)$ gl obal, dynamic
MLP (d → 4d → d) O(n d^2) $ O(d^2)$ no ne
Conv k \times k $ O(k^2 n d^2)$ $O( k^2 d^2)$ local , static

Proof. Attention: QK^\top is an n \times d by d \times n product → 2n^2 d FLOPs; weighting V adds 2 n^2 d; the projections Q, K, V, O cost 4 \times 2nd^2 = 8nd^2 — hence the total \Theta(n^2 d + n d^2). Conv: each position (n of them) combines k^2 neighbors over d input/output channels → 2 k^2 n d^2. Two consequences: (i) for large n, attention costs n^2 — the wall of high-resolution images; (ii) for d \gg n, the projections dominate — identical to the MLP; the ratio depends on n/d, not on a “transformers always more expensive” rule. ∎

Note — The lesson of the ViT paper’s data ViT without massive pre-training is inferior to ResNet; only from hundreds of millions of images (JFT-300M) onward does it dominate: the transformer trades convolutional inductive bias (locality, translation) for the ability to ingest data. This architecture×data interaction is THE lesson of the paper — and the guiding thread of the module.

1.2. DeiT — training matters as much as the architecture

Touvron et al. (2021) obtain competitive ViTs on ImageNet-1k alone (1.3 M images): strong augmentation (RandAugment, mixup, cutmix), regularization (stochastic depth, tuned weight decay), the AdamW optimizer, and token distillation: a second token \texttt{[dist]} is supervised by the outputs of a convnet teacher (RegNet); the student thereby learns the inductive bias that the convnet provides for free. Moral: comparing architectures with unequal training recipes is meaningless — we will return to this with ConvNeXt (ch. 2) and the experimental protocol (ch. 8).

1.3. Swin — local attention via shifted windows

To curb attention’s cubic growth in image size, the Swin Transformer (Liu et al., ICCV 2021): (i) attention computed within local windows of M \times M patches (cost linear in the number of windows); (ii) each subsequent layer shifts the windows by M/2 — cross-window connections arise from the shift; (iii) progressive patch merging like a convolutional encoder — Swin serves as a dense backbone (detection, segmentation) where ViT was 1 token = 1 class. ViT (global, data-dependent, quadratic) and Swin (hierarchical, local, linear) embody the two poles that all subsequent architectures negotiate.

2. Modern convnets: the ConvNeXt lesson

After a decade of “transformers win everywhere”, the ConvNeXt paper (Liu et al., CVPR 2022) asks the reverse question: what are modernized convnets worth under an equal training recipe? Method: start from a ResNet and align it step by step with the ViT choices — each step is ablated and measured:

# Change ImageNet gain
1 modern training (90→300 epochs, AdamW, augmentation, label smoothing…) 76 → 78.8 (+2.8 — the largest gain from a single line!)
2 ResNeXt block design: 3×3 conv → depthwise, controlled width +0.6
3 macro-design: patch ratio 4 (stem conv 4×4, not 16), “stacked” proportion of blocks per stage (1:1:3:1) +0.8
4 inverted bottleneck (thin→wide→thin) and large kernels (7×7 depthwise) +0.7
5 micro: GELU, fewer normalizations/activations, LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm, “transformer-like” blocks +0.7

Paper’s conclusion: the thus “modernized” convnet matches or exceeds Swin at comparable throughput. The methodological lesson is stronger than the result: training (line 1) and scale details matter more than the architecture family — an indispensable preamble to chapter 8 on reproducibility, and a spectacular application of the “one change at a time” protocol.

Note — Depthwise, the missing link Depthwise convolution (one spatial filter per channel, no cross-channel mixing — see MobileNet, ch. 6) is what enables large 7×7 kernels at contained cost: cost O(k^2 n d) instead of O(k^2 n d^2). ConvNeXt borrows it from mobile convnets; the wide receptive field is a reminder that vision does not need global attention to be effective — only a wide range of dependencies.

3. Mixture of Experts (MoE)

Definition 3.1 — Sparse MoE layer A MoE feed-forward layer replaces the single MLP with E experts (identical MLPs with distinct parameters) and a routing gate g that computes per-token weights: y = \sum_{i=1}^{E} \mathbb{1}[i \in \mathrm{TopK}(g(x), k)]\; g_i(x)\; E_i(x), \qquad g(x) = \mathrm{softmax}(W_g x), typically k = 1 (Switch) or k = 2 (Mixtral), E = 8 to 128. Per token, only k experts are executed: total parameters grow with E, but FLOPs per token grow as k, not as E — capacity and compute are decoupled.

Proposition 3.2 — Capacity/cost decoupling For a MoE layer of dimension d, expansion r = 4, E experts, top-k: parameters \approx 2 E r d^2; FLOPs per token \approx 2 k r d^2 (plus the routing Ed, negligible); loading memory \propto E, activation memory \propto k.

Proof. An MLP d \to r d \to d has 2 r d^2 parameters and 4 r d^2 FLOPs per token (two matrix products); with E experts, the parameters are multiplied by E; the computation executes only the k selected experts — the factor is k, and the routing (a d \times E matrix) costs 2Ed, below 4krd^2 as soon as r \geq 1. ∎

3.1. Balancing the load

The canonical risk: the gate learns to send everything to the same experts (routing collapse) — the other experts receive no more gradient and die. The historical remedy of Shazeer et al. (2017): noise in the routing + an auxiliary loss; Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) formalizes the load balancing loss: \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}} = \alpha\, E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i\, P_i, \qquad f_i = \frac{\text{tokens sent to } i}{\text{total tokens}}, \quad P_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t} p_i(x_t), where P_i is the average probability assigned to expert i by the gate (differentiable) and f_i the fraction actually routed (non-differentiable); the product is minimal (1/E) when the load is uniform, and the minimization pushes the gate via P_i. Added to this are the capacity (\text{tokens}) \times \text{cap. factor} per expert — beyond it, tokens “overflow” (passed on to the rest of the network) — and per-expert parallelization (expert parallelism: each device holds experts).

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class MoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=1024, r=4096, E=8, k=2, alpha=0.01):
        super().__init__()
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList(
            [nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, r), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(r, d)) for _ in range(E)])
        self.gate = nn.Linear(d, E)
        self.k, self.E, self.alpha = k, E, alpha

    def forward(self, x):                      # x: (B*T, d)
        logits = self.gate(x)                  # (B*T, E)
        probs = logits.softmax(-1)
        topk_w, topk_i = probs.topk(self.k, dim=-1)
        topk_w = topk_w / topk_w.sum(-1, keepdim=True)
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for e in range(self.E):
            mask = (topk_i == e)               # tokens assigned to expert e
            sel = mask.any(-1)
            if sel.any():
                out[sel] += topk_w[sel].masked_fill(~mask[sel], 0).sum(-1, keepdim=True) \
                            * self.experts[e](x[sel])
        f = torch.zeros(self.E, device=x.device)     # routed fraction (non-diff.)
        f.scatter_add_(0, topk_i.flatten(),
                       torch.ones(topk_i.numel(), device=x.device))
        f = f / x.size(0)
        aux = self.alpha * self.E * (f * probs.mean(0)).sum()
        return out, aux                           # (output, auxiliary loss)

3.2. Panorama and readings

  • Shazeer et al. 2017 (“Outrageously Large Neural Networks”): MoE over LSTM, noisy top-k, proof that E can grow without exploding the compute;
  • GShard / Switch: translation at 600B parameters, then the top-1 single expert and clean balancing — the era of transformer MoE;
  • Mixtral 8x7B (2024): an open LLM, 8 experts top-2, 47B active parameters out of 39B × 8 — MoE arrives in everyday models; analysis in the M2 Large Language Models module (inference);
  • beyond: finely learned routings (residual capacity, shared experts, MoE on alternating layers). Reading question: what do the experts actually specialize in? (Interpretability studies often show syntactic more than topical specializations.)

Example 3.3 — A routing in practice A MoE layer d = 1024, r = 4096, E = 8, k = 2: parameters 2 \times 8 \times 4 \times 1024^2 \approx 67B per layer; FLOPs per token \approx 2 \times 2 \times 4 \times 1024^2 \approx 17 M — like a standard dense MLP; a 32-layer model loads \approx 2{,}1B parameters but activates only \approx 0{,}53B per token. Compare with the compute-matched dense model (larger d): the MoE wins on data-abundant tasks, pays in memory and system complexity (all parameters must reside in memory — see ch. 6 for the remedies).

4. Self-supervision I: contrast (SimCLR and its lineage)

Definition 4.1 — Self-supervised pre-training Manufacture the labels from the data itself (rotations, neighborhoods, paired patches, masking) and pre-train without annotation; the model learns transferable representations (linear probing, fine-tuning). The dominant pattern in vision: two families — contrastive (bring two views of the same content closer, push the others apart — this chapter) and generative/masking (reconstruct what is missing — chapter 5).

4.1. SimCLR — the complete recipe

  • two views: compositional stochastic augmentations (crop + flip + color + blur); batch of N images → 2N views; two views of the same image = positive pair; all other pairs = negatives;
  • encoder f(\cdot) (ResNet) + projection head g(\cdot) (2-layer MLP): the loss is applied on the output of g, but one transfers before the projection — the projection leaves the instance information in a dedicated subspace and protects the encoder’s features;
  • NT-Xent loss (normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy): \ell_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)} {\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}, \qquad \mathrm{sim}(u, v) = \frac{u^\top v}{\\|u\\| \\|v\\|}, averaged over both directions (i,j) and (j,i); \tau (0.1–0.5) controls the hardness of the negatives.

Example 4.2 — Why the temperature The term \mathrm{sim}/\tau rescales the similarities (into [-1/\tau, 1/\tau]): a small \tau sharpens the softmax — the closest negatives dominate the gradient (“hard” learning); a large \tau flattens it — a uniform gradient over all negatives. With cosine similarities that are bounded, without \tau the softmax would be too flat to discriminate: \tau is the single parameter that tunes this concentration/uniformity trade-off (see the alignment/uniformity analysis of Wang & Isola, 2020: NT-Xent pushes positives to align and negatives to spread uniformly over the sphere).

4.2. MoCo and BYOL — two decisive variations

  • MoCo (He et al., 2020): contrast requires large batches of negatives; MoCo replaces them with a queue of features from previous batches (“free” negatives) and a momentum encoder (exponential average of the weights) so that the negatives in the queue stay consistent — contrast without a big batch;
  • BYOL (Grill et al., 2020): no negatives at all — the online network predicts the output of the target (momentum-updated); one expects a trivial collapse (a constant = perfect solution); it does not happen, thanks to the asymmetric prediction head and the absence of gradient computation on the target side — a famous counter-example to “negatives are mandatory” and an excellent discussion topic (what happens with lower-dimensional projections? see the VICReg literature, which adds variance and covariance terms);
  • CLIP (Radford et al., 2021): image-text contrast — 400 M pairs, two encoders, a 2×NT-Xent similarity matrix — detailed in the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 5; it is the same mathematical object as SimCLR, the positive pair being (image, caption).

Proposition 4.3 — NT-Xent without negatives does not work as is If one removes the denominator (the negatives) in \ell_{i,j}, the loss reduces to -\mathrm{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau, minimized by z_i = z_j = 0 (or any common constant): collapse is the optimal solution.

Proof. Without negatives, \ell_{i,j} = -\exp(\mathrm{sim}/\tau) only penalizes the intra-pair similarity; the constant encoder z \equiv c achieves \mathrm{sim} = 1 for every pair — global optimum, useless representation. Negatives (SimCLR/MoCo), the asymmetric target (BYOL) or variance terms (VICReg) are three distinct solutions to this identification problem. ∎

def nt_xent(z1, z2, tau=0.5):
    """z1, z2: (N, d) head outputs for the two views; already normalized."""
    import torch
    import torch.nn.functional as F
    z1, z2 = F.normalize(z1, dim=-1), F.normalize(z2, dim=-1)
    z = torch.cat([z1, z2], 0)                      # (2N, d)
    sim = z @ z.t() / tau                           # (2N, 2N)
    N = z1.size(0)
    mask = ~torch.eye(2 * N, dtype=torch.bool, device=z.device)
    sim_i = sim.masked_fill(~mask, float("-inf"))
    target = (torch.arange(2 * N) + N) % (2 * N)
    return F.cross_entropy(sim_i, target)           # softmax over the negatives

5. Self-supervision II: masking (MAE, BEiT, DINOv2)

5.1. Masked Autoencoders (MAE)

He et al. (CVPR 2022): mask 75% of the patches and reconstruct the missing pixels. Two asymmetries make the whole design:

  • asymmetric encoder: only the 25% of visible patches enter the ViT — the training cost is reduced accordingly (and this is what makes high masking effective: the task remains difficult yet cheap);
  • lightweight decoder: the masked tokens (learned embeddings + positions) are re-inserted after the encoder; a small decoder (often narrow and shallow) reconstructs each missing patch; the decoder is discarded at transfer time.

Definition 5.1 — MAE loss With \Omega the set of masked patches and x_i the targets (normalized pixels: per-patch normalization by mean/variance; variant: targets from a VQ-VAE — BEiT): \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAE}} = \frac{1}{\|\Omega\|} \sum_{i \in \Omega} \\| \hat x_i - x_i \\|^2, averaged over the masked patches only. Per-patch normalization recenters the task on structure (relative to the patch) rather than on absolute luminance.

Example 5.2 — Why 75% and not 15% (BERT) Images have enormous spatial redundancy: masking 15% of an image can be solved by local interpolation (in the filter sense, not the network sense) — the encoder learns nothing semantic. At 75%, interpolation no longer suffices: reconstructing a half-masked face requires having understood “face”. Text, on the other hand, is non-redundant: 15% is enough to force syntax and semantics. The same idea — “mask until the task becomes semantic” — governs the rates in audio (wav2vec, 40–75%) and in video — and we will find this principle again in the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3, on the diffusion side (how much noise is needed for denoising to be difficult?).

5.2. BEiT and DINOv2

  • BEiT (Bao et al., ICLR 2022): replace the pixels with discrete visual tokens — a pre-trained VQ-VAE quantizes the patches into codes; the objective becomes a classification of the masked patches toward the codes (like BERT’s MLM — see the NLP module); in practice more stable than pixel regression;
  • DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023): the contrastive + masking duo, trained on a curated corpus LVD-142M (automated selection: similarity with reference corpora, deduplication); distillation between a teacher (momentum-updated, centered — no labels) and multi-resolution students produces features that are ready to use (linear) for classification, depth, segmentation — one of the best current open backbones, and the direct bridge to the lab session;
Family Signal Strengths Weaknesses
Contrastive (SimCLR, MoCo, DINO) view matching highly discriminative representations sensitive to augmentations; negatives to manage
Masking (MAE, BEiT) reconstruction of the missing little a priori; scalable; strong in dense tasks (seg.) weaker in “global” linear probing
Self-distillation (DINOv2) self-improved teacher-target best of both worlds, curated data complex recipes

6. Efficient architectures: MobileNet and distillation

6.1. Depthwise separable — the exact accounting

Proposition 6.1 — Cost of a depthwise separable convolution A standard k \times k convolution, d_{\mathrm{in}} \to d_{\mathrm{out}}, over an h \times w map, costs h w k^2 d_{\mathrm{in}} d_{\mathrm{out}} MACs. Its depthwise decomposition (one k \times k filter per channel; h w k^2 d_{\mathrm{in}} MACs) + pointwise (1 \times 1 mixing the channels; h w d_{\mathrm{in}} d_{\mathrm{out}} MACs) costs h w\, k^2 d_{\mathrm{in}} + h w\, d_{\mathrm{in}} d_{\mathrm{out}}. For d_{\mathrm{in}} = d_{\mathrm{out}} = d, the ratio to the standard conv is \frac{k^2 d + d^2}{k^2 d^2} = \frac{1}{d} + \frac{1}{k^2}, that is, for k = 3, d = 256: \approx 0{,}12 — a factor ~8 of compute saved at comparable “functional sector” (at the price of reduced per-layer expressiveness).

Proof. Accounting for the decomposition: depthwise — for each of the d_{\mathrm{in}} channels, a k \times k filter slides over h w positions; h w k^2 d_{\mathrm{in}}. Pointwise — at each position, a d_{\mathrm{in}} \times d_{\mathrm{out}} matrix product over h w positions; h w d_{\mathrm{in}} d_{\mathrm{out}}. The sum factors as h w d (k^2 + d), to be compared with h w d^2 k^2 — hence the ratio (k^2 + d)/(k^2 d) = 1/d + 1/k^2. ∎

  • MobileNetV1 (Howard, 2017): stacks separable + RELU blocks; two compression hyperparameters: width multiplier \alpha (channels × \alpha) and resolution multiplier \rho (input × \rho) — cost \approx \alpha^2 \rho^2;
  • MobileNetV2 (Sandler, 2018): block with inverted bottleneck (expand d \to 6d pointwise → depthwise → project 6d \to d') and linear bottleneck (no nonlinearity after the projection — ReLU would destroy information in the low-dimensional space); the shortcuts connect the bottlenecks — this is the block ConvNeXt will reuse (ch. 2) and EfficientNet will stack;
  • EfficientNet (Tan & Le, 2019): compound scaling — depth, width and resolution grow together: \text{depth} = \alpha^{\phi}, \text{width} = \beta^{\phi}, \text{resolution} = \gamma^{\phi} with \alpha \beta^2 \gamma^2 \approx 2 (FLOPs × ~2 per step of \phi) — the architecture as a parameterized family, not an isolated model; a forerunner of the scaling laws of chapter 7.

6.2. Knowledge distillation

Definition 6.2 — Temperature KD (Hinton et al., 2015) With z_p, z_e the logits of the teacher and the student and T the temperature: \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}(y, z_e) \;+\; \lambda\, T^2\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z_p / T)\,\big\\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(z_e / T)\big), \lambda \in [0{,}1], T typically 3–20. At high temperature, the teacher’s “soft” distribution reveals the structure of the errors (“this cat looks like a lynx, not like a truck”): bit-rich per-class information that the hard (1-of-K) label does not contain; the T^2 factor compensates for the shrinking gradients.

Applications: compression (student = MobileNet, ch. 6.1 — the standard production chain); self-distillation (DeiT, ch. 1; Born-Again Networks); and label-free distillation on massive unlabeled data (Noisy Student, DINOv2). Bridges: quantization/pruning on the deployment side is covered in the Computer Vision (P2) module, chapter 8; distilled LLMs in the M2 Large Language Models module.

7. Training at scale: recipes and scaling laws

7.1. The modern recipe

  • learning rate: linear warm-up (a few thousand steps — stabilizes Adam and the norms; indispensable for large batches) then cosine decay; decoupled weight decay (AdamW);
  • linear scaling rule (Goyal et al., 2017): batch × k ⇒ lr × k (valid as long as the batch stays “under the horizon”; beyond, diminishing gains — hence LARS/LAMB which normalize the gradient per layer);
  • regularization at scale: label smoothing — the target \mathrm{CE} is (1-\varepsilon) on the correct class and \varepsilon/(K-1) elsewhere; stochastic depth; mixup/cutmix;
  • mixed precision: bf16 (or fp16 + loss scaling) — beware of reductions (sums in fp32) and sensitive functions; the “zero” of chapter 8: a single hardware configuration does not transpose identically;
  • minimal distributed setup: data parallel (DDP); beyond that, state/optimizer sharding (FSDP/ZeRO); the Emerging Subjects in ML module covers distributed SGD in depth.

7.2. Scaling laws — Kaplan then Chinchilla

Definition 7.1 — Power law for the loss Kaplan et al. (2020): for transformers trained with N parameters and D tokens, the test loss empirically follows L(N, D) \approx E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, with irreducible E \> 0 and \alpha, \beta \> 0 (around 0.3–0.5); valid over several orders of magnitude — the most cited graph of modern deep learning.

Theorem 7.2 — Compute-optimal optimum (symmetric case, Chinchilla sketch) With the compute budget C \approx 6ND fixed, minimizing L leads to a point (N^\*, D^\*) where N^\* and D^\* both grow as \sqrt{C} — models and data must be enlarged together, in roughly equal parts (tokens per parameter ≈ constant, ~20 in the paper).

Proof. (Case \alpha = \beta, as a sketch; the general case is an exercise.) We minimize A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta under the constraint ND = C/6. Set \mu = C/6 and D = \mu/N: F(N) = A N^{-\alpha} + B N^{\alpha}/\mu^{\alpha} (using \beta = \alpha). F'(N) = -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \alpha B N^{\alpha-1}/\mu^{\alpha} = 0 gives N^{2\alpha} = A \mu^{\alpha} / B, that is N^\* = (A/B)^{1/2\alpha} \mu^{1/2} — proportional to \mu^{1/2} \propto C^{1/2}, and D^\* = \mu / N^\* \propto \mu^{1/2} \propto C^{1/2}. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) estimates \alpha \approx 0{,}34, \beta \approx 0{,}28 by regression and confirms ~C^{0{,}5} — revealing that GPT-3 and the like were “too big for their data”; at equal budget, 70B/1.4T beats 280B/300G. ∎

7.3. MuP — transferring hyperparameters (sketch)

The practical problem: the scaling law applies to losses, but every model size requires its own hyperparameters (lr above all) — and one cannot grid-search a 70B model. µP (muParametrization) (Yang & Hu — Tensor Programs IV–V): a rule for parametrizing the initializations and the lr by width (hidden layers; multipliers in d^{-1/2}, d^{-1}…) under which the training behavior becomes independent of width; one can then tune the hyperparameters on a small model and transfer them with zero trials to the large one (muTransfer) — spectacular savings on very large runs. We demonstrate the idea on an MLP of variable width (exercise 9) and refer to the paper for the full program.

Note — Reading a recipe A serious training paper publishes: the exact configuration (lr, schedule, wd, batch, precision), seeds and variances, the FLOPs budget, hardware and duration, and the ablation justifying every ingredient. It is this level of detail that enables replication — the subject of the next chapter.

8. Reproducible research: experimental protocol

Definition 8.1 — Minimal experimental protocol Any reported experiment: (1) a precise question; (2) a falsifiable hypothesis; (3) a protocol: fixed and versioned dataset, a single train/val/test split, a main metric declared before the experiment; (4) a baseline retrained under the same conditions; (5) n \geq 3 seeds with mean ± standard deviation; (6) the ablations; (7) the compute budget reported; (8) code (and ideally data) published.

8.1. Seeds matter (really)

Picard (2021) — “torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need” — trains identical classifiers over 10,000 seeds: the accuracy gap between seeds reaches several points; the choice of seed can flip a ranking of methods. Bouthillier et al. (2021) generalize: the variance due to seeds (init, data order, augmentations) must be estimated and reported; comparing two means without variability is comparing noise. Tools: Student’s t confidence intervals over n runs; paired tests when the seeds are coupled; correction for multiple testing (see the Refresher in Statistics module for tests, the Machine Learning module for model selection).

Example 8.2 — A “gain” that isn’t one Method A: 79.4% ± 0.6 (5 seeds); baseline B: 78.9% ± 0.5 (5 seeds). Difference of means 0.5; standard deviation of the difference \approx \sqrt{0{,}6^2/5 + 0{,}5^2/5} \approx 0{,}35 → statistic t \approx 1{,}4, to be compared with t_{0{,}975}(8) \approx 2{,}3: not significant. The right conclusion: “comparable to the baseline”, not “+0.5% state of the art”. In the project (ch. 11), every results table carries mean ± standard deviation and the name of the test used.

8.2. Replication: the module’s method

  1. pick a number: one row, one table of the pivotal paper;
  2. scale down: same task, model ×0.2 (µP, ch. 7, helps transpose the hyperparameters); a budget declarable in GPU-hours;
  3. instrument: experiment tracking (versioned configs + seeds + curves — W&B/tensorboard; determinism of the loaders; no leakage: the test set is looked at only at the end);
  4. report: paper vs replication table ± standard deviation; analyze the gap (data? recipe? hardware?) — that is where one learns.

Note — Leaks and common pitfalls Splitting per image on video data (nearly identical neighbors); normalization computed over the whole set; selecting the best checkpoint on the test set; an implicit “dev set = test set” after 50 config edits; comparison at unequal training budgets (see DeiT vs ViT, ch. 1; ConvNeXt, ch. 2). Each chapter of the module rereads its pivotal paper with this grid: what is the baseline? the seeds? the budget? the metric declared a priori?

11. Projects: read, explain, replicate

11.1. Reading seminar (half the module)

Each pair takes charge of one pivotal paper among: ViT, DeiT, Swin, ConvNeXt, Switch, SimCLR, MAE, MobileNetV2, EfficientNet, Chinchilla, µP — and presents it in 20 minutes + 10 of discussion: motivation, contribution, decisive experiment, protocol flaws. Grading grid: understanding (5), rendering of the experiments (5), critical eye (5), answering questions (5).

11.2. Replication project (the other half)

One number, one replication, one budget. Typical topics:

  • mini ViT on CIFAR-10/100: 4×4 patches, 6 blocks — replicate the accuracy vs dataset size curve (10k → 50k); discussion: inductive bias (ch. 1);
  • SimCLR vs supervised training: same ResNet-18, same data; linear evaluation; augmentation ablation (ch. 4);
  • mini MAE: 75% masking on unlabeled STL-10; reconstruct and transfer (ch. 5);
  • mini MoE: 8 experts top-2 on a character transformer; plot f_i (balancing; ch. 3) with and without the auxiliary loss;
  • mini scaling: fit L(N, D) = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta over a grid of MLPs/transformers; compare the predicted optimum with theorem 7.2 (ch. 7);
  • µP on an MLP: verify that the optimal lr becomes independent of width under the parametrization (ch. 7);
  • distillation: ResNet-18 teacher → MobileNetV2 student on a dataset of your choice; accuracy vs latency curves (ch. 6).

11.3. Deliverables

  1. repository: code (deterministic, fixed seeds), versioned config, training/evaluation scripts;
  2. report (6–8 pages): question, protocol (def. 8.1), paper/replication table with n seeds ± standard deviation, analysis of the gaps, budget (GPU-hours);
  3. defense: 15 minutes, including “the figure of the paper you wish you had seen” — your best addition to the original protocol.

Note — Grading and budget Seminar 30% · project 60% · participation 10%. Compute budget capped (team GPU credits); a “small but rigorous” project (5 seeds, intervals, ablation) beats a “big but opaque” one: that is the spirit of the module.

Exercises

  1. Patch embedding. For a 224×224 image, patches 16, d = 768: how many tokens, what size for the positional embedding, how many parameters in the projection; compare with patches 14 (ViT-B/14) — attention vs projection FLOPs.
  2. Complexities. Prove the “attention” row of proposition 1.1 (count every matrix product); for n = 196, d = 768, which term dominates; from which n does attention exceed the MLP?
  3. Recipe vs architecture. ConvNeXt gains +2.8 points from the training recipe alone; if a future paper compares two architectures with different recipes, by how much might its “gain” be tainted? Propose a fair protocol (def. 8.1).
  4. MoE. With E = 16, k = 2, d = 1024, r = 4096, 24 layers: total parameters, active parameters, FLOPs/token; what fraction of the model can an 80 GB device serve in bf16? (params ≈ 2 GB per billion in bf16.)
  5. Balancing. Show that \sum_i f_i P_i \geq 1/E, with equality iff the gate assigns a uniform probability and the routing is uniform; why does the factor E in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}} make the loss invariant to the number of experts?
  6. NT-Xent. Implement the loss of ch. 4 and verify: (a) N = 1 makes the loss degenerate (no negatives — explain the code’s behavior); (b) the gradient with respect to z_i pushes toward z_j and away from the most similar negatives; (c) the effect of \tau \in \{0{,}1, 0{,}5, 2\} on the gradient norm.
  7. MAE. On natural images, why does normalizing each patch by its own mean/variance change the task; estimate the loss of a constant decoder that predicts, for every masked patch, the mean of the visible patches (the bound to beat).
  8. Separable. Derive the cost of a MobileNetV2 block (6× expand, 3×3 depthwise, project) as a function of d, d'; for d = 24, d' = 96, compare with the equivalent ResNet block and verify the ~8 factor of proposition 6.1.
  9. General Chinchilla. Redo the proof of theorem 7.2 with \alpha \neq \beta and show N^\* \propto C^{\alpha/(2\alpha+\beta)}, D^\* \propto C^{\beta/(2\alpha+\beta)}; apply to \alpha = 0{,}34, \beta = 0{,}28 and compare with the symmetric case.
  10. µP sketch. On an MLP of width d with N(0, \sigma^2) initialization, compute the variance of the activations as a function of \sigma^2 and d (one layer); which dependence on d keeps the variance constant; how does this foreshadow hyperparameter transfer?
  11. Seeds. With the numbers of example 8.2, how many seeds would be needed for the 0.5% gap to become significant at 95%? (Statistical power; take \sigma \approx 0{,}55.)
  12. Paper critique. Take the main table of DeiT: identify the baselines, seeds, budgets; what additional experiment would you ask the authors for; write a one-page “review” (structure: summary, strengths, weaknesses, questions).
