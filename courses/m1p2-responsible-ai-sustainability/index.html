0. Refresher: optimization, duality, complexity

L3 reminders — the foundation (see Machine Learning module, ch. 0; Optimal Transport module, ch. 0; Optimization for AI module, ch. 0)

  • Linear program (LP): \min_x c^\top x subject to Ax \leq b, x \geq 0 — the feasible set is a polyhedron; the optimum (if it exists) is attained at a vertex (extreme point); solved in polynomial time (interior points, simplex in practice).
  • Duality: the dual of an LP is an LP; d^\star \leq p^\star always (weak duality), equality under conditions (Slater); the dual variables \lambda \geq 0 are interpreted as marginal prices — how much an extra unit of each resource is worth. This “economic” reading will be our central tool: the price of carbon, the price of a MWh, the price of a GPU hour.
  • Integer variables (ILP): as soon as one variable is integral (choose / do not choose), the problem becomes NP-hard in general (see Refresher CS module, ch. 7); it is handled by continuous relaxation + branch and bound (ch. 4).
  • Partial derivatives, convexity: convexity distinguishes “easy” (a local minimum is global) from “hard” — see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 1.

Proposition 0.1 — The lemma that serves the whole module (proved) Consider the LP \min\{c^\top x : Ax = b,\ x \geq 0\} and its dual \max\{b^\top \lambda : A^\top \lambda \leq c\}. Call a vector \lambda dual feasible if A^\top\lambda \leq c, and write \mu = c - A^\top\lambda \geq 0 for its reduced costs. Then b^\top \lambda \leq c^\top x for every feasible x and every dual feasible \lambda. If equality is attained, both solutions are optimal.

Proof. Using dual feasibility together with x \geq 0 first, then Ax = b: c^\top x \geq (A^\top\lambda)^\top x = \lambda^\top A x = \lambda^\top b .

If c^\top x = b^\top\lambda, then x attains a dual lower bound: x and \lambda are optimal. The gap c^\top x - b^\top\lambda, zero, decomposes as \mu^\top x, whence the complementary slackness \mu_i x_i = 0. ∎

1. Multi-objective optimization and the Pareto front

A loss such as “error + λ · energy” looks like a sensible way to obtain a frugal model, but the single number it returns hides the trade-off that a decision maker needs to see. This chapter replaces the single score by the Pareto front: the set of solutions that cannot be improved on one objective without being worsened on another.

What breaks Three habits break the analysis: summing objectives that have no common unit, leaving part of the footprint (the discarded training runs, the hardware manufacturing) outside the accounting boundary, and selling a single KPI as a proof of sustainability.

“Maximize accuracy”, “minimize energy”, “minimize latency”: three objectives with no common unit. Adding them naively — a “magic loss” — hides the trade-offs; the Pareto formalism makes them explicit and negotiable.

Definition 1.1 — Dominance and Pareto optimality Let there be q objectives F = (F_1, \dots, F_q), all to be minimized over a feasible set \mathcal{X}. A solution x dominates x' if F_i(x) \leq F_i(x') \text{ for all } i \quad \text{and} \quad F_j(x) < F_j(x') \text{ for at least one } j. A solution is Pareto-optimal if no solution dominates it; the image of the set of these solutions is the Pareto front \mathcal{P} = F(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Par}}) \subset \mathbb{R}^q. In the plane, it is the “southwest frontier” of the set of attainable values.

Theorem 1.2 — Weighted scalarization produces Pareto points (proved) Let w \in \mathbb{R}^q_+, w \neq 0, and x_w a minimizer of the scalar function F_w(x) = \sum_i w_i F_i(x) over \mathcal{X}. Then:

  • if w \geq 0 (positive or zero weights): x_w is weakly Pareto-optimal;
  • if w > 0 (all weights strictly positive): x_w is Pareto-optimal.

Proof. Suppose x_w is dominated by some x' \in \mathcal{X}: F_i(x') \leq F_i(x_w) for all i, with a strict inequality for one index j. Summing against w: F_w(x') = \sum_i w_i F_i(x') \leq \sum_i w_i F_i(x_w) = F_w(x_w), and if w_j > 0 the inequality becomes strict: F_w(x') < F_w(x_w) contradicts the minimality of x_w.

Hence (b); (a) follows by noting that with a zero weight on the strictly better component, only the weak inequality remains (weak optimality). ∎

Proposition 1.3 — The converse is false: Pareto points that no weighting finds (worked example) Objectives F_1(x) = x and F_2(x) = 1 - x^2 over x \in [0, 1].

Proof. F_1 is strictly increasing, F_2 strictly decreasing: every point of [0,1] is Pareto-optimal, and the front is the arc \{(t,\, 1-t^2) : t \in [0,1]\}. It is a concave arc (it sags above the chord (0,1)–(1,0): in the middle, (0.5\,;\,0.75) against (0.5\,;\,0.5) for the chord), typical of “hollowed” fronts.

Yet, for every weighting w > 0, the function F_w(x) = w_1 x + w_2(1 - x^2) is concave in x (second derivative -2w_2 < 0): its minimum over [0,1] is attained at the endpoints, never inside.

Weighted scalarizations thus produce only the two endpoints x=0 and x=1: the whole middle of the front — for example x = 0.5 — is Pareto-optimal but invisible to any weighting. Geometry: a weighting traces a line of negative slope in the (F_1,F_2) plane and picks the lowest point; on a hollowed front, the line can rest only at the endpoints.

In practice: training networks (non-convex landscapes, often hollowed fronts) and then sweeping only the weights visits only part of the trade-off — other tools are needed (\varepsilon-constraints, Chebyshev method — def. 1.4; multi-objective evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II). ∎

Definition 1.4 — Two scalarizations that see the whole front

  • \varepsilon-constraints: minimize F_1(x) subject to F_i(x) \leq \varepsilon_i (i \geq 2). By sweeping the \varepsilon, one visits the whole front — including the non-convex part. It is also the most dialogue-friendly formulation: “I impose at most k kWh on myself, what is the best accuracy?”
  • Chebyshev: \min_x \max_i\, w_i\,(F_i(x) - z_i^\star), where z^\star is the ideal point (the minimum of each objective separately). Every Pareto-optimal solution minimizes such a function for some w > 0 (under a standard assumption); the resulting w reads as an exchange rate.

Example 1.5 — The ε-constraint method recovers the hollow part of the front Return to prop. 1.3: F_1(x) = x, F_2(x) = 1 - x^2 on [0,1].

The \varepsilon-constraint problem reads: minimize x subject to 1 - x^2 \leq \varepsilon, that is x \geq \sqrt{1-\varepsilon}, for \varepsilon \in [0, 1]. Its solution is x_\varepsilon = \sqrt{1-\varepsilon}, and as \varepsilon sweeps [0,1] the point x_\varepsilon sweeps the whole interval: every point of the front is reached, including x = 0.5, obtained for \varepsilon = 0.75. The weighted sums of theorem 1.2 found only the two endpoints.

The price to pay is that each value of \varepsilon is a constrained problem, usually harder to solve than an unconstrained weighted sum; the reward is that the parameter \varepsilon is a budget expressed in the units of the objective (“at most 0.75”), which a decision maker can discuss, whereas a weight has no unit.

Common mistakes when drawing a front

  1. Comparing points that were not measured under the same conditions — another test set, another hardware, another batch size — produces a front of artefacts.
  2. Reporting one run per configuration: if the seed-to-seed spread of accuracy is 0.5 point, two points closer than that are not ordered, and a “dominated” model may simply be unlucky.
  3. Reading a front as a recommendation: the front lists the rational options; choosing among them requires a value judgement (a carbon price, a latency requirement) that must be stated explicitly.

Example 1.6 — Accuracy vs training energy: the module’s most cited front On a given task (say image classification), for every energy budget E (kWh measured at the wall, ch. 6), the best attainable accuracy \mathrm{acc}(E) defines a point of the front. Typically: rapid growth (the under-trained model gains a lot for little energy), then an elbow, then a plateau (scaling law — see the Deep Learning P2 module, ch. 1).

Typical Pareto-rational decisions: stop at the elbow, where -d\,\mathrm{acc}/dE drops; the points beyond the elbow are dominated in the “carbon-cost” sense as soon as the kWh is valued at its carbon price (ch. 2).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# example: two models (growing size), fictitious but realistic measurement
E = np.array([0.4, 1.1, 2.6, 5.0, 9.5, 18.0])      # kWh (measured at the wall)
A = np.array([71.2, 78.9, 84.1, 87.0, 88.3, 89.0]) # accuracy % on a validation set

# Pareto front (minimize E, maximize A): keep the non-dominated points
keep = [True]*len(E)
for i in range(len(E)):
    for j in range(len(E)):
        if E[j] <= E[i] and A[j] >= A[i] and (E[j], A[j]) != (E[i], A[i]):
            keep[i] = False
print("front points:", list(zip(E[keep], A[keep])))
# marginal gain of acc per kWh -- the "elbow" of the front
print("d.acc/dE:", np.diff(A)/np.diff(E))
plt.loglog(E, 100 - A, "o-"); plt.xlabel("E (kWh)"); plt.ylabel("error (%)")
plt.show()

2. Optimizing under resource constraints

This chapter puts resource limits inside the optimization problem instead of checking them afterwards. The resources are GPU hours, watts, megabytes of memory and — the module’s signature angle — carbon, which depends on when and where one computes. The electric grid is not uniformly green: its carbon intensity varies by a factor of up to 10 depending on the hour and the region, which leaves a large margin of manoeuvre to a scheduler.

Definition 2.1 — Carbon intensity and emissions of a computation The carbon intensity c_t (gCO2eq/kWh) is the amount of CO2 emitted per kWh consumed on the grid, at hour t, in region r — public hourly data (grid operators, providers such as Electricity Maps). For a power P_t (kW) consumed during hour t:

Key result — emissions of a computation \text{emissions (gCO}_2\text{eq)} \;=\; \sum_t c_t \cdot P_t \cdot \Delta t .

This is to be multiplied by the PUE (ch. 6) if one counts the whole data center. Unlike the kWh, this criterion is non-uniform in time: this is precisely what makes scheduling profitable.

Theorem 2.2 — Temporal shifting gains (at most) the dispersion of c_t (proved) Let there be a deferrable job of duration T hours (at constant power P), executable within a window [0, H]. Let \bar c be the mean and c_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq c_{(H)} the sorted intensities of the window’s hours.

Then the minimal emission is attained by executing the job during the T cleanest hours, and equals P\,\Delta t \sum_{s=1}^{T/\Delta t} c_{(s)}. In particular the maximal relative gain compared to an average immediate launch is \frac{\bar c - \text{mean of the } T \text{ smallest}}{\bar c} \leq \frac{\max c - \min c}{\max c}.

Proof. The decision variable 0 \leq x_t \leq 1 (fraction of the job done at hour t) gives the LP \min \sum_t c_t x_t \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_t x_t = T/\Delta t, a continuous “knapsack”. The optimal solution saturates the x_t of the smallest c_t (monotone permutation).

Exchange argument: if x_t < 1, x_{t'} > 0 with c_{t'} > c_t, transferring \varepsilon from t' to t reduces the cost by \varepsilon(c_{t'} - c_t) > 0. The gain bound follows by comparing with \bar c, itself between c_{(1)} and c_{(H)}. ∎

What the theorem does not say: the real constraints A training run is not a “knapsack”: it has a minimal duration, checkpoints that are costly to move, a deadline (a paper submission!), sometimes dependencies (pipeline, ch. 2 of the Deep Learning P2 module).

The true formulation is an ILP:

  • variables x_{j,t} \in \{0,1\} “task j runs at hour t”;
  • \sum_t x_{j,t} = d_j (duration);
  • precedence constraints s_j + d_j \leq s_k, windows;
  • machine capacity \sum_j P_j x_{j,t} \leq C;
  • objective \min \sum_{j,t} c_t P_j x_{j,t}.

This is exactly the ground of ch. 4 (branch and bound) — and the heart of the “carbon-aware computing” paper by Radovanovic et al. (Google), deployed in production to shift deferrable tasks toward the cleanest windows and regions (references); the Emerging ML module (ch. 2–3) covers the systems side (federation, placement).

Example 2.3 — One training run, 43 possible start times A 6-hour training run (1 GPU, 0.4 kW, PUE 1.1) can start at any time within the next 48 hours. Hourly intensities (fictitious data but of realistic shape: solar in the middle of the day, wind at night, gas at the evening peak):

import numpy as np
c = np.array([ 85, 72, 60, 55, 58,110,220,310,380,340,260,160,
              95, 70, 55, 45, 38, 42,120,280,420,470,390,240])  # gCO2/kWh, 0h..23h
c = np.concatenate([c, c])            # two identical days (simplification)
T, P, pue = 6, 0.4, 1.1

best = min(range(48 - T + 1), key=lambda s: c[s:s+T].sum())
worst = max(range(48 - T + 1), key=lambda s: c[s:s+T].sum())
emi = lambda s: P*pue*c[s:s+T].sum() / 1000               # kg CO2eq
print(f"best slot {best}h-{best+T}h: {emi(best):.2f} kg")
print(f"worst slot {worst}h-{worst+T}h: {emi(worst):.2f} kg")
print(f"relative gain: {1 - emi(best)/emi(worst):.0%}")
# expected: best 12h-18h, about 0.15 kg; worst 18h-24h, about 0.84 kg;
# a gain of about 82% (a factor 5.6) between the solar afternoon and the evening peak

Dual interpretation: if the scheduler minimizes the electricity bill \sum_t p_t x_t plus a carbon price \pi (€/gCO2eq) times the emissions \sum_t c_t x_t, hours are ranked by p_t + \pi c_t. At \pi = 0 the schedule follows the cheapest hours; as \pi grows it moves towards the cleanest ones, and the LP (ch. 4) tells exactly at which carbon price each hour enters or leaves the schedule. This is a shadow price: ch. 0 kept its promise.

Proposition 2.4 — A budget constraint has a price: the sensitivity of the optimum (proved for a smooth one-dimensional case) Let \mathrm{err}(E) be the best error attainable with an energy E, assumed decreasing, convex and differentiable, and consider the budgeted problem p^\star(B) = \min\{\mathrm{err}(E) : E \leq B\}. Then p^\star(B) = \mathrm{err}(B), the Lagrange multiplier of the budget constraint is \lambda^\star = -\mathrm{err}'(B) \geq 0, and \lambda^\star = -\frac{d p^\star}{dB} : the multiplier is the error saved by the last kWh of budget.

Proof. Since \mathrm{err} is decreasing, the constraint is active and p^\star(B) = \mathrm{err}(B). The Lagrangian L(E, \lambda) = \mathrm{err}(E) + \lambda (E - B) is stationary in E at E = B when \mathrm{err}'(B) + \lambda = 0, i.e. \lambda^\star = -\mathrm{err}'(B), which is non-negative because \mathrm{err} decreases; convexity makes these KKT conditions sufficient.

Differentiating p^\star(B) = \mathrm{err}(B) gives \frac{dp^\star}{dB} = \mathrm{err}'(B) = -\lambda^\star . (The same identity holds for convex problems in any dimension, wherever p^\star is differentiable: Boyd and Vandenberghe, §5.6.) ∎

Example 2.5 — Where to stop: the multiplier read in numbers Suppose measurements fit \mathrm{err}(E) = 30\,E^{-1/2} % (with E in kWh), a typical power-law shape.

  • With a budget B = 4 kWh, the error is 15 % and \lambda^\star = 15\,B^{-3/2} = 1.875 point per kWh: one more kWh buys almost two points of error.
  • With B = 16 kWh, the error is 7.5 % but \lambda^\star = 15 \cdot 16^{-3/2} \approx 0.23 point per kWh: the same extra kWh now buys a quarter of a point.

If the organization has decided that one point of error is worth at most 0.5 kWh of extra consumption (an exchange rate of 2 points per kWh), the rational budget is where \lambda^\star = 2, i.e. B^{3/2} = 7.5: B \approx 3.8 \text{ kWh}. The multiplier turns the vague “elbow” of example 1.6 into a number that can be negotiated.

3. Model frugality: distillation, quantization, pruning

The cheapest kg CO2 is the one not emitted: reducing the model’s cost at equal quality is the first lever of sustainable AI. Three families, complementary, all “Pareto-active” (ch. 1).

Definition 3.1 — Distillation (Hinton, Vinyals, Dean, 2015) Train a small student to reproduce the soft outputs of a large teacher: \mathcal{L} = \alpha\, T^2\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z_T/T)\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(z_S/T)\big) + (1-\alpha)\,\ell(y, z_S), T is the temperature. At high temperature, the teacher’s logits deliver similarities between classes (dark knowledge), far more information per example than the one-hot label alone.

Reference: Hinton et al. 2015; for the LLM case (logit distillation), see the Deep Learning P2 module, ch. 5.

Example 3.2 — What the temperature reveals, in numbers A teacher outputs the logits z_T = (4, 2, 0) for an image of a cat, the classes being (cat, dog, car).

  • At T = 1 the softmax gives (0.867,\ 0.117,\ 0.016): almost a one-hot label, and the student learns little more than “cat”.
  • At T = 4 the logits become (1, 0.5, 0) and the softmax gives (0.506,\ 0.307,\ 0.186): the teacher now says clearly that this cat looks much more like a dog than like a car.

This ranking of the wrong classes is the “dark knowledge” that a hard label does not carry. The factor T^2 in the loss compensates for the fact that the gradient of the softened term with respect to the student’s logits scales like 1/T^2. Without it, raising the temperature would silently reduce the weight of the teacher’s term.

Proposition 3.3 — Uniform quantization error (proved) Quantizing on b bits a value x \in [x_{\min}, x_{\max}] means storing the integer code q(x) = \mathrm{clip}\big(\mathrm{round}((x - x_{\min})/s),\, 0, 2^b-1\big), with step s = \frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{2^b - 1} (written \Delta below), and reconstructing \hat x = x_{\min} + s\, q(x). Then, for every x \in [x_{\min}, x_{\max}]:

Key result — uniform quantization error |\hat x - x| \;\leq\; \frac{\Delta}{2}.

(Libraries usually store the offset as an integer zero point z = \mathrm{round}(-x_{\min}/s), so that \hat x = s\,(q - z) and the value 0 is represented exactly; this only affects the bound at the edges of the range.)

For a network of L layers with Lipschitz constants \mathrm{Lip}_1, \dots, \mathrm{Lip}_L and quantizations of steps \Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_L: \|\hat f(x) - f(x)\| \;\leq\; \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \Big(\Delta_\ell/2\Big) \prod_{m > \ell} \mathrm{Lip}_m.

Proof. First inequality: nearest-neighbor rounding of a value inside a cell of width \Delta moves it by at most \Delta/2; clipping only moves values closer to the bounds.

Second: by induction on depth. Let \varepsilon_\ell be the perturbation at the output of layer \ell, and assume (standard assumption) that quantizing the weights of layer \ell shifts its output by at most \Delta_\ell/2.

Layer \ell+1, Lipschitz, propagates \varepsilon_\ell to \mathrm{Lip}_{\ell+1}\,\varepsilon_\ell, then its own quantization adds at most \Delta_{\ell+1}/2: \varepsilon_{\ell+1} \leq \mathrm{Lip}_{\ell+1}\,\varepsilon_\ell + \Delta_{\ell+1}/2. Unrolling from \varepsilon_1 = \Delta_1/2, each \Delta_\ell/2 is multiplied by the Lipschitz constants of the subsequent layers, giving the announced sum (partial products \prod_{m>\ell}\mathrm{Lip}_m). ∎

Definition 3.4 — Magnitude pruning Given trained weights W and a target sparsity \rho \in (0,1), magnitude pruning sets to zero the fraction \rho of weights with the smallest absolute values |W_{ij}|, then usually fine-tunes the remaining ones. It is unstructured when individual weights are removed, structured when the score is computed per channel, head or layer (for instance the norm of a whole filter) and whole blocks are removed. Only the structured version shrinks the dense matrices that ordinary hardware multiplies, which is why it is the one that delivers measured speedups.

Example 3.5 — Why bits matter more than FLOPs at inference When a language model generates text one token at a time with a small batch, every token requires reading all the weights from memory once, and the arithmetic units mostly wait for the data: generation is memory-bound.

A 7-billion-parameter model stored in 16-bit floats occupies 14 GB; with a memory bandwidth of 1 TB/s, at most 1000/14 \approx 71 tokens per second can be produced for one sequence. In int8 the weights occupy 7 GB (at most about 143 tokens/s), in int4 3.5 GB (about 286 tokens/s).

Energy follows the same logic: in Horowitz’s classic measurements (45 nm technology), reading 32 bits from DRAM costs about 640 pJ, while a 32-bit floating-point multiplication costs about 3.7 pJ — moving data costs two orders of magnitude more than computing on it. Halving the number of bits per weight therefore roughly halves the dominant energy term of generation, provided the kernels read the compressed weights directly.

Common mistakes with frugality claims

  1. Evaluating the compressed model on the benchmark used to calibrate it, instead of on the deployment distribution.
  2. Counting zeros as savings: 90% unstructured sparsity gives no speedup on hardware that multiplies dense matrices.
  3. Reporting the energy of the student’s inference while forgetting the teacher’s training and the distillation run itself; the saving is real only once the number of queries served pays back these one-off costs (ch. 6 gives the break-even computation).

4. Sustainable decision support: linear and mixed integer programming

The core business of operations research: turning a complex decision into a mathematical model that a solver drives to the optimum — not “nearly”. This is the toolbox of the module’s instructor (ch. 8) and the language of the case studies (ch. 5).

Definition 4.1 — Standard form and modeling (recipe) General ILP: \min_{x}\; c^\top x \quad \text{subject to}\quad Ax \leq b,\; x_i \in \mathbb{Z} \;\;(i \in I),\; x \geq 0. Modeling recipe (to know by heart):

  1. index: what do we decide on? (task × hour, site × capacity);
  2. variables: continuous (flows, powers) or binary (yes/no decisions, activation);
  3. constraints: physical (capacity, conservation), business (deadline, regulatory);
  4. objective: cost, carbon, or both (bi-objective, ch. 1).

Three writing reflexes: “at least one of” = \sum x_j \geq 1; “if-then” = big-M; “fixed activation cost” = z \in \{0,1\}, x \leq M z.

Proposition 4.2 — Continuous relaxation: a bound and the integrality gap (proved on an example) The relaxation (which drops x_i \in \mathbb{Z}) is an LP whose feasible set contains every integer solution. For a minimization problem its optimum \rho^\star therefore satisfies \rho^\star \leq v^\star, where v^\star is the integer optimum (immediate proof by inclusion of sets); for a maximization problem, such as the knapsack below, the inequality is reversed: \rho^\star \geq v^\star. The gap |v^\star - \rho^\star| is the integrality gap.

Example (fractional vs integer knapsack, to be maximized): capacity 10, items (v_i, w_i) \in \{(10,7), (6,5), (5,4)\}:

Proof. Relaxation. By decreasing value/weight ratio: \frac{10}{7} > \frac{5}{4} > \frac{6}{5}. Take item 1 whole (7 units), leaving 3 units of capacity for item 3 (next ratio): \rho^\star = 10 + \frac{3}{4}\cdot 5 = 13.75.

ILP. The integer solutions: \{1\} (10, weight 7), \{2,3\} (11, weight 9), \{1\} + nothing else fits — v^\star = 11.

Integrality gap 13.75 - 11 = 2.75, i.e. 25%: branch and bound (def. 4.3) will have to close it while proving that no integer choice exceeds 11. ∎

Definition/Theorem 4.3 — Branch and bound: correctness (proved) Algorithm. (written for a minimization problem)

  1. Solve the relaxation of the current problem;
  2. if the solution is integral, it is a candidate (upper bound);
  3. otherwise choose a fractional variable x_i and create two subproblems x_i \leq \lfloor \bar x_i\rfloor, x_i \geq \lceil \bar x_i\rceil (branch);
  4. prune a subproblem if its relaxation is infeasible, already solved, or has bound \rho^\star \geq the current upper bound (bound).

Correctness.

Proof. Each branching partitions the set of integer solutions (the two children cover everything, without overlap); subproblems pruned “by bound” cannot contain an optimum: their relaxation lower-bounds all their integer solutions (prop. 4.2), so none improves the current upper bound; the other cases prune empty sets.

At termination, the tree has covered the whole space except regions proven non-optimal: the best integer candidate found is the global optimum. (Termination is guaranteed in a finite number of branchings, the variables being bounded by the constraints.) ∎

In practice: solvers, formulations, difficulty The quality of an ILP formulation is judged by the quality of its relaxation: two formulations exactly equivalent in integer solutions can differ by a factor of 1000 in solving time (this is the modeler’s whole art — see Wolsey, references). Open-source solvers: CBC, HiGHS; commercial: Gurobi, CPLEX. The module does not ask you to run them by hand: exercise 3 walks through a branch and bound on a 4-item knapsack, then has it checked by PuLP:

Example 4.4 — Reading shadow prices on a two-variable LP A lab runs two kinds of jobs:

  • a job of type 1 brings a value 3, uses 1 GPU hour and 2 kWh;
  • a job of type 2 brings 2, uses 1 GPU hour and 1 kWh.

This week, 4 GPU hours and 6 kWh are available. The LP is \max\ 3x_1 + 2x_2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad x_1 + x_2 \leq 4,\ \ 2x_1 + x_2 \leq 6,\ \ x \geq 0. The vertices of the feasible polygon are (0,0), (3,0), (2,2) and (0,4), with values 0, 9, 10, 8. The optimum is x^\star = (2,2), \quad \text{value } 10, and both constraints are active.

The dual is \min\ 4y_1 + 6y_2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_1 + 2y_2 \geq 3,\ \ y_1 + y_2 \geq 2,\ \ y \geq 0. Complementary slackness (both x_j^\star > 0) makes both dual constraints active, so y^\star = (1, 1), \quad \text{of value } 4 + 6 = 10, equal to the primal value as prop. 0.1 requires.

Interpretation: one more GPU hour is worth 1, one more kWh is worth 1.

Check on the energy side: with 7 kWh, the active constraints x_1 + x_2 = 4, 2x_1 + x_2 = 7 give x = (3, 1) and value 11, exactly one unit more. If someone proposes to buy an extra kWh of certified renewable energy at a price above 1 unit of value, the model says no — for this week’s portfolio of jobs.

5. Case studies: data centers and electric vehicles

This chapter applies the LP and ILP toolbox of ch. 4 to two deployments where carbon, cost and physical capacity are traded off for real. In both cases the model fits on one page, and the dual variables carry the economic message.

Case study 5.1 — Data center powered by renewable energies Data. A compute site: flexible load (deferrable jobs: training runs, indexing, simulations) and rigid load (interactive services). Local energies: solar s_t (daytime slots), wind w_t, grid complement g_t \geq 0 at intensity c_t.

Model (sliding-window LP). \min \sum_t c_t\, g_t\,\Delta t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \begin{cases} g_t + s_t + w_t \;\geq\; \mathrm{PUE}\cdot u_t & \text{(power balance)}\\ \sum_{t \in \text{day}} u_t \;\geq\; D & \text{(total deferred work)}\\ u_t \leq U^{\max}, \quad u_t = U^{\text{rigid}}_t + f_t,\; f_t \geq 0 &\end{cases} where f_t is the power allocated to flexible work.

Dual reading. The multiplier of the total-work constraint is the marginal emission of one more kWh of flexible work, in gCO2eq per kWh. It is zero as long as some hour with spare capacity has surplus solar or wind power; otherwise it equals \mathrm{PUE}\cdot c_t for the cleanest hour that still has spare capacity, and it rises sharply when only the evening peak is left.

This is a dynamic price signal derived from the model, and the same mechanism as the industrial deployment described by Radovanovic et al. (references): planning ahead with forecasts of s_t, w_t, c_t (forecasts to which the Machine Learning module, ch. 5–6, applies directly).

Case study 5.2 — Coordinated charging of an electric vehicle fleet Data. N vehicles, each plugged in from a_i to d_i, need E_i (kWh), max power p_i.

Grid: hourly intensity c_t, tariff p_t^\text{elec}, local transformer capacity K (the real “bottleneck” of company parking lots: without coordination, everyone plugs in at 6 pm).

Model. \min_{x_{i,t} \geq 0}\; \sum_{i,t}\; c_t\, x_{i,t} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \begin{cases} \sum_{a_i \leq t < d_i} x_{i,t}\,\Delta t \;\geq\; E_i & \forall i \;\;\text{(energy need)}\\ 0 \leq x_{i,t} \leq p_i & \forall (i,t) \\ \sum_i x_{i,t} \;\leq\; K & \forall t \;\;\text{(transformer capacity)}\end{cases} Pure LP (continuous): solved to the optimum in seconds for thousands of vehicles. The dual delivers the usage prices of the transformer hour by hour — enough to price the incentive rather than forbid.

ILP variant: fast charging available when needed (z_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}, x \leq M z, fixed cost \kappa z); stochastic variant: unexpected departures (scenarios, recourse); robust variant (ch. 8: uncertainty on c_t).

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

# 3 vehicles, 8 hourly slots from 18h to 2h, transformer capacity 11 kW
T, K = 8, 11.0
c = np.array([300, 380, 320, 140, 60, 55, 52, 58])    # gCO2/kWh per slot
veh = [ (0, 8, 20.0, 7.0), (0, 8, 28.0, 7.0), (2, 8, 15.0, 3.5) ]  # (a, d, E, p): slots a <= t < d
n = len(veh) * T                                      # variable x[i, t] = idx i*T + t
cost = np.array([c[idx % T] for idx in range(n)])     # minimize sum c_t x_it

A_ub, b_ub = [], []                                   # capacity per slot
for t in range(T):
    row = [1.0 if idx % T == t else 0.0 for idx in range(n)]
    A_ub.append(row); b_ub.append(K)
A_eq, b_eq = [], []                                   # energy need
for i, (a, d, E, p) in enumerate(veh):
    row = [1.0 if (idx // T == i and a <= idx % T < d) else 0.0 for idx in range(n)]
    A_eq.append(row); b_eq.append(E)
bounds = [(0, p) for (a, d, E, p) in veh for _ in range(T)]
res = linprog(cost, A_ub=np.array(A_ub), b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=np.array(A_eq),
              b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
x = res.x.reshape(len(veh), T)
print("optimal emissions (kg CO2eq):", round(res.fun / 1000, 2))
print(np.round(x, 1))   # the cars wait for the low-carbon slots
print(res.ineqlin.marginals)   # transformer prices (negative: scipy's sign convention)

Example 5.3 — What the solver says about the transformer The program above returns 6.42 kg CO2eq. The four night slots (52–60 g/kWh) are saturated at 11 kW, as is the 21h slot (140 g/kWh); the remaining 8 kWh go to the 18h slot (300 g/kWh), the cleanest of the evening ones.

Removing the transformer limit (K very large) lets each vehicle charge only at night, for 3.60 kg: the capacity of the transformer costs 2.8 kg per evening.

The dual variables say the same thing hour by hour: the multiplier of the capacity constraint is 300 - c_t for each saturated slot — from 160 g/kWh at 21h to 248 g/kWh at 0h — because one more kW at that hour replaces one kWh charged at 300 g. These are the numbers to put in front of the facility manager: they price an upgrade of the transformer, or an incentive to leave the car plugged in longer, in kg of CO2 avoided.

What these cases teach (meta-lesson)

  1. Structure matters more than size: these models with thousands of variables solve in seconds because they are linear; a single non-linear term can change everything.
  2. The dual is a product: dynamic prices, incentive signals, marginal value of carbon.
  3. The hard part is not the optimizer but the data (forecasts, measurements) and governance (who accepts that their task waits?) — optimization is the art of making these trade-offs explicit.

6. Measuring the footprint: energy, PUE, carbon accounting

Every optimization of the previous chapters minimizes a number — kWh, gCO2eq — that someone must first measure. This chapter turns that number into a protocol: what is measured, at which boundary, with which conversion factors and with what uncertainty, so that two claims can be compared and audited (ch. 7).

Definition 6.1 — Energy of a job, measurement boundary and PUE The energy of a job is the integral of the power drawn while it runs, E_{\mathrm{IT}} = \int P(t)\,dt, in kWh. Its value depends on the boundary of the measurement: software counters read the power of one component — NVML for the GPU board, RAPL for the CPU package and the DRAM — while a meter at the wall plug sees the whole server, fans and power supply included.

The building adds cooling, power conversion losses and lighting, summarized by the power usage effectiveness \mathrm{PUE} = \frac{\text{total energy of the facility}}{\text{energy of the IT equipment}} \;\geq\; 1, so that the energy attributable to the job is E = \mathrm{PUE}\cdot E_{\mathrm{IT}}. Values around 1.1 are reported for the most efficient hyperscale sites; industry surveys give averages around 1.5.

Definition 6.2 — Operational and embodied emissions The operational emissions of a job are C_{\mathrm{op}} = E \cdot \mathrm{CI}, where \mathrm{CI} is a carbon intensity in gCO2eq/kWh (def. 2.1). The intensity can be location-based (the physical mix of the grid where and when the job ran) or market-based (the contractual factor of the electricity purchased, certificates included); the GHG Protocol asks companies to report both, and only the first one describes the physics of the grid (ch. 7, pitfall 3).

The embodied emissions come from manufacturing, transporting and disposing of the hardware; a job that occupies a machine for a duration T_{\mathrm{job}} out of a service life T_{\mathrm{life}} is charged the share C_{\mathrm{emb}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{job}}}{T_{\mathrm{life}}}\; C_{\mathrm{manuf}}. The footprint reported for the job is C = C_{\mathrm{op}} + C_{\mathrm{emb}}.

Example 6.3 — The footprint of a three-day fine-tuning, step by step A fine-tuning runs for 72 h on a server with 8 GPUs.

  1. GPU counters. NVML reports an average of 300 W per GPU, i.e. 8 \times 0.3 \times 72 = 172.8 kWh.
  2. Wall meter. A meter at the wall reads an average of 3.4 kW for the whole server, i.e. E_{\mathrm{IT}} = 3.4 \times 72 = 244.8 kWh: the GPU-only figure misses 29\% of the server’s consumption.
  3. Facility. With a PUE of 1.2, E = 293.8 kWh.
  4. Operational emissions. On a low-carbon grid at 55 g/kWh, C_{\mathrm{op}} \approx 16.2 kg; on a grid dominated by gas and coal at 400 g/kWh, C_{\mathrm{op}} \approx 117.5 kg.
  5. Embodied share. Assume C_{\mathrm{manuf}} = 3000 kgCO2eq for the server and a service life of 4 years (35\,040 h) — published estimates of embodied footprints vary by a factor of several, so this is an assumption to be stated, not a datum. Then C_{\mathrm{emb}} = \frac{72}{35\,040} \times 3000 \approx 6.2 \text{ kg}.

The lesson is in the ratio: the embodied share is about 28\% of the total on the low-carbon grid and about 5\% on the fossil one. The cleaner the electricity, the more the manufacturing of the hardware matters — and the less a “we use green power” argument settles the question.

Proposition 6.4 — Relative uncertainties of a product add in quadrature (proved to first order) Let C = X_1 X_2 \cdots X_n be a product of independent positive factors, each with a small relative standard deviation r_i = \sigma_i / X_i. Then, to first order, the relative standard deviation of C is

Key result — relative uncertainties add in quadrature r_C \;\approx\; \sqrt{r_1^2 + \cdots + r_n^2}.

The worst-case relative error is at most \prod_i (1 + r_i) - 1 \approx r_1 + \cdots + r_n when each factor is off by at most r_i.

Proof. Take logarithms: \log C = \sum_i \log X_i. A small perturbation \delta X_i changes \log X_i by \delta X_i / X_i to first order, so \delta C / C \approx \sum_i \delta X_i / X_i . The variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of the variances, which gives r_C^2 \approx \sum_i r_i^2. For the worst case, \prod_i (1+r_i) - 1 is the largest relative increase of the product when each factor increases by its maximal relative error, and its expansion to first order is \sum_i r_i. ∎

Example 6.5 — An honest error bar on example 6.3 Take a relative uncertainty of 10\% on E_{\mathrm{IT}} (software counters and averaging), 5\% on the PUE and 30\% on the carbon intensity (an annual average used for a job whose hourly intensity is unknown). Proposition 6.4 gives r_C \approx \sqrt{0.01 + 0.0025 + 0.09} \approx 0.32 . The operational footprint on the fossil grid should therefore be published as about 117 kg \pm 32\%, i.e. a one-standard-deviation range of roughly 80 to 155 kg — and not as “117.5 kg”.

The simulation below gives a 90% interval of about 70 to 200 kg. The quadrature sum also says where to invest effort: the intensity term dominates, so obtaining the hourly intensity of the region (Electricity Maps, grid operators) improves the estimate far more than a better power meter would.

Definition 6.6 — Training versus inference: the break-even time A model trained once with energy E_{\mathrm{train}} then serves N queries per day at an energy e_q per query. Inference overtakes training after D^\star = \frac{E_{\mathrm{train}}}{N\, e_q} \text{ days}.

Patterson et al. (2021) estimate the training of GPT-3 at about 1\,287 MWh. Under the assumption of 0.3 Wh per query:

  • 10 million queries a day consume 3 MWh a day, and inference overtakes training after about 430 days;
  • at 100 million queries a day, after about 43 days.

The same authors report that at Google, inference accounted for about three fifths of the energy spent on machine learning in 2019–2021 (Patterson et al., 2022). A footprint that counts only training therefore describes the beginning of the life of a widely used model, not its life.

The protocol sheet — what any published figure must state

  1. The task and the quality reached.
  2. The hardware: model and number of accelerators, and the server.
  3. The measurement method and its boundary (NVML, RAPL, wall meter) and the sampling period.
  4. The duration and the energy E_{\mathrm{IT}}, including the failed runs and the hyperparameter search, which Strubell et al. (2019) showed can multiply the cost of the final run many times over.
  5. The PUE and its source.
  6. The carbon intensity: location- or market-based, region, temporal resolution (hourly or annual), source.
  7. The embodied share and the assumptions behind it.
  8. An uncertainty range (prop. 6.4).
  9. The code and configuration needed to redo the measurement.

Tools such as CodeCarbon or the experiment-impact-tracker of Henderson et al. (2020) automate items 3 to 6 by reading the counters and a regional intensity table; when the counters are not accessible they fall back to estimates based on the nominal power of the processor, which must then be stated as such.

Common mistakes in measurement

  1. Multiplying the nominal maximal power (TDP) of a GPU by the duration: a GPU rarely runs at its TDP for a whole job, so the result overestimates, sometimes by a factor of two.
  2. Confusing kW (a power) and kWh (an energy): “the cluster consumes 40 kWh” says nothing without a duration.
  3. Measuring the GPU only and calling it the job’s energy (example 6.3: 29\% missing).
  4. Comparing two models measured with different boundaries or different intensity sources.
  5. Forgetting idle time: a reserved but idle node still draws a large fraction of its peak power, and that energy belongs to the project that reserved it.

7. Limits, rebound effect and greenwashing

The previous chapters optimize and measure; this one asks what those numbers can and cannot prove. It lists the classic pitfalls of carbon accounting, models the rebound effect that can cancel an efficiency gain, and ends with an audit grid for “green AI” claims.

Honesty note — the five pitfalls of carbon accounting

  1. Average vs marginal. Adding up kWh at a country’s average intensity underestimates the real effect: new consumption calls, at peak time, on marginal generation means (often gas: 400–600 g/kWh). The “average or marginal?” debate changes conclusions by a factor of 2 to 5 — honesty requires testing both.
  2. Chosen scope. Counting training but not inference (which, at the scale of millions of users, often dominates); forgetting hardware manufacturing (depreciation: up to a third of the footprint depending on the study); forgetting storage and network.
  3. Offsets and RECs. Buying green certificates does not change the physics of the grid: nighttime computing in a coal region emits coal, even covered with certificates. Claiming “net zero” on that basis is documented greenwashing.
  4. Rebound effect (Jevons). Making AI four times cheaper in energy often increases… total AI consumption: new uses (and training loops) absorb the gain. Efficiency is necessary, not sufficient: only a cap constraint (an absolute carbon budget, ch. 1: \varepsilon-constraints) turns it into a guarantee. Proposition 7.1 below makes this quantitative.
  5. False precision. “273.4 kg” when the actual hourly intensity is unknown to ±30%: publish ranges and assumptions, not magic numbers.

Proposition 7.1 — Rebound in a constant-elasticity model (proved) A service (queries answered, images generated) is consumed in quantity Q, which responds to its unit cost p with a constant price elasticity \varepsilon > 0: Q = A\,p^{-\varepsilon}. An efficiency gain divides the energy per unit of service by k > 1, from e_0 to e_0/k, and the unit cost is proportional to that energy, p = p_0/k. Then the total energy E = Q\,e goes from E_0 to

Key result — rebound in a constant-elasticity model E = E_0\, k^{\varepsilon - 1}.

Hence:

  • if \varepsilon < 1, energy decreases, but by less than the factor k (partial rebound);
  • if \varepsilon = 1, it does not change at all (full rebound);
  • if \varepsilon > 1, it increases (backfire — the Jevons paradox).

The rebound fraction, the share of the expected saving 1 - 1/k that is lost, is R = 1 - \frac{1 - k^{\varepsilon-1}}{1 - 1/k}.

Proof. Before the gain, E_0 = A\,p_0^{-\varepsilon}\,e_0. After it, E = A\,(p_0/k)^{-\varepsilon}\,(e_0/k) = A\,p_0^{-\varepsilon} e_0 \cdot k^{\varepsilon}\cdot k^{-1} = E_0\,k^{\varepsilon - 1}. The sign of \varepsilon - 1 decides whether k^{\varepsilon-1} is below, equal to or above 1. The realized saving is 1 - E/E_0 = 1 - k^{\varepsilon - 1}, and R is one minus the ratio of the realized saving to the expected one. ∎

Example 7.2 — The same efficiency gain, three outcomes An inference stack becomes k = 4 times more efficient.

  • With \varepsilon = 0.5, total energy becomes 4^{-0.5} = 0.5 times the initial one: the expected saving was 75\%, the realized one is 50\%, so a third of the gain is absorbed (R = 1/3).
  • With \varepsilon = 1, nothing is saved.
  • With \varepsilon = 1.2, total energy is multiplied by 4^{0.2} \approx 1.32: the “green” optimization increases consumption by about a third.

Two cautions keep the model honest. First, the elasticity of AI services is unknown, is not constant, and varies between uses; the proposition is a way to reason, not a forecast. Second, energy is only part of the unit cost (hardware, staff and margins count too): if it is a fraction \varphi of the cost, the price only falls to p_0(1 - \varphi + \varphi/k) and the rebound is weaker — exercise 9 quantifies this.

Proposition 7.3 — Audit grid for a “green AI” claim

Audit question Red flag
What scope? “our model” without inference, without manufacturing, without data
What intensity, what source, what time? annual national average for a localized data center; “contractual” intensity
Compared to what? “−80%” without reference or protocol (vs what? at equal accuracy?)
Reproducible figures? no code, no config, no hardware specified
Measured real benefit? carbon gain “enabled” by the AI not quantified (this is the hardest — and the most important)

The final question — “does AI reduce more emissions than it causes?” — is legitimate, open, and beyond the reach of a simple measurement: it requires a full life-cycle model. The module provides the tools to pose it honestly, not to answer it with a slogan.

Professional positioning The “responsible AI” engineer is neither an activist nor an accountant: they are someone who knows how to (i) model a trade-off (ch. 1), (ii) optimize under resources (ch. 2, 4, 5), (iii) reduce the cost of models (ch. 3), (iv) measure with a protocol (ch. 6), (v) state the limits of their figures, rebound effects included (ch. 7). This complete chain — that is the module’s bet — is exactly what the “AI sustainability” teams of major players are looking for.

                  reduction="batchmean") * T*T
    return alpha*kd + (1-alpha)*F.cross_entropy(zs, y)
