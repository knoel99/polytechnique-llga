0. Review: optimization and concentration tools

L3 background used throughout the module
  • Gradient: \(\nabla f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) points in the direction of steepest ascent; for \(f(x)=\frac12\|Ax - b\|^2\), \(\nabla f(x) = A^\top(Ax - b)\).
  • Convexity: \(f\) is convex if \(f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \leq \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y)\). A local minimum of a convex function is global — the reason so many ML losses are designed convex.
  • Gradient descent: \(x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta \nabla f(x_t)\), convergence \(\mathcal{O}(1/t)\) for a step \(\eta < 1/L\) (\(L\) = Lipschitz constant of the gradient).
  • Symmetric matrices: diagonalizable (\(A = Q \Lambda Q^\top\), \(Q\) orthogonal); \(A \succeq 0\) (positive) iff all its eigenvalues are \(\geq 0\). For a symmetric matrix, \(\sup_{\|v\| \leq 1} v^\top A v = \lambda_{\max}(A)\) (Rayleigh quotient) — the hidden tool of PCA (chapter 6).

0.1. Why concentration inequalities in an ML course?

All of generalization theory (chapter 7) rests on one simple question: is the empirical risk \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f)\), computed on \(n\) examples, close to the true risk \(\mathcal{R}(f)\)? The answer is given by concentration inequalities — results of probability analysis that we use here as a black box, proved once and for all.

Proposition 0.1 — Markov's inequality Let \(Z \geq 0\) be an integrable random variable. For every \(t > 0\), $$\mathbb{P}(Z \geq t) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{t}.$$
Writing \(Z = Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z \geq t\} + Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z < t\}\) and noting that \(Z\,\mathbf{1}\{Z \geq t\} \geq t\,\mathbf{1}\{Z \geq t\}\), monotonicity of the expectation gives \(\mathbb{E}[Z] \geq t\,\mathbb{P}(Z \geq t)\).∎
Proposition 0.2 — Chebyshev's inequality Let \(X\) have finite variance. For every \(t > 0\), $$\mathbb{P}\big(|X - \mathbb{E}[X]| \geq t\big) \leq \frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{t^2}.$$
Apply Markov to \(Z = (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 \geq 0\) with threshold \(t^2\) and note that \(\{|X - \mathbb{E}[X]| \geq t\} = \{Z \geq t^2\}\).∎

Chebyshev gives a \(1/(nt^2)\) decay for a mean of \(n\) variables: usable but slow, and with no exponential control. The extra ingredient — independence — provides the exponential magic.

Theorem 0.3 — Hoeffding's inequality Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be independent with \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\) almost surely and \(S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\). For every \(t > 0\), $$\mathbb{P}\big(S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n] \geq t\big) \leq \exp\!\Big(-\,\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i - a_i)^2}\Big),$$ and the same bound holds for the deviation \(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{E}[S_n] - S_n \geq t)\). In particular, for losses in \([0,1]\): \(\mathbb{P}\big(|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) - \mathcal{R}(f)| \geq \varepsilon\big) \leq 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}\).
Sketch in three moves. (i) Hoeffding's lemma. For centered \(X \in [a,b]\), one bounds its generating function: by convexity of \(u \mapsto e^{su}\), the curve lies below the chord joining \((a, e^{sa})\) and \((b, e^{sb})\), hence \(e^{sX} \leq \frac{b - X}{b - a}e^{sa} + \frac{X - a}{b - a}e^{sb}\); taking expectations, \(\mathbb{E}[e^{sX}] \leq \frac{b}{b-a}e^{sa} - \frac{a}{b-a}e^{sb} = e^{\varphi(s)}\) with \(\varphi(0) = \varphi'(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi''(s) \leq (b-a)^2/4\), so \(\varphi(s) \leq s^2(b-a)^2/8\) (second-order Taylor remainder). (ii) Multiplication. For centered \(S_n\), independence gives \(\mathbb{E}[e^{sS_n}] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[e^{sX_i}] \leq \exp\big(s^2 \sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2/8\big)\). (iii) Chernoff. \(\mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq e^{-st}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{sS_n}] \leq \exp\big(-st + \tfrac{s^2}{8}\sum_i(b_i - a_i)^2\big)\) for all \(s > 0\); the right-hand side is minimal at \(s^\star = 4t/\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\), which gives \(\exp(-2t^2/\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2)\).∎
Why this is central in ML Hoeffding says: for a fixed \(f\), \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f)\) is exponentially close to \(\mathcal{R}(f)\). But the learner chooses \(\hat f_n\) while looking at the data — one must then control \(\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}}\) over the whole class: this is precisely the subject of chapter 7 (uniform convergence, VC dimension, Rademacher complexity). Without this supplement, nothing can be proved about overfitting.

1. The statistical framework of learning

Definition 1.1 — iid framework and risk One observes an iid sample \(D_n = (X_i, Y_i)_{i \leq n}\) with law \(\mathbb{P}\) on \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}\). For a loss \(\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_+\), the risk of a predictor \(f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}\) is $$\mathcal{R}(f) = \mathbb{E}\big[\ell(Y, f(X))\big],$$ and the (observable) empirical risk: \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(Y_i, f(X_i))\). The Bayes risk is \(\mathcal{R}^\star = \inf_f \mathcal{R}(f)\), the infimum over all measurable functions.
Definition 1.2 — Empirical risk minimization (ERM) Given a model class \(\mathcal{F}\), learning solves $$\hat f_n \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \ \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f).$$ All of ML consists in choosing \(\mathcal{F}\) (linear? tree-based? network?), \(\ell\) (quadratic, logistic, hinge...), and in controlling the gap \(\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{R}(f)\).

The excess risk decomposes into two contributions of different natures: $$\underbrace{\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \mathcal{R}^\star}_{\text{excess risk}} = \underbrace{\inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{R}(f) - \mathcal{R}^\star}_{\text{approximation error}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{R}(f)}_{\text{estimation error}}.$$ The first depends only on the choice of \(\mathcal{F}\) (modeling bias), the second on the sample and the size of \(\mathcal{F}\) (variance, chapter 7). Enlarging \(\mathcal{F}\) decreases the first and increases the second: the whole dilemma is there.

1.1. Bias-variance

Proposition 1.3 — Bias-variance decomposition (quadratic loss) Assume \(Y = f^\star(X) + \varepsilon\) with \(f^\star(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]\) and noise \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2\). For an estimator \(\hat f_n\) built on \(D_n\) independent of the test pair \((X, Y)\): $$\mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \hat f_n(X))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}} + \mathbb{E}\big[\underbrace{\text{bias}^2(\hat f_n)}_{\text{underfitting}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat f_n)}_{\text{overfitting}}\big].$$
Condition on \(X = x\) and on \(D_n\) (the total expectation is then taken over both). On the one hand \(\hat f_n(x)\) depends only on \(D_n\), and \(Y = f^\star(x) + \varepsilon\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x, D_n] = 0\); the cross term therefore vanishes: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \hat f_n(x))^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \mathbb{E}\big[(f^\star(x) - \hat f_n(x))^2\big].$$ On the other hand, for any variable \(Z\), \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = (\mathbb{E}Z)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(Z)\) applied to \(Z = f^\star(x) - \hat f_n(x)\) (the variance is over the randomness of \(D_n\)): $$\mathbb{E}\big[(f^\star(x) - \hat f_n(x))^2\big] = \big(f^\star(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f_n(x)]\big)^2 + \mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f_n(x)\big).$$ Finally integrate over \(x \sim \mathbb{P}_X\).∎

A class \(\mathcal{F}\) that is too small ⇒ high bias; too large ⇒ high variance. This decomposition is diagnostic: high training and test errors ⇒ underfitting (enlarge the model); low training and high test ⇒ overfitting (regularize, more data, chapters 2 and 7). No algorithm is universally better (the "no free lunch" theorem): prior knowledge about the data matters as much as the algorithm.

1.2. Cross-validation

The risk \(\mathcal{R}\) is unobservable: it is estimated by setting data aside. k-fold: split into \(k\) blocks, train \(k\) times while rotating the test block, average. Leave-one-out (\(k = n\)) has low bias but high estimation variance and costs \(n\) trainings; \(k = 5\) or \(10\) is the standard compromise. Program rules: never fit preprocessing on the test set; a final test set that is looked at only once; validation serves to choose hyperparameters and models (chapter 9 for the nested version).

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), Ridge(alpha=1.0))
scores = cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error")
print(-scores.mean(), scores.std())

2. Linear regression and regularization

2.1. Ordinary least squares

Definition 2.1 — Gaussian linear model One observes \(y = X\theta + \varepsilon\) where \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) is fixed (the design), \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p\), and one assumes: (H1) \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\); (H2) \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I_n\) (homoscedasticity); (H3) independent components; (H4, optional) \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n)\). The least squares estimator minimizes \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(\theta) = \tfrac1n \|y - X\theta\|^2\).
Proposition 2.2 — Normal equations If \(X^\top X\) is invertible, the unique minimizer is $$\hat\theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y.$$
Expand \(\|y - X\theta\|^2 = \theta^\top X^\top X\,\theta - 2\,\theta^\top X^\top y + \|y\|^2\). The gradient in \(\theta\) is \(2X^\top X \theta - 2X^\top y\) (symmetric matrix for the quadratic term); setting it to zero gives the normal equations \(X^\top X\theta = X^\top y\). The Hessian \(2X^\top X \succeq 0\) is positive definite as soon as \(\mathrm{rank}(X) = p\): the function is strictly convex, the stationary point is the unique global minimum.∎
Geometry: an orthogonal projection \(\hat y = X\hat\theta = P y\) with \(P = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\) the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathrm{Im}(X)\). One checks \(P^2 = P = P^\top\); the residual \(e = y - \hat y\) satisfies \(X^\top e = 0\), i.e. \(e \perp \mathrm{Im}(X)\). The \(h_{ii} = P_{ii}\) (the leverages) measure the influence of each observation, with \(\sum_i h_{ii} = p\).
Theorem 2.3 — Gauss-Markov Under (H1)-(H3), for every \(c \in \mathbb{R}^p\), the estimator \(c^\top\hat\theta\) is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) of \(c^\top\theta\): among the \(a^\top y\) with \(\mathbb{E}[a^\top y] = c^\top\theta\) for every \(\theta\), it has minimal variance.
A linear estimator \(a^\top y\) is unbiased for every \(\theta\) iff \(a^\top X = c^\top\). Set \(a_\star = X(X^\top X)^{-1}c \in \mathrm{Im}(X)\): \(a_\star^\top X = c^\top\), so \(a_\star\) is admissible, and \(\mathrm{Var}(a_\star^\top y) = \sigma^2 \|a_\star\|^2 = \sigma^2 c^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}c\), which is indeed the variance of \(c^\top\hat\theta\). Let \(a\) be any admissible vector and \(h = a - a_\star\); then \(h^\top X = 0\), so \(h \perp \mathrm{Im}(X) \ni a_\star\), hence $$\mathrm{Var}(a^\top y) = \sigma^2\|a\|^2 = \sigma^2\big(\|a_\star\|^2 + \|h\|^2\big) \geq \sigma^2\|a_\star\|^2 = \mathrm{Var}(c^\top\hat\theta),$$ with equality iff \(a = a_\star\).∎
Inference: confidence intervals (under H4) Under Gaussian noise, \(\hat\theta \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\theta,\ \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\big)\) (a linear variable of a Gaussian vector), and \(\hat\sigma^2 = \tfrac{1}{n-p}\|y - X\hat\theta\|^2\) is an unbiased estimator independent of \(\hat\theta\). Writing \(v_j = [(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{jj}\), the statistic \((\hat\theta_j - \theta_j)/(\hat\sigma\sqrt{v_j})\) follows a Student distribution with \(n - p\) degrees of freedom, giving the 95% confidence interval: $$\hat\theta_j \pm t_{0.975}^{\,n-p}\; \hat\sigma \sqrt{v_j}.$$ (Reminder on usual distributions: cf. Refresher Statistics module.) The (estimator, standard deviation) pair reported by any serious regressor is read here: a coefficient whose interval contains \(0\) is not "significant".
Residual analysis — the model's quality control The assumptions (H1)-(H4) are checked graphically on the residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\) (standardized \(r_i = e_i/\big(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}}\big)\)): residuals vs fitted values (a curvature betrays a missing nonlinearity; a funnel, heteroscedasticity — think of transforming \(y\) or weighting); Gaussian quantile-quantile plot (departure of the noise from normality); spotting high-leverage points \(h_{ii} \gg 2p/n\) that "pull" the line.

2.2. Regularization: ridge and lasso

Definition 2.4 — Ridge and lasso $$\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_\theta \Big\{\tfrac1n\|y - X\theta\|^2 + \lambda \|\theta\|_2^2\Big\} = (X^\top X + n\lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y,$$ $$\hat\theta_{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_\theta \Big\{\tfrac1n\|y - X\theta\|^2 + \lambda \|\theta\|_1\Big\}.$$ Ridge shrinks all coefficients (a solution always exists); the lasso produces coefficients that are exactly zero (variable selection).
Proposition 2.5 — Ridge: spectral effect Write the SVD \(X = U\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_j)\,V^\top\). Then $$\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_j \frac{\sigma_j\, u_j^\top y}{\sigma_j^2 + n\lambda}\; v_j, \qquad\text{whereas}\qquad \hat\theta = \sum_j \frac{u_j^\top y}{\sigma_j}\;v_j.$$ Ridge damps each direction \(v_j\) by the factor \(\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2 + n\lambda)\): the well-explained directions (large \(\sigma_j\)) pass through, the ill-conditioned directions (collinearities, \(\sigma_j \to 0\)) are crushed — exactly the ones that made the least squares variance explode. In particular \(\|\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}}\|\) decreases from \(\|\hat\theta\)\) toward \(0\) as \(\lambda\) grows.
Proposition 2.6 — Lasso in orthogonal dimension 1: soft thresholding If \(X^\top X = I_p\) (orthonormal columns) and \(z = X^\top y\), then $$\hat\theta_{\text{lasso},j} = \mathrm{sign}(z_j)\,\big(|z_j| - \lambda/2\big)_+,$$
The functional separates: \(\sum_j \big\{\tfrac12(z_j - \theta_j)^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}|\theta_j|\big\}\). For \(z_j > 0\), the optimum is either \(\theta_j = 0\) (cost \(z_j^2/2\)), or a stationary point in the region \(\theta_j > 0\) where the norm-1 term is differentiable: \(z_j - \theta_j = \lambda/2\), of cost \(\lambda z_j/2 - \lambda^2/8 < z_j^2/2\) as soon as \(z_j > \lambda/2\). Hence the thresholding; the case \(z_j < 0\) is symmetric.∎
It is this "dead zone" around zero that sets coefficients exactly to zero: in arbitrary dimension, the path \(\lambda \mapsto \hat\theta_{\text{lasso}}(\lambda)\) is piecewise linear (the LARS algorithm of Efron et al., 2004), which lasso_path visualizes.
Geometry of regularization Constraint \(\|\theta\|_1 \leq t\) = diamond: the corners on the axes explain the sparsity; \(\|\theta\|_2 \leq t\) = ball: isotropic shrinkage. Regularization is the first weapon against variance — and the ancestor of the weight decay of neural networks (\(\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2\) added to the loss in the Deep Learning module).
Proposition 2.7 — Bayesian reading: ridge = Gaussian prior, lasso = Laplacian prior Assume \(y = X\theta + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\). (i) If \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)\), the MAP (maximum a posteriori) is exactly \(\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}}\) with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). (ii) If the coordinates of \(\theta\) are iid with Laplacian density \(p(\theta_j) = \tfrac{1}{2b}e^{-|\theta_j|/b}\), the MAP is \(\hat\theta_{\text{lasso}}\) with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/b\).
By Bayes' formula, \(-\log p(\theta \mid y) = \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|y - X\theta\|^2 - \sum_j \log p(\theta_j) + \text{const.}\) (i) \(-\log p(\theta_j) = \theta_j^2/(2\tau^2) + \text{const.}\): minimizing amounts to minimizing \(\|y - X\theta\|^2 + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\|\theta\|_2^2\). (ii) \(-\log p(\theta_j) = |\theta_j|/b + \text{const.}\): minimize \(\|y - X\theta\|^2 + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{b}\|\theta\|_1\). In both cases, renormalizing by \(1/n\) and \(2\) identifies the announced \(\lambda\).∎
Moral: regularizing = encoding a prior (small / sparse coefficients); the Laplacian prior, peaked at zero, "pushes" coefficients toward zero more strongly than the Gaussian.

3. Classification: logistic regression and SVM

3.1. Logistic regression

Definition 3.1 For \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\), one directly models $$\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x) = \sigma(\langle \theta, x\rangle) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\langle \theta, x\rangle}},$$ where \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid. The log-odds is linear: \(\log \tfrac{p}{1-p} = \langle\theta, x\rangle\) — hence an interpretable reading of the coefficients (odds ratio \(\approx e^{\theta_j}\) for \(x_j\) increased by one unit).
Proposition 3.2 — Likelihood and cross-entropy Maximizing the likelihood \(\prod_i \sigma(\theta^\top x_i)^{y_i}(1 - \sigma(\theta^\top x_i))^{1 - y_i}\) is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy $$\hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta \ \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \log\!\big(1 + e^{-y'_i \langle\theta, x_i\rangle}\big), \qquad y'_i \in \{-1, +1\}.$$
\(-\log\)-likelihood = \(\sum_i -y_i\log p_i - (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\) with \(p_i = \sigma(\theta^\top x_i)\). Writing \(y'_i = 2y_i - 1\), one checks \(-y_i \log p_i - (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) = \log(1 + e^{-y'_i \theta^\top x_i})\): for \(y_i = 1\), \(1 - p_i = \sigma(-\theta^\top x_i)\) and \(1/(1-p_i) = 1 + e^{\theta^\top x_i}\).∎
Theorem 3.3 — Convexity, gradient, Hessian Set \(p_i(\theta) = \sigma(\theta^\top x_i)\) and \(L(\theta) = \tfrac1n\sum_i \log(1 + e^{-y'_i\theta^\top x_i})\). Then: (i) \(L\) is convex, and strictly convex if \(\ker X = \{0\}\); (ii) \(\nabla L(\theta) = \tfrac1n X^\top(p(\theta) - y)\); (iii) \(\nabla^2 L(\theta) = \tfrac1n X^\top \mathrm{diag}\big(w_i(\theta)\big) X\) with \(w_i = p_i(1 - p_i) \in \,]0, 1/4]\).
(i) Let \(g(z) = \log(1 + e^{-z})\). Then \(g'(z) = \tfrac{-e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = -\sigma(-z)\) and $$g''(z) = \sigma(-z)\big(1 - \sigma(-z)\big) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} > 0,$$ (the rate of change of \(\sigma'\) — or direct differentiation of \(\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)\)). Hence \(g\) is convex; each \(g(\pm\theta^\top x_i)\) is convex in \(\theta\) (composition with an affine map), and \(L\), a sum, is convex. (ii) \(\nabla_\theta\, g(-y'_i\theta^\top x_i) = -y'_i x_i\, \sigma(y'_i \theta^\top x_i)\) (chain rule with \(g'\) above); noting that \(y'_i \sigma(y'_i\theta^\top x_i) \in \{p_i,\ p_i - 1\}\) depending on \(y_i\), everything compacts into \(\tfrac1n\sum_i x_i(p_i - y_i)\). (iii) The Jacobian of \(p(\theta)\) is \(\mathrm{diag}(w_i)X\) (since \(\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)\)), giving the announced Hessian, which is a positive scalar times \(X^\top X \succeq 0\); it is positive definite if the columns of \(X\) are independent.∎
Practical consequences Convex + smooth ⇒ a unique global optimum, reached by gradient descent or Newton. The Newton method here reads \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top(y - p)\): this is exactly the iteratively reweighted regression (IRLS) well known from GLMs. This is the classification loss of neural networks (Deep Learning module) and of LLMs (softmax output = multiclass generalization \(\log\sum_k e^{s_k} - s_{y}\)).

3.2. Margin SVM

Definition 3.4 — SVM (soft margin, primal) $$\min_{\theta, b, \xi} \ \tfrac12 \|\theta\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y'_i(\langle\theta, x_i\rangle + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i, \quad \xi_i \geq 0.$$ Maximize the margin \(\gamma = 1/\|\theta\|\) while tolerating the violations \(\xi_i\) (soft constraints, \(C\) = unit penalty). The classifier is \(x \mapsto \mathrm{sign}(\langle\theta, x\rangle + b)\).
Theorem 3.5 — Dual of the SVM The primal above has the same solution as the dual problem $$\max_{\alpha} \ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac12 \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i\alpha_j y'_i y'_j \langle x_i, x_j\rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C, \ \sum_i \alpha_i y'_i = 0,$$ and the primal solution is reconstructed via \(\theta = \sum_i \alpha_i y'_i x_i\).
Lagrangian. Associate a multiplier \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) with each margin constraint and \(\mu_i \geq 0\) with \(\xi_i \geq 0\): $$\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\|\theta\|^2 + C\textstyle\sum_i \xi_i - \sum_i \alpha_i\big[y'_i(\theta^\top x_i + b) - 1 + \xi_i\big] - \sum_i \mu_i \xi_i.$$ Stationary conditions (KKT). \(\partial_\theta \mathcal{L} = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = \sum_i \alpha_i y'_i x_i\) (the predictor is a combination of the data!); \(\partial_b \mathcal{L} = 0 \Rightarrow \sum_i \alpha_i y'_i = 0\); \(\partial_{\xi_i} \mathcal{L} = 0 \Rightarrow C = \alpha_i + \mu_i\), i.e. \(\alpha_i \leq C\). Substitute back: the term \(\theta^\top\theta\) against \(-\sum\alpha_i y'_i\theta^\top x_i\) gives \(-\tfrac12\|\theta\|^2\), then replacing \(\|\theta\|^2\) by the double sum; the \(\xi_i\) disappear exactly (\((C - \alpha_i - \mu_i)\xi_i = 0\)); what remains is the announced dual function with \(0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C\). The primal is convex with affine constraints: Slater (strictly feasible, \(C\) large enough or \(\xi\) large enough) guarantees strong duality — dual optimum = primal optimum, and the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient.∎
Reading the complementarity conditions — who are the support vectors? The KKT complementarity conditions \(\alpha_i\,[y'_i(\theta^\top x_i + b) - 1 + \xi_i] = 0\) and \((C - \alpha_i)\,\xi_i = 0\) sort the points into three families:
CasePosition of the pointRole
\(\alpha_i = 0\)outside the margin band, well classifiednone — any data point
\(0 < \alpha_i < C\)exactly on the margin \((\xi_i = 0)\)"free" support vector
\(\alpha_i = C\)inside the band or misclassified \((\xi_i > 0)\)"paying" support vector
The solution depends only on the points with \(\alpha_i > 0\) (dual sparsity): this is the robustness of SVMs to distant data, and the \(\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{supp}})\) prediction cost. The constant \(b\) is recovered from any point on the margin: \(b = y'_i - \theta^\top x_i\).
Theorem 3.6 — Margin generalization bounds (sketch) Let \(\mathcal{F}_R = \{x \mapsto \langle\theta, x\rangle : \|\theta\| \leq R,\ \|x\| \leq 1\}\). Its Rademacher complexity (definition in chapter 7) satisfies \(\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}_R) \leq R/\sqrt{n}\), independently of the dimension of \(\mathcal{X}\). It follows, with high probability, for any score function with margin \(\gamma\) (losses dominated by the hinge): $$\mathcal{R}(f) \ \leq\ \hat{\mathcal{R}}_{n,\gamma}(f) + \frac{2R}{\gamma\sqrt{n}} + \sqrt{\tfrac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.$$ Sketch. (i) The hinge \((1 - t)_+\) is 1-Lipschitz: the contraction lemma brings the loss back to the score. (ii) For the class of bounded scores, \(\mathbb{E}_\sigma \sup_{\|\theta\| \leq R} \tfrac1n\theta^\top\sum_i \sigma_i x_i = \tfrac{R}{n}\,\mathbb{E}_\sigma\big\|\sum_i \sigma_i x_i\big\| \leq \tfrac{R}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big\|\sum_i\sigma_ix_i\big\|^2} = \tfrac{R}{n}\sqrt{n} = \tfrac{R}{\sqrt n}\) (Cauchy-Schwarz, then orthogonality of the \(\sigma_ix_i\) and \(\|x_i\| \leq 1\)).∎ This "margin, not dimension" viewpoint watches over overfitting; it is developed in chapter 7 and in the Statistical Learning Theory module (P1 option).

4. Kernels and RKHS

Definition 4.1 — Positive definite kernel \(k : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) is a positive definite kernel if there exists a map \(\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}\) (a Hilbert space) such that \(k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle\). Equivalent: for every \(n\), every \((x_i)\) and every \((c_i) \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\sum_{ij} c_i c_j k(x_i, x_j) \geq 0\) — the Gram matrix \(K = (k(x_i, x_j))\) is symmetric positive.
Examples polynomial \(k(x,x') = (\langle x,x'\rangle + c)^p\) (explicit feature map: all monomials of degree \(\leq p\), dimension \(\binom{d+p}{p}\)); Gaussian (RBF) \(k(x,x') = \exp(-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2)\) — feature map of infinite dimension: expanding \(e^{2\gamma\langle x,x'\rangle}e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}e^{-\gamma\|x'\|^2}\) as a power series in \(\langle x, x'\rangle\) exhibits \(\phi(x)\) = a renormalized Gaussian vector; on graphs: \(k = \exp(-\beta L)\) (diffusion on the Laplacian, cf. Graph ML module) or the shortest-path kernel — kernels are the ancestor of GNNs!

4.1. The Moore-Aronszajn theorem

Theorem 4.2 — Moore-Aronszajn (sketch) If \(k\) is a positive definite kernel, there exists a unique Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\) of functions \(\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that (i) \(k_x := k(\cdot, x) \in \mathcal{H}\) for every \(x\), and (ii) the reproducing property: \(f(x) = \langle f, k_x\rangle\) for every \(f \in \mathcal{H}\), every \(x\). It is called the RKHS of \(k\).
Construction. Set \(\mathcal{H}_0 = \mathrm{Span}\{k_x : x \in \mathcal{X}\}\) (finite combinations) and, for \(f = \sum_i \alpha_i k_{x_i}\), \(g = \sum_j \beta_j k_{y_j}\), $$\langle f, g\rangle_0 := \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\beta_j\, k(x_i, y_j).$$ This form is well defined (independent of the decompositions: reduce to the case \(\langle k_x, k_y\rangle_0 = k(x,y)\) and expand), bilinear, and positive by the very definition of a positive definite kernel; definiteness follows from: \(|f(x)|^2 = |\langle f, k_x\rangle_0|^2 \leq \|f\|_0^2\, k(x,x)\) (Cauchy-Schwarz). Completion. This inequality above all shows that the evaluation \(f \mapsto f(x)\) is 1-Lipschitz-continuous on \(\mathcal{H}_0\): it extends to the completion \(\mathcal{H}\), whose elements are therefore genuine functions (local uniform limits of elements of \(\mathcal{H}_0\)). The reproducing property, true on \(\mathcal{H}_0\) by construction, passes to the limit; uniqueness follows from the density of the \(k_x\).∎

4.2. The representer theorem and kernel regression

Theorem 4.3 — Representer theorem Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be the RKHS of \(k\) and \(\hat f \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \tfrac1n\sum_i \ell(y_i, f(x_i)) + \lambda\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2\) for an arbitrary convex loss. Then \(\hat f \in \mathrm{Span}\{k_{x_i} : i \leq n\}\): $$\hat f = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\, k(\cdot, x_i).$$
Decompose \(\mathcal{H} = V \oplus V^\perp\) with \(V = \mathrm{Span}\{k_{x_i}\}\), and \(f = f_V + f_\perp\). The reproducing property gives \(f(x_i) = \langle f, k_{x_i}\rangle = \langle f_V, k_{x_i}\rangle = f_V(x_i)\): the loss term sees only \(f_V\). Since \(\|f\|^2 = \|f_V\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2\), any nonzero orthogonal component costs norm without changing anything on the data: the optimum lies in \(V\).∎
Proposition 4.4 — Kernel ridge regression (KRR): closed formula The minimizer in \(\mathcal{H}\) of \(\tfrac1n\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2\) is \(\hat f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(x_i, x)\) where $$\alpha = (K + n\lambda I_n)^{-1} y, \qquad K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j).$$
By the representer theorem, substitute \(f = \sum_i\alpha_ik(\cdot,x_i)\). The empirical risk becomes \(\tfrac1n\|y - K\alpha\|^2\) and \(\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2 = \sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_j k(x_i,x_j) = \alpha^\top K\alpha\). Minimize \(\tfrac1n\|y - K\alpha\|^2 + \lambda\,\alpha^\top K\alpha\) in \(\alpha\); the gradient \( \tfrac2n K(K\alpha - y) + 2\lambda K\alpha = 0\) factors through \(K\) (symmetric): \((K + n\lambda I)\alpha = y\), invertible since \(K \succeq 0\).∎
KRR is to kernels what ridge is to the linear case — in infinite dimension but with only \(n\) active degrees of freedom. The SVM of chapter 3 is its "hinge" analogue: the representer theorem justifies a posteriori the form \(\theta = \sum_i\alpha_i y'_i \phi(x_i)\) obtained by duality.
The kernel trick — and its limit Replacing \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) by \(k(x_i, x_j)\) in the SVM dual = working in an infinite-dimensional space without ever computing \(\phi\) (it suffices that \(k\) be directly computable: strings, graphs, sequences...). Powerful, but costly in \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) memory and \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) computation: it is this dimensional limit that motivated deep learning (cf. Deep Learning module), which learns the representation \(\phi\) instead of fixing it.

5. Trees, forests, boosting

5.1. Decision trees (CART)

Recursive partition of \(\mathcal{X}\) by axis-aligned cuts (binary questions "\(x_j \leq t\)?"). At each leaf, one predicts the mean (regression) or the majority class (classification). Growth: at each node, choose \((j, t)\) maximizing the impurity gain; then pruning to avoid overfitting. The exact impurity criteria, for a node \(D\) containing the classes in proportions \(p_1, \dots, p_K\):

Definition 5.1 — Usual impurities $$\text{entropy: } H(D) = -\sum_{k=1}^K p_k \log_2 p_k, \qquad \text{Gini: } G(D) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K p_k^2 = \sum_k p_k(1 - p_k).$$ In regression, one uses the squared error \(Q(D) = \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i \in D}(y_i - \bar y_D)^2\). For a split \(D = D_L \cup D_R\), the gain is $$\Delta(j, t) = I(D) - \frac{|D_L|}{|D|} I(D_L) - \frac{|D_R|}{|D|} I(D_R).$$
Gini and entropy are nearly interchangeable With two classes (\(p = p_1 = 1 - p_2\)): \(G(p) = 2p(1-p)\) and \(H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\). Both are maximal at \(p = 1/2\), zero at \(0\) and \(1\), and their expansion around \(1/2\) coincides to first order: \(H(p) = 1 - \frac{2}{\ln 2}(p - \tfrac12)^2 + o\big((p-\tfrac12)^2\big)\) and \(G(p) = \frac12 - 2(p - \tfrac12)^2\). CART uses Gini (no logarithm, direct computation); ID3/C4.5 use entropy (information gain). The choice is rarely decisive — pruning is. CART's cost-complexity pruning minimizes \(R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|\text{leaves}(T)|\) over the family of subtrees: yet another fit/complexity trade-off (chapter 7).

5.2. Random forests (bagging)

Theorem 5.2 — Bagging reduces variance Let \(T_1, \dots, T_B\) be predictors with the same variance \(\sigma^2\), pairwise correlation \(\rho\). The average \(A_B = \frac1B\sum_b T_b\) satisfies $$\mathrm{Var}(A_B) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2.$$
\(\mathrm{Var}(A_B) = \frac{1}{B^2}\big[\sum_b \mathrm{Var}(T_b) + \sum_{b \neq b'} \mathrm{Cov}(T_b, T_{b'})\big] = \frac{1}{B^2}\big[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\big] = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2\), which rewrites as announced. iid case (\(\rho = 0\), for example Gaussian variables with the same law): \(\mathrm{Var} = \sigma^2/B\) — divide the variance by \(B\) by averaging independent replicates.∎
Why it works so well for trees As \(B \to \infty\), the residual variance tends toward the floor \(\rho\sigma^2\): the whole art consists in lowering \(\rho\). Random forests combine two mechanisms: (i) the bootstrap (resampling the rows); (ii) the random selection of \(m_{\text{try}} < d\) candidate variables at each split (decorrelation), which forces the trees to differ structurally. Unpruned trees have low bias and high variance — exactly the profile that bagging can correct. Bonus: each tree leaves out on average \(e^{-1} \approx 37\%\) of the data (out-of-bag), a free risk estimate — we return to this in chapter 9. Forests remain a hard-to-beat tabular baseline.

5.3. Boosting: gradient descent in function space

Definition 5.3 — The functional problem One seeks \(f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) minimizing $$\Phi(f) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big),$$ where \(\ell\) is convex and differentiable in its second variable (quadratic, logistic...). One builds \(f\) as a sum \(f_M = \sum_{m=0}^M \eta\, h_m\) of simple functions (typically small trees), chosen one at a time — a stagewise additive model.
Theorem 5.4 — Boosting is functional gradient descent Assume \(f_{m-1}\) built and set \(g_i = \frac{\partial\, \ell}{\partial f}(y_i, f_{m-1}(x_i))\) (pseudo-residuals, up to opposite sign). Then, to first order in \(\eta\): $$\Phi(f_{m-1} + \eta h) = \Phi(f_{m-1}) - \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n h(x_i)\,\underbrace{(-g_i)}_{\text{pseudo-residuals}} + o(\eta),$$ so the direction \(h\) that lowers \(\Phi\) the most is the one aligned with the pseudo-residuals \(-g_i\). Gradient boosting fits \(h_m\) by regression (trees) on the pairs \((x_i, -g_i)\).
This is a Taylor expansion of the one-variable function \(s \mapsto \ell(y_i, f_{m-1}(x_i) + s\,h(x_i))\) around \(s = 0\), summed over \(i\) and divided by \(n\) (with \(s = \eta\)): \(\ell(y_i, f_{m-1}(x_i) + \eta h(x_i)) = \ell(y_i, f_{m-1}(x_i)) + \eta\, h(x_i)\, \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial f}(y_i, f_{m-1}(x_i)) + o(\eta)\). Quadratic case \(\ell(y, f) = \frac12(y - f)^2\): \(g_i = f_{m-1}(x_i) - y_i\), hence \(-g_i = y_i - f_{m-1}(x_i)\) — the true residuals: each tree predicts the residuals of the previous ones, and \(\Phi(f + \eta h)\) is exact (no remainder) for \(h\) = residuals. Logistic case \(\ell(y, p) = -y\log p - (1-y)\log(1-p)\) composed with the sigmoid: \(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial f} = \sigma(f) - y\) (computation of chapter 3); pseudo-residuals \(y_i - p_i\).∎
Learning rate and early stopping The step \(\eta\) (typically \(0.01\)–\(0.1\)) is not the optimal descent constant: one gives up exact line search for a small step that (i) limits the variance of each tree, (ii) lets the following trees correct the previous ones — hence more iterations needed. The number \(M\) of iterations plays the role of effective capacity: \(\Phi(f_M)\) decreases mechanically with \(M\), but the generalization risk decreases then rises again; early stopping (choosing \(M\) on validation) is the true regularization of boosting — same logic as the norm \(\|\theta\|\) in ridge (chapter 7): an increasing \(M\) corresponds to a growing dictionary of directions. Friedman (2001) formalized this paradigm; XGBoost/LightGBM made it industrial (second order, leaf regularization, histograms); the M2 module "From Boosting to Foundation Models" starts from there.

6. Unsupervised: k-means, EM, PCA

6.1. k-means

Definition 6.1 — Criterion and Lloyd's algorithm For a partition \(C_1, \dots, C_K\) and centers \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K\), one minimizes $$W(C, \mu) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$$ by alternating: (i) reassigning each point to the nearest centroid, (ii) recentering the centroids. Complexity \(\mathcal{O}(n\,k\,d\,T)\) for \(T\) iterations.
Theorem 6.2 — Convergence of Lloyd Lloyd's algorithm terminates in a finite number of iterations, at a stationary point of \(W\) (a local minimum, not necessarily global).
Step (ii). With the partition fixed, the best center of a cluster \(S\) is its barycenter: by Huygens' identity (expand the square, the cross term cancels), $$\sum_{i \in S}\|x_i - \mu\|^2 = \sum_{i \in S}\|x_i - \bar x_S\|^2 + |S|\,\|\mu - \bar x_S\|^2 \ \geq\ \sum_{i \in S}\|x_i - \bar x_S\|^2.$$ So (ii) cannot increase \(W\), and strictly decreases it if a center was not at the barycenter. Step (i). With the centers fixed, each point \(x_i\) contributes \(\min_k \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2\): reassignment to the nearest cannot increase \(W\), and strictly decreases it as soon as one point changes cluster for a closer center. Conclusion. \(W\) is decreasing, bounded below by \(0\); since there are only \(k^n\) partitions and as many associated center configurations, \(W\) can strictly decrease only finitely many times — the algorithm reaches a configuration where nothing moves: a stationary point.∎
Proposition 6.3 — k-means++ and its guarantee k-means++ initialization: choose \(\mu_1\) uniformly; then choose \(x\) as the next center with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\) (squared distance to the nearest center). Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) show $$\mathbb{E}\big[W_{\text{k-means++}}\big] \leq 8\,(\ln k + 2)\, W^\star,$$ where \(W^\star\) is the global optimum — an \(\mathcal{O}(\log k)\) guarantee, optimal in general. Idea of the proof: at each draw, the probability of falling in a "costly" cluster (high mass × high cost) is at least proportional to its cost; as long as a cluster is poorly covered, its cost keeps a fixed chance of being reduced; one "covers" all the clusters after an expected \(\mathcal{O}(\log k)\) effective draws, each coverage reduces the cost by a geometric factor via the triangle inequality. In practice: run k-means++ several times and keep the best \(W\).

6.2. Gaussian mixtures and EM

Model 6.4 — GMM $$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \, \varphi(x ; \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \pi_k \geq 0, \sum_k \pi_k = 1,$$ with latent variable \(Z \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) with law \(\pi\), \(X \mid Z = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)\).
Theorem 6.5 — EM increases the log-likelihood For any law \(q\) on \(Z\) and any \(\theta\), set $$\mathcal{L}(q, \theta) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p_\theta(x, Z)\big] + H(q), \qquad H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(Z)].$$ Then: $$\log p_\theta(x) = \mathcal{L}(q, \theta) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\big) \ \geq\ \mathcal{L}(q, \theta).$$ It follows that the EM algorithm — E-step: \(q_t = p_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid x)\); M-step: \(\theta_{t+1} \in \arg\max_\theta \mathcal{L}(q_t, \theta)\) — satisfies \(\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x) \geq \log p_{\theta_t}(x)\), with equality iff at a stationary point.
Decomposition. Start from \(\log p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big]\) (a constant for \(q\)) and add-subtract \(\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(Z)]\): $$\log p_\theta(x) - \mathcal{L}(q,\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\log \frac{q(Z)}{p_\theta(Z \mid x)}\Big] = \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\big) \ \geq\ 0$$ (Kullback-Leibler divergence). E-step. The minimum of \(\theta \mapsto \mathrm{KL}(q \|\, p_\theta(\cdot|x))\) over \(q\), with \(\theta = \theta_t\) fixed, is reached at \(q = p_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid x)\), where the KL is zero: thus \(\mathcal{L}(q_t, \theta_t) = \log p_{\theta_t}(x)\), the bound is tight. M-step. \(\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x) \geq \mathcal{L}(q_t, \theta_{t+1}) \geq \mathcal{L}(q_t, \theta_t) = \log p_{\theta_t}(x)\) — the log-likelihood can only increase. Strict increase occurs as long as the M-step improves \(\mathcal{L}\).∎
EM for GMMs, explicit formulas E-step: compute the responsibilities \(\gamma_{ik} = \mathbb{P}(z_i = k \mid x_i) \propto \pi_k \varphi(x_i;\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\) (normalized over \(k\)). M-step: maximizing \(\mathcal{L}\) (equivalent to a weighted complete likelihood) gives $$\pi_k = \frac1n\sum_i \gamma_{ik}, \qquad \mu_k = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik} x_i}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}, \qquad \Sigma_k = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^\top}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}.$$ In practice: initialize with k-means, constrain \(\Sigma_k\) diagonal if \(d\) is large, beware of singularities (variance \(\to 0\)). EM will reappear in HMMs (NLP module), VAEs (Deep Learning), probabilistic PCA below, and graph clustering (stochastic block models, Graph ML module).

6.3. Principal component analysis

Theorem 6.6 — PCA: the two viewpoints are equivalent Let \(X\) be the centered matrix (\(n\) rows, columns of zero mean) and \(\hat\Sigma = \tfrac1n X^\top X\). The following two problems have the same solutions — the eigenvectors \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) of \(\hat\Sigma\) associated with the \(k\) largest eigenvalues: (P1) maximum variance: \(\max \big\{\mathrm{Var}(Xv) : \|v\| = 1\big\} = \max\, v^\top\hat\Sigma v\) (solved successively under \(v \perp v_1, \dots\)); (P2) minimal reconstruction error: \(\min \big\{\sum_i \|x_i - P_V x_i\|^2 : \dim V = k\big\}\), \(P_V\) the orthogonal projection.
Rayleigh lemma. Diagonalize \(\hat\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^\top\) with \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots\). For \(\|v\| = 1\), \(v^\top\hat\Sigma v = \sum_j \lambda_j c_j^2 \leq \lambda_1 \sum_j c_j^2 = \lambda_1\), reached at \(v = v_1\); on the orthogonal of \(v_1\), same argument with \(\lambda_2\), etc. This is (P1). Equivalence. For \(V\) with orthonormal basis \(v_1, \dots, v_k\), the projection is a sum of orthogonal projections: \(P_V x = \sum_j \langle v_j, x\rangle v_j\) and \(\|P_V x\|^2 = \sum_j \langle v_j, x\rangle^2\) (Pythagoras). Hence $$\sum_i \|x_i - P_V x_i\|^2 = \sum_i \|x_i\|^2 - \sum_{j=1}^k v_j^\top \hat\Sigma\, v_j\; n = \text{const.} - n\sum_j v_j^\top\hat\Sigma v_j.$$ Minimizing the reconstruction error ⟺ maximizing the sum of the Rayleigh quotients — solved by the same \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) (P1).∎
SVD. For \(X = U\Sigma V^\top\) (singular value decomposition of the centered matrix), \(X^\top X = V\Sigma^2V^\top\): components = columns of \(V\), scores \(Z = U\Sigma\), variance explained by component \(j\) = \(\sigma_j^2 \big/ \sum_l \sigma_l^2\). The choice of \(k\) (elbow, cumulative variance) is yet another capacity choice — cf. chapter 7.
Probabilistic PCA (PPCA, Tipping & Bishop 1999) Latent variable model: \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_k)\), \(X = WZ + \mu + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_d)\). The maximum likelihood is reached at \(\hat\mu = \bar x\) and \(\hat W\) whose spanned space is that of the first \(k\) principal components (up to a rotation; the columns are \(\sqrt{\lambda_j - \hat\sigma^2}\, v_j\)), with \(\hat\sigma^2\) = mean of the neglected eigenvalues. Classical PCA is therefore the limit \(\sigma^2 \to 0\) of a generative model; in return, PPCA provides a likelihood (model comparison!), an EM for missing data, and mixtures of subspaces. (The complete proof — integrating the marginal law \(p(x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, WW^\top + \sigma^2 I)\) and maximizing — is an excellent exercise; it can be found in the original paper.)

7. Generalization theory

This chapter justifies mathematically what precedes: when and why does empirical risk minimization generalize? It is the theoretical foundation of the Statistical Learning Theory module (P1 option) and the reading grid for all the hyperparameters of the course (regularization, margin, tree depth, number of boosting iterations).

7.1. Why uniform convergence is necessary

Proposition 7.1 — The estimation gap is controlled by the supremum Let \(f_\mathcal{F} \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{R}(f)\). Then $$\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{F}) \ \leq\ 2\, \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}}\ \big|\, \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) - \mathcal{R}(f) \,\big|.$$
Interleave the empirical risk: \(\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{F}) = \big[\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(\hat f_n)\big] + \big[\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(\hat f_n) - \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f_\mathcal{F})\big] + \big[\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f_\mathcal{F}) - \mathcal{R}(f_\mathcal{F})\big]\). The middle term is \(\leq 0\) (definition of ERM); the other two are bounded by the supremum.∎
Example 7.2 — When pointwise convergence is not enough: the sinusoids Take \(\mathcal{X} = [0, 2\pi]\), \(Y \in \{-1, +1\}\) balanced, and the class \(\mathcal{F} = \{x \mapsto \mathrm{sign}(\sin(\omega x)) : \omega > 0\}\). For every sample \(x_1 < \cdots < x_n\) and every label \(y \in \{-1,+1\}^n\), there exists \(\omega\) such that \(\mathrm{sign}(\sin(\omega x_i)) = y_i\) for every \(i\) (choose \(\omega\) large enough to fit an alternation between each pair of consecutive points). ERM therefore reaches \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n = 0\) always, whereas for a fixed \(f\), \(\mathcal{R}(f) = 1/2\) (perfect decorrelation of the signs): Hoeffding (theorem 0.3) holds for each fixed \(f\), but the supremum over \(\mathcal{F}\) equals \(1/2\). Without uniform control, learning proves nothing.

7.2. Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension

Definition 7.3 — Shattering and VC dimension A class \(\mathcal{F}\) of classifiers shatters the points \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) if every label of \(\{-1,+1\}^n\) is realizable by some \(f \in \mathcal{F}\). The growth function \(\Pi_\mathcal{F}(n) = \max_{x_1, \dots, x_n} \#\{(\mathrm{sign}\,f(x_1), \dots, \mathrm{sign}\,f(x_n)) : f \in \mathcal{F}\}\). The VC dimension is \(d_{\mathrm{VC}} = \max\{n : \Pi_\mathcal{F}(n) = 2^n\}\) (possibly \(+\infty\)).
Examples 7.4 — Three VC dimensions computed by hand (i) Thresholds on \(\mathbb{R}\), \(f_t = \mathbf{1}\{x \leq t\}\): \(d_{\mathrm{VC}} = 1\). One point is shattered (\(t\) before/after). Two points \(x_1 < x_2\) are not: the label \((0, 1)\) would require \(x_1 > t\) and \(x_2 \leq t\), impossible. (ii) Intervals \(f_{a,b} = \mathbf{1}\{x \in [a, b]\}\): \(d_{\mathrm{VC}} = 2\). Two points: all labels (via an empty \([a,b]\), enclosing, or exactly fitted); three points \(x_1 < x_2 < x_3\): the label \((1, 0, 1)\) is unrealizable (an interval containing \(x_1, x_3\) contains \(x_2\)). (iii) Affine hyperplanes in \(\mathbb{R}^d\): \(d_{\mathrm{VC}} = d + 1\). The \(d+1\) vertices of a simplex are shattered (to each vertex associate the hyperplane through the opposite face); \(d + 2\) points never are — this is Radon's theorem: any family of \(d+2\) points can be partitioned into two subsets with intersecting convex hulls, and no hyperplan can separate this partition. (iv) Example 7.2 shows a class of infinite VC dimension — which learns nothing.
Lemma 7.5 — Sauer-Shelah lemma (statement) If \(d_{\mathrm{VC}}(\mathcal{F}) = d\), then for every \(n \geq d\), $$\Pi_\mathcal{F}(n) \ \leq\ \sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{n}{i} \ \leq\ \Big(\frac{e\,n}{d}\Big)^{d}.$$ Intuition: a class of dimension \(d\) can realize only labels "determined" by at most \(d\) points — the growth is polynomial of degree \(d\) in \(n\), not exponential. (Proof by induction on \(n + d\), exercise; cf. Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, ch. 6.)

7.3. The VC bound by double sampling (sketch)

Theorem 7.6 — Vapnik-Chervonenkis bound With probability at least \(1 - \delta\) over the sample, simultaneously for every \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) (loss in \([0,1]\), VC dimension \(d\), \(n \geq d\)): $$\mathcal{R}(f) \ \leq\ \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) + \sqrt{\frac{8\,\big(d \log \tfrac{2 e n}{d} + \log \tfrac{4}{\delta}\big)}{n}}.$$
Sketch in three moves — the Vapnik-Chervonenkis double-sampling argument. (1) Ghost sample. Draw \(2n\) iid points and split into two halves \(S, S'\) with empirical risks \(\hat R_S, \hat R_{S'}\). By Hoeffding (theorem 0.3), each is within \(\varepsilon/4\) of \(\mathcal{R}\) with high probability; consequently, if some \(f\) commits \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(f) + \varepsilon \leq \mathcal{R}(f)\), then \(\hat R_S(f)\) and \(\hat R_{S'}(f)\) differ by at least \(\varepsilon/2\): we have replaced the unobservable risk \(\mathcal{R}\) by a second observable sample. (2) Symmetrization by permutations. Conditionally on the \(2n\) points, the labels \(f(x_i)\) of a given \(f\) take only \(\Pi_\mathcal{F}(2n)\) values; by randomly swapping the roles \(S \leftrightarrow S'\) (random permutation), the probability that a better \(f\) of the class separates the two halves by \(\varepsilon/2\) is bounded by \(2\,\Pi_\mathcal{F}(2n)\, e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8}\): a simple counting over the \(\Pi_\mathcal{F}(2n)\) distinct behaviors, each subject to a binomial Hoeffding. (3) Sauer. Bound \(\Pi_\mathcal{F}(2n) \leq (2en/d)^d\), solve in \(\varepsilon\) the inequality \((2en/d)^d e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8} \leq \delta\): this is the announced square root.∎
Reading the bound The excess risk is \(\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{d/n}\,\big)\) up to logarithmic constants: to divide the error by two, one needs four times more data — or a class four times smaller. Practical rules: (i) \(n \gg d\) is the healthy regime; (ii) capacity control (reduce the effective \(d\) by regularization, margin, sparsity) pays as much as more data; (iii) the bound is conservative — it explains, it does not predict exact performance.

7.4. Rademacher complexity

Definition 7.7 — Empirical Rademacher complexity For a sample \(S = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\) and iid Rademacher variables \(\sigma_i\) uniform on \(\{-1, +1\}\): $$\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) = \mathbb{E}_\sigma\Big[\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(x_i)\Big].$$ This is the capacity of \(\mathcal{F}\) to "fit the noise": if a class can align itself with purely random labels, it will also memorize your data.
Theorem 7.8 — Oracle bounds via Rademacher For an \(L\)-Lipschitz loss, with probability \(\geq 1 - \delta\): $$\mathcal{R}(\hat f_n) \ \leq\ \inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathcal{R}(f) + 2\, L\, \mathbb{E}_S\big[\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F})\big] + \sqrt{\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}},$$ and for bounded linear classes \(\mathcal{F}_R = \{x \mapsto \langle\theta, x\rangle : \|\theta\| \leq R, \|x\| \leq 1\}\): \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}_R) \leq R/\sqrt n\) (computation of theorem 3.6). Sketch. (i) symmetrization by a ghost sample (as in 7.3, without counting this time — the supremum stays on the empirical side); (ii) Ledoux-Talagrand contraction lemma: composing with a Lipschitz loss only multiplies the complexity by the constant \(L\).∎

Rademacher versus VC: the Rademacher complexity is data-dependent (it is computed on the sample, by simulating the \(\sigma_i\)), without a logarithmic factor, and applies to continuous classes of real-valued functions (scores, margins). It is the standard tool of modern bounds — including the SVM margin bounds (theorem 3.6) and the aggregation bounds of chapter 8.

7.5. Regularizing = controlling capacity

Why this is central in ML Reread the course: ridge and lasso constrain \(\|\theta\|\) — and the norm-\(R\) ball has Rademacher complexity \(R/\sqrt n\) (theorem 7.8); the SVM maximizes the margin \(\gamma\), which divides the complexity by \(\gamma\) (theorem 3.6); boosting stops early, limiting the effective size of the tree dictionary; tree pruning (5.1) and rank selection in PCA (6.3) do the same. This is structural risk minimization: a scale of nested classes \(\mathcal{F}_1 \subset \mathcal{F}_2 \subset \cdots\), each with its complexity \(\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{F}_j)\), and a trade-off tuned on data \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f) + \text{complexity}\). The "lambda" you tune in the labs is exactly this theory-practice slider.

8.1. Jesse Read: learning sets of labels (multi-label, streams, extreme)

Definition 8.1 — Multi-label problem Each example \(x\) is associated with a set of labels \(Y \subseteq \{1, \dots, L\}\), viewed as a vector of \(\{0,1\}^L\). One learns \(h : \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}^L\). Usual losses: Hamming (error rate per label, \(\frac1L\sum_j \mathbf{1}\{h_j(x) \neq y_j\}\)) and subset accuracy (subset accuracy: the whole vector must be correct). Applications: text tagging, genomics, recommendation — cf. the running project (exercise 12) and the NLP module.

The taxonomy popularized by Jesse Read's thesis (Scalable Multi-label Classification, Waikato, 2010) and by the classifier chains paper separates methods into two families: problem transformation (bring multi-label back to standard binary/multiclass problems) and algorithm adaptation (modify kNN, trees, SVMs... to directly ingest label vectors). Three canonical transformations:

8.2. Erwan Le Pennec: aggregating estimators rather than choosing one

Facing a family \(f_1, \dots, f_M\) of candidate estimators (different kernels, different wavelet bases, different values of \(\lambda\)...), the classical statistician selects one. The alternative carried by Erwan Le Pennec and his co-authors is to aggregate them: predict with a weighted combination \(f_{\hat\pi} = \sum_m \hat\pi_m f_m\), the weights being learned from data — typically exponential weights (Gibbs measure) $$\hat\pi_m \ \propto\ \exp\big(-\lambda\, n\, \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f_m)\big), \qquad \sum_m \hat\pi_m = 1.$$ The risk of the aggregate is then controlled by an oracle inequality: pay the price of the best candidate up to a \(\log M\) term, with no assumption on which one is best.

Example 8.3 — Two estimators, exponential weights \(M = 2\), empirical risks \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f_1) = 0{,}30\) and \(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_n(f_2) = 0{,}38\), \(n = 100\), \(\lambda = 1\): \(\hat\pi_1/\hat\pi_2 = \exp\big(n\lambda(0{,}38 - 0{,}30)\big) = e^{8} \approx 3000\); the aggregate ≈ \(f_1\). With \(n = 10\): ratio \(e^{0{,}8} \approx 2{,}2\), an almost uniform mixture. Moral: \(\lambda\) tunes the selectivity according to the amount of data — exactly the role of the temperature in Gibbs mechanisms, and we recover the softmax formula of chapter 3. If a third candidate emerges on fresh data, the aggregate will rebalance — a robustness that a hard selection (argmin) does not have.
Typical result (Montuelle & Le Pennec, 2014/2019) In the fixed-design regression model, for a generic collection of linear estimators (projections, ridge, thresholding algorithms, wavelet thresholding estimators...), the PAC-Bayesian aggregation procedure with exponential weights satisfies an oracle inequality in probability: with probability \(1 - \delta\), the risk of the aggregate is less than the risk of the best estimator of the collection plus an explicit remainder in \(\sqrt{\log(M/\delta)/n}\) (and no longer only in expectation — the in-probability guarantee, a finer one, is the specific contribution of this work; long version: PAC-Bayesian aggregation of linear estimators, Nonparametric Statistics, 2019; preprint arXiv:1410.0661). Aggregation is robust to the worst case: one never bets on a single candidate.
Connection with the rest of the course The guiding thread is the same: bagging (theorem 5.2) = aggregation with uniform weights of bootstrap versions of one estimator; boosting (5.3) = iterative construction of the dictionary, each tree receiving a weight; exponential weights = the update mechanism of AdaBoost and Gibbs algorithms; regularization (7.5) = why the aggregate generalizes better than the best empirical candidate. On this last point, Erwan Le Pennec's nonparametric statistics provides the guardrails: optimal kernel selection for density estimation, needlet thresholding for models with random coefficients (with É. Gautier), estimation of copula densities by thresholding (with F. Autin and K. Tribouley, 2010) — always the same grammar: a dictionary, a penalty, an oracle inequality. NL-Means image denoising can be reread this way as a local aggregation of kernel estimators (Salmon & Le Pennec, 2009).

9.2. Nested cross-validation

Selecting a model and estimating its risk on the same folds is a well-documented optimism bias: the retained score is that of a "champion" chosen among the candidates on those same data — the exact mechanism of selection overfitting (chapter 7!). Nested cross-validation separates the two roles: the inner loop (on \(k-1\) folds) chooses the hyperparameters, the outer loop (the remaining fold) evaluates the model thus chosen. Cost: \(k_{\text{int}} \times k_{\text{ext}}\) trainings — the price of statistical honesty.

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
grid = GridSearchCV(pipe, {"logisticregression__C": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5)
outer_scores = cross_val_score(grid, X, y, cv=10)   # outer loop: honest estimation
print(outer_scores.mean(), outer_scores.std())

9.3. Data leakage

The four classic leaks — and the antidote (1) Standardizing/imputing before the split: the test statistics contaminate the training. (2) Target encoding (target encoding) computed on the whole set: the answer leaks into the features. (3) Duplicates of the same entity (patient, sensor) split across both train and test: one evaluates memory, not generalization. (4) Variables posterior to the predicted event (reimbursement amount to predict a default). The antidote fits in one rule: every transformation that "learns" lives inside the pipeline, hence re-estimated on each training fold — this is exactly what scikit-learn's Pipeline and ColumnTransformer do, including for nested validators. 
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                      ("sc",  StandardScaler())]), num_cols),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat_cols),
])
model = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("clf", HistGradientBoostingClassifier())])
# model.fit on the train only; nothing is learned outside fit().

9.4. Imbalanced classes

With 0.5% of fraud, the trivial classifier "everything is normal" reaches 99.5% accuracy. Three levers: (i) the loss — weight the classes via class_weight="balanced": the \(\propto 1/n_k\) weighting restores the balance in ERM; (ii) resampling — oversampling the minority (SMOTE: interpolate between minority neighbors) or undersampling the majority, always inside the training pipeline only (otherwise: leakage); (iii) evaluation — precision/recall, PR-AUC; and the decision threshold chosen after calibration (9.5), not at 0.5 by default.

9.5. Probability calibration

Definition 9.1 — Calibration A probability predictor \(\hat p\) is calibrated if, among the cases where it announces \(p\), the observed frequency of the event equals \(p\): \(\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid \hat p(X) = p) = p\). The reliability diagram (observed frequency vs predicted probability, per bin) and the Brier score \(\frac1n\sum_i(\hat p_i - y_i)^2\) (a squared loss! cf. chapter 2) measure it.

Two standard post-processings, learned on a separate calibration set: Platt scaling — fit a logistic regression (chapter 3) on the noisy score: \(\hat p = \sigma(a\,s(x) + b)\); isotonic regression — the nonparametric monotone function closest to the reliability bins (more flexible, more data needed). Caution: discrimination and calibration are two distinct properties — a model may excellently order examples (high AUC) while announcing systematically overconfident probabilities. SVM scores are not probabilities: going through calibration is indispensable as soon as one wants probabilities (cost/benefit decisions, stacking).

from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV

9.6. Big data: what does size change?

When \(n\) explodes, theory plays for you (bounds in \(\sqrt{d/n}\), chapter 7) but computation costs become the limiting factor: switch to incremental methods (partial_fit/SGD: the stochastic gradient descent of chapter 0, one pass at a time), to hashing for sparse text features, to histogram implementations (HistGradientBoosting), and to the stream methods of chapter 8 (River) when the data do not fit in memory. Golden rule of the program: a complete reference pipeline — train/val/test split → preprocessing learned on train only → nested selection → single evaluation on test → error inspection (confusion matrix, residuals of chapter 2, subgroups) — to be replicated in all the labs and the running project.

  6. Bagging and correlation. Derive again the formula \(\mathrm{Var}(A_B) = \rho\sigma^2 + \tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\) of theorem 5.2, then comment: what happens if the \(B\) trees are identical (\(\rho = 1\))? Why does a small \(m_{\text{try}}\) decrease \(\rho\) — and at what price?
    Solution Expand the variance of the sum with equicorrelated covariances \(\tfrac1{B^2}[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2]\). If \(\rho = 1\), all the trees are identical: the variance remains \(\sigma^2\), bagging is useless. Reducing \(m_{\text{try}}\) randomizes the candidate splits and decorrelates the trees; in exchange, each tree, seeing fewer variables, may miss the "right" split — its individual bias increases slightly. The optimal trade-off is found by validation (chapter 9).∎

    Solution (i) For \(x_1 < x_2 < x_3\), the label \((1,0,1)\) is unrealizable: an interval containing \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) contains the whole interval \([x_1, x_3]\), hence \(x_2\). The bound is (very) conservative — it guarantees the consistency of the ERM principle (error \(\to 0\) as \(n/d \to \infty\)), not actual performance; this is why one also evaluates by cross-validation (9.2).∎

  10. Multi-label: Hamming versus subset accuracy. On example 8.2 (labels \(S, P, s\)), one tests on 10 documents of which 6 follow the pattern "\(S=1, P=0 \Rightarrow s=1\)" and 4 are "all zero". BR learns \(h_s \equiv 0\) (it sees only \(x\)), CC learns \(h_s(x, S=1, P=0) = 1\). Compute the Hamming and subset accuracy losses of the two methods, and explain when BR becomes competitive again.
    Solution CC is perfect on the learned patterns: Hamming \(0\), accuracy \(1\). BR errs on the label \(s\) of the 6 "pure science" documents: Hamming \(= \frac{6}{10 \times 3} = 0{,}2\), accuracy \(= \frac4{10} = 0{,}4\). If the \(S \to s\) dependency did not exist (labels conditionally independent given \(x\)), BR would be optimal for Hamming — the chain would only add noise; it is the targeted loss (Hamming vs fine dependencies) that dictates the method (table in section 8.1).∎

What link with the softmax of chapter 3, and what role does \(\lambda\) play when one doubts the validity of the empirical risks (small \(n\))?
    Solution Weights \(\propto \exp(-100\lambda \hat R_m)\): for \(\lambda = 0{,}1\), ratios \(e^{-0{,}2} \approx 0{,}82\) and \(e^{-2} \approx 0{,}14\) (soft mixture); for \(\lambda = 1\), \(e^{-2} \approx 0{,}14\) and \(e^{-20} \approx 2\cdot10^{-9}\); for \(\lambda = 10\), quasi-hard selection. This is a softmax on the scores \(-n\lambda\hat R_m\) — \(\lambda\) plays the role of the inverse temperature. At small \(n\), the \(\hat R_m\) are noisy (chapter 0): taking \(\lambda\) small keeps a mixture, robust to estimation error — precisely the logic of the oracle inequalities of chapter 8.∎
