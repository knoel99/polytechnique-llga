Exercises

  1. Closed-form ridge. Show that \(\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}}\) is indeed given by \((X^\top X + n\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\) by setting the gradient to zero, then that the problem is always strictly convex as soon as \(\lambda > 0\).
    Solution Gradient: \(\nabla_\theta\big(\tfrac1n\|y - X\theta\|^2 + \lambda\|\theta\|_2^2\big) = \tfrac2n X^\top(X\theta - y) + 2\lambda\theta\); setting it to zero gives \((X^\top X + n\lambda I)\theta = X^\top y\). The Hessian \(\tfrac2n(X^\top X + n\lambda I) \succeq 2\lambda I \succ 0\) is positive definite for \(\lambda > 0\), even if \(X^\top X\) is singular: unique global minimum — this is the first practical benefit of regularization (cf. proposition 2.5 for the spectral reading).∎
  2. Gauss-Markov in dimension 1. Model \(y_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i\) centered, independent, with variance \(\sigma^2\). Show that among the linear unbiased estimators \(a^\top y\) of \(\theta\), the empirical mean has minimal variance.
    Solution Unbiasedness: \(\mathbb{E}[a^\top y] = \theta\,\textstyle\sum_i a_i = \theta\) for every \(\theta\), hence \(\sum_i a_i = 1\). Variance: \(\mathrm{Var}(a^\top y) = \sigma^2\|a\|^2\). Minimizing \(\|a\|^2\) under the constraint \(\mathbf{1}^\top a = 1\) (Lagrange multipliers, chapter 0) gives the uniform \(a_i = 1/n\). This is the case \(X = \mathbf 1\) of theorem 2.3; the estimator \(\frac1n\sum y_i\) is its geometric projection.∎
  3. Cross-entropy. Verify that minimizing the logistic cross-entropy amounts to maximizing the Bernoulli likelihood \(\prod_i \sigma(\theta^\top x_i)^{y_i}(1-\sigma)^{1-y_i}\), and compute \(\nabla_\theta \hat{\mathcal{R}}_n = \frac1n X^\top(\sigma(X\theta) - y)\). (All the steps are in theorem 3.3: redo them without notes.)
  4. Kernel duel. (i) Show that \(k(x, x') = (\langle x, x'\rangle + 1)^2\) on \(\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^2\) is a kernel by exhibiting an explicit feature map. (ii) Is the function \(k(x,x') = \cos(x - x')\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) a positive definite kernel?
    Solution (i) Expand: \((x_1x'_1 + x_2x'_2 + 1)^2 = x_1^2x_1'^2 + 2x_1x_1'x_2x_2' + x_2^2x_2'^2 + 2x_1x'_1 + 2x_2x'_2 + 1 = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle\) with \(\phi(x) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1,\ \sqrt2\,x_2,\ 1)\) — dimension 6 versus 2: the kernel trick saves the explicit passage. (ii) Yes: \(\cos(x - x') = \cos x\cos x' + \sin x \sin x' = \langle(\cos x, \sin x),\,(\cos x', \sin x')\rangle\) (addition formula). A dimension-2 feature map, unit norm.∎
  5. Dual derivation of the SVM. Take up the primal of theorem 3.5: write the Lagrangian with multipliers \((\alpha_i, \mu_i)\), set the gradients in \((\theta, b, \xi)\) to zero, and recover (a) the dual function, (b) the three families of points of the table in section 3.2 via the complementarity conditions.
  6. Bagging and correlation. Derive again the formula \(\mathrm{Var}(A_B) = \rho\sigma^2 + \tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\) of theorem 5.2, then comment: what happens if the \(B\) trees are identical (\(\rho = 1\))? Why does a small \(m_{\text{try}}\) decrease \(\rho\) — and at what price?
    Solution Expand the variance of the sum with equicorrelated covariances \(\tfrac1{B^2}[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2]\). If \(\rho = 1\), all the trees are identical: the variance remains \(\sigma^2\), bagging is useless. Reducing \(m_{\text{try}}\) randomizes the candidate splits and decorrelates the trees; in exchange, each tree, seeing fewer variables, may miss the "right" split — its individual bias increases slightly. The optimal trade-off is found by validation (chapter 9).∎
  7. EM for a mixture of two 1D Gaussians. Write the two steps, implement (NumPy), and check on a mixture \(0{,}4\,\mathcal{N}(-2, 1) + 0{,}6\,\mathcal{N}(3, 1)\) that the log-likelihood is increasing over 50 iterations (theorem 6.5: plot \(\log p_{\theta_t}\) and check the increase, strict away from a stationary point).
  8. VC by hand. (i) Show that the intervals \([a,b]\) of \(\mathbb{R}\) do not shatter 3 aligned points (example 7.4(ii)). (ii) Compute the VC bound (theorem 7.6) for \(d = 10\), \(n = 1000\), \(\delta = 5\%\) and comment ("how much data per dimension?").
    Solution (i) For \(x_1 < x_2 < x_3\), the label \((1,0,1)\) is unrealizable: an interval containing \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) contains the whole interval \([x_1, x_3]\), hence \(x_2\). (ii) \(\sqrt{8(10\log(2e \cdot 1000/10) + \log 8)/1000} \approx \sqrt{8 \times (58{,}3 + 2{,}1)/1000} \approx 0{,}70\): a theoretical error margin of 70 points! The bound is (very) conservative — it guarantees the consistency of the ERM principle (error \(\to 0\) as \(n/d \to \infty\)), not actual performance; this is why one also evaluates by cross-validation (9.2).∎
  9. k-means++. Explain why the naive initialization can cost \(\mathcal{O}(\log k)\) times the optimum (give an example: clusters of very unequal sizes), and simulate both initializations on anisotropic blobs while measuring the ratio \(W_{\text{final}}/W^\star\) (proposition 6.3: aim for the guarantee \(8(\ln k + 2)\)).
  10. Multi-label: Hamming versus subset accuracy. On example 8.2 (labels \(S, P, s\)), one tests on 10 documents of which 6 follow the pattern "\(S=1, P=0 \Rightarrow s=1\)" and 4 are "all zero". BR learns \(h_s \equiv 0\) (it sees only \(x\)), CC learns \(h_s(x, S=1, P=0) = 1\). Compute the Hamming and subset accuracy losses of the two methods, and explain when BR becomes competitive again.
    Solution CC is perfect on the learned patterns: Hamming \(0\), accuracy \(1\). BR errs on the label \(s\) of the 6 "pure science" documents: Hamming \(= \frac{6}{10 \times 3} = 0{,}2\), accuracy \(= \frac4{10} = 0{,}4\). If the \(S \to s\) dependency did not exist (labels conditionally independent given \(x\)), BR would be optimal for Hamming — the chain would only add noise; it is the targeted loss (Hamming vs fine dependencies) that dictates the method (table in section 8.1).∎
  11. Exponential weights. Three estimators with empirical risks \(0{,}30\), \(0{,}32\), \(0{,}50\), \(n = 100\). Compute the weights \(\hat\pi\) for \(\lambda \in \{0{,}1\,;\ 1\,;\ 10\}\) (definition in chapter 8) and plot \(\max_m \hat\pi_m\) as a function of \(\lambda\). What link with the softmax of chapter 3, and what role does \(\lambda\) play when one doubts the validity of the empirical risks (small \(n\))?
    Solution Weights \(\propto \exp(-100\lambda \hat R_m)\): for \(\lambda = 0{,}1\), ratios \(e^{-0{,}2} \approx 0{,}82\) and \(e^{-2} \approx 0{,}14\) (soft mixture); for \(\lambda = 1\), \(e^{-2} \approx 0{,}14\) and \(e^{-20} \approx 2\cdot10^{-9}\); for \(\lambda = 10\), quasi-hard selection. This is a softmax on the scores \(-n\lambda\hat R_m\) — \(\lambda\) plays the role of the inverse temperature. At small \(n\), the \(\hat R_m\) are noisy (chapter 0): taking \(\lambda\) small keeps a mixture, robust to estimation error — precisely the logic of the oracle inequalities of chapter 8.∎
  12. Running project. On the 20 Newsgroups dataset (count vectorizer → TF-IDF → logistic/forest), build the complete pipeline with nested cross-validation (9.2), report the confusion matrix and macro F1, and calibrate the probabilities (9.5) before choosing the decision threshold. This text preprocessing will be redone "in a modern neural version" in the NLP module.
