4. Kernels and RKHS

Definition 4.1 — Positive definite kernel \(k : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) is a positive definite kernel if there exists a map \(\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}\) (a Hilbert space) such that \(k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle\). Equivalent: for every \(n\), every \((x_i)\) and every \((c_i) \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\sum_{ij} c_i c_j k(x_i, x_j) \geq 0\) — the Gram matrix \(K = (k(x_i, x_j))\) is symmetric positive.
Examples polynomial \(k(x,x') = (\langle x,x'\rangle + c)^p\) (explicit feature map: all monomials of degree \(\leq p\), dimension \(\binom{d+p}{p}\)); Gaussian (RBF) \(k(x,x') = \exp(-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2)\) — feature map of infinite dimension: expanding \(e^{2\gamma\langle x,x'\rangle}e^{-\gamma\|x\|^2}e^{-\gamma\|x'\|^2}\) as a power series in \(\langle x, x'\rangle\) exhibits \(\phi(x)\) = a renormalized Gaussian vector; on graphs: \(k = \exp(-\beta L)\) (diffusion on the Laplacian, cf. Graph ML module) or the shortest-path kernel — kernels are the ancestor of GNNs!

4.1. The Moore-Aronszajn theorem

Theorem 4.2 — Moore-Aronszajn (sketch) If \(k\) is a positive definite kernel, there exists a unique Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\) of functions \(\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that (i) \(k_x := k(\cdot, x) \in \mathcal{H}\) for every \(x\), and (ii) the reproducing property: \(f(x) = \langle f, k_x\rangle\) for every \(f \in \mathcal{H}\), every \(x\). It is called the RKHS of \(k\).
Construction. Set \(\mathcal{H}_0 = \mathrm{Span}\{k_x : x \in \mathcal{X}\}\) (finite combinations) and, for \(f = \sum_i \alpha_i k_{x_i}\), \(g = \sum_j \beta_j k_{y_j}\), $$\langle f, g\rangle_0 := \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\beta_j\, k(x_i, y_j).$$ This form is well defined (independent of the decompositions: reduce to the case \(\langle k_x, k_y\rangle_0 = k(x,y)\) and expand), bilinear, and positive by the very definition of a positive definite kernel; definiteness follows from: \(|f(x)|^2 = |\langle f, k_x\rangle_0|^2 \leq \|f\|_0^2\, k(x,x)\) (Cauchy-Schwarz). Completion. This inequality above all shows that the evaluation \(f \mapsto f(x)\) is 1-Lipschitz-continuous on \(\mathcal{H}_0\): it extends to the completion \(\mathcal{H}\), whose elements are therefore genuine functions (local uniform limits of elements of \(\mathcal{H}_0\)). The reproducing property, true on \(\mathcal{H}_0\) by construction, passes to the limit; uniqueness follows from the density of the \(k_x\).∎

4.2. The representer theorem and kernel regression

Theorem 4.3 — Representer theorem Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be the RKHS of \(k\) and \(\hat f \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \tfrac1n\sum_i \ell(y_i, f(x_i)) + \lambda\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2\) for an arbitrary convex loss. Then \(\hat f \in \mathrm{Span}\{k_{x_i} : i \leq n\}\): $$\hat f = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\, k(\cdot, x_i).$$
Decompose \(\mathcal{H} = V \oplus V^\perp\) with \(V = \mathrm{Span}\{k_{x_i}\}\), and \(f = f_V + f_\perp\). The reproducing property gives \(f(x_i) = \langle f, k_{x_i}\rangle = \langle f_V, k_{x_i}\rangle = f_V(x_i)\): the loss term sees only \(f_V\). Since \(\|f\|^2 = \|f_V\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2\), any nonzero orthogonal component costs norm without changing anything on the data: the optimum lies in \(V\).∎
Proposition 4.4 — Kernel ridge regression (KRR): closed formula The minimizer in \(\mathcal{H}\) of \(\tfrac1n\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2\) is \(\hat f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(x_i, x)\) where $$\alpha = (K + n\lambda I_n)^{-1} y, \qquad K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j).$$
By the representer theorem, substitute \(f = \sum_i\alpha_ik(\cdot,x_i)\). The empirical risk becomes \(\tfrac1n\|y - K\alpha\|^2\) and \(\|f\|_\mathcal{H}^2 = \sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_j k(x_i,x_j) = \alpha^\top K\alpha\). Minimize \(\tfrac1n\|y - K\alpha\|^2 + \lambda\,\alpha^\top K\alpha\) in \(\alpha\); the gradient \( \tfrac2n K(K\alpha - y) + 2\lambda K\alpha = 0\) factors through \(K\) (symmetric): \((K + n\lambda I)\alpha = y\), invertible since \(K \succeq 0\).∎
KRR is to kernels what ridge is to the linear case — in infinite dimension but with only \(n\) active degrees of freedom. The SVM of chapter 3 is its "hinge" analogue: the representer theorem justifies a posteriori the form \(\theta = \sum_i\alpha_i y'_i \phi(x_i)\) obtained by duality.
The kernel trick — and its limit Replacing \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) by \(k(x_i, x_j)\) in the SVM dual = working in an infinite-dimensional space without ever computing \(\phi\) (it suffices that \(k\) be directly computable: strings, graphs, sequences...). Powerful, but costly in \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) memory and \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) computation: it is this dimensional limit that motivated deep learning (cf. Deep Learning module), which learns the representation \(\phi\) instead of fixing it.
