Most industrial data is neither images nor text: it is tables — customers, sensors, transactions, records — and on this ground gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) still dominate deep networks. This module explains why, by descending into the mathematics of boosting and the engineering of XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, then examines how foundation models (TabPFN, LLMs) move the needle: tables learned in-context, text-to-SQL, synthetic data. It is the master's module that connects "classical" machine learning — the kind you will meet in industry — to the generative foundations at the heart of LLGA. 0. Reminders: trees, forests, boosting

Reminders from the Machine Learning module (M1 P1, ch. 5) — what is assumed acquired
  • CART trees: recursive partitions of the space by univariate thresholds \(x_j \leq t\), splits chosen by maximizing the impurity decrease (Gini/variance), depth = control of the bias-variance trade-off;
  • Bias-variance decomposition: \(\mathrm{Err} = \mathrm{bias}^2 + \mathrm{variance} + \mathrm{noise}\), and the role of ensembles: bagging and random forests reduce variance by averaging decorrelated trees;
  • AdaBoost: iterative reweighting of misclassified examples, prediction by weighted vote of weak classifiers;
  • Gradient boosting: at each iteration a small tree is fit on the residuals of the current model, then added with a step \(\eta > 0\);
  • Evaluation (ch. 9): cross-validation, data leakage, benchmark protocols — we return to this in ch. 9 of the present module.
Definition 0.1 — Notation of the module A tabular dataset is a matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) whose columns (features) are heterogeneous: numerical (price, age, log-ratio), categorical (country, product code), ordinal, binary, dates, sometimes textual; the target is \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\) (classification) or \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) (regression). We write \(f\) for the model, \(L\) for the loss, \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) for the sample. A single row can contain scales ranging from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{7}\) and massively skewed columns — it is this heterogeneity that structures the whole module.
The module's storyline M1 (cf. Machine Learning module ch. 5) presented boosting as an idea: "one fits trees on the residuals". This module takes it seriously as a central object of study: (i) we prove the formulas of the industrial engines (optimal leaf weights, split gain, GOSS, ordered statistics); (ii) we explain why they win on tables, based on the large empirical studies of 2021-2025; (iii) we explore the two deep counter-attacks — attention over features (ch. 5) and tabular foundations (TabPFN, ch. 6) or linguistic ones (ch. 7); (iv) we finish with synthetic generation (ch. 8) and an honest comparison protocol (ch. 9), extending ch. 9 of the Machine Learning module.

1. Why GBDTs (still) dominate tabular data

Images, text, speech, and graphs have been conquered by deep networks. Tables — which remain the majority format of enterprise data (CRM, sensors, finance, health, logs) — resist. This finding was established rigorously by three major empirical studies, which serve as the module's guiding thread.

Definition 1.1 — GBDT (extended reminder) A gradient boosted decision tree is an additive model $$ F_M(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta\, h_m(x), $$ where each \(h_m\) is a shallow regression tree fit on the negative of the gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction (ch. 2). The structural hyperparameters: number of trees \(M\), learning rate \(\eta\), maximum depth (≈ order of interaction), subsampling rate (rows/columns), leaf regularization.
Study 1.2 — Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux (NeurIPS 2022) "Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on typical tabular data?": over dozens of medium-sized OpenML datasets (up to \(\sim\!10,000\) rows, regression and classification), tree-based models (XGBoost, scikit-learn GBDT) systematically beat deep tabular networks, even after reasonable tuning. The paper isolates two mechanistic causes:
  • networks are sensitive to uninformative features: removing as many as possible helps networks far more than trees, which naturally "isolate" useful columns (a tree can simply never split on a useless column);
  • networks struggle with irregular target functions: neither smooth nor monotone, with "steps" and local interactions — the natural terrain of threshold splits.
A random rotation experiment on the columns confirms the diagnosis: after rotation, trees degrade sharply (their axis-aligned splits lose their meaning) whereas networks — rotation-invariant by construction — fare better: the axis-aligned structure of real tables is information that trees exploit and MLPs ignore.
Proposition 1.3 — Trees are invariant under monotone transformation of columns Let \(h\) be a tree and, for each column \(j\), \(g_j : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) a strictly increasing function. If \(\tilde{x}_j = g_j(x_j)\), then the tree trained on the transformed data induces the same partition of the space (up to the thresholds) and makes the same predictions. In particular: log-transforming, whitening, or clipping a skewed column changes (almost) nothing for a GBDT.
A split \(x_j \leq t\) partitions the examples into \(\{i : x_{ij} \leq t\}\) and its complement. Since \(g_j\) is strictly increasing, \(x_{ij} \leq t \iff g_j(x_{ij}) \leq g_j(t)\): every split on \(x_j\) is a split on \(\tilde{x}_j\), with the image threshold \(g_j(t)\). By recursion over the depth, the partitions coincide, so the leaves receive the same examples and the predictions (leaf means/majorities) are identical. ∎
Why this proposition matters It explains a robust empirical result: GBDTs win especially when features have skewed, heavy, multimodal distributions — precisely the cases where a network, which adds and multiplies activations, suffers from scales and distribution tails, whereas a tree only looks at the order of values. McElfresh et al. (TabZilla, NeurIPS 2023: When do neural nets outperform boosted trees on tabular data?) quantifies this over 176 datasets and 19 algorithms: GBDTs dominate on skewed/heavy-tailed features, and the most decisive performance factor is not the model family but the tuning budget — "lightly tuning a GBDT matters more than choosing between a GBDT and a network".
Example 1.4 — The 2021-2023 debate in three studies (i) Shwartz-Ziv & Armon (Tabular data: Deep learning is not all you need, Information Fusion 2022): XGBoost beats deep models on most of their datasets, and tuning does not close the gap; GBDT+network ensembles = the best of both. (ii) Grinsztajn et al. (2022): the gap is real at medium sizes and stems from inductive biases (study 1.2). (iii) McElfresh et al. (2023): on the largest available benchmark, no family dominates everywhere; a simple hybrid (average of a GBDT + a lightly tuned MLP) is the best "default". Moral: the question is not "which is best" but "in which regime" — the subject of ch. 10.

2. Boosting: functional gradient descent

Boosting is most cleanly written as gradient descent in the space of functions — a view due to Friedman (2001), which generalizes AdaBoost (Freund & Schapire, 1997; cf. Machine Learning module ch. 5 for the historical account).

Definition 2.1 — Functional gradient descent We look for \(F^\star = \arg\min_F \sum_i L(y_i, F(x_i))\) in a space of functions. The "gradient at point \(x_i\)" is the variable \(r_i = -\left[\dfrac{\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F = F_{m-1}}\). The boosting iteration: (1) compute the \(r_i\); (2) fit a weak learner \(h_m\) (shallow tree) by least squares to the \(r_i\); (3) update \(F_m = F_{m-1} + \eta\, h_m\).
Proposition 2.2 — Residuals for the quadratic loss For \(L(y, a) = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - a)^2\), one has exactly \(r_i = y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i)\): "fitting on the residuals" is a special case of definition 2.1.
\(\partial L / \partial a = a - y\), so \(-\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))/\partial F = y_i - F(x_i)\). ∎
The role of each ingredient \(\eta\) (shrinkage): a small step ⇒ each tree corrects what the previous ones left behind — equivalent to a regularization of the descent step, to be combined with more trees. Subsampling (Friedman, 2002): fitting each tree on a fraction of the rows injects bagging into the descent and decorrelates the trees (direct link with the M1 forests). Limited depth: a tree of depth \(d\) models only order-\(d\) interactions — boosting learns a sum of "simple piecewise" functions, which matches the irregularity of tabular targets (study 1.2). Early stopping: \(M\) chosen on validation — the analogue of truncating an expansion.
Example 2.3 — One iteration by hand Regression, quadratic loss, current model \(F_0 \equiv \bar{y}\). Residuals \(r_i = y_i - \bar{y}\). A depth-1 tree fit to the residuals chooses the column \(j^\star\) and the threshold \(t^\star\) minimizing the within-leaf variance of the \(r_i\) — that is, best explaining what the constant model could not say. New prediction \(F_1(x) = \bar{y} + \eta\,(a\, \mathbf{1}[x_{j^\star} \leq t^\star] + b\,\mathbf{1}[x_{j^\star} > t^\star])\), where \(a, b\) are the per-leaf residual means. The following iterations attack the residuals of \(F_1\), and so on.
What ch. 3 adds Definition 2.1 says what to fit; XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost optimize how: closed-form leaf weights via a second-order Taylor expansion, histogram-based splits, large-gradient sampling, symmetric trees, ordered statistics for categoricals. All choices that take the GBDT from "nice idea" to industrial default on tables.

3. XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost: three engineering approaches to gradient

3.1. XGBoost: the second derivative and explicit regularization

Definition 3.1 — XGBoost's regularized objective At iteration \(t\), with \(g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i} L(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i)\) and \(h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i} L(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i)\), one minimizes $$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[ g_i\, f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i\, f_t(x_i)^2 \Big] + \Omega(f_t), \qquad \Omega(f) = \gamma\, T + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2, $$ where \(f_t\) is a tree with \(T\) leaves of weights \(w_1, \dots, w_T\). This is the second-order Taylor expansion of the loss, exact for the quadratic loss, very good for the log-loss.
Theorem 3.2 — Optimal weights and split gain (XGBoost) For a fixed tree, write \(G_j = \sum_{i \in \text{leaf } j} g_i\) and \(H_j = \sum_{i \in \text{leaf } j} h_i\). Then $$ w_j^\star = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}, \qquad \mathcal{L}^\star = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda} + \gamma T, $$ and splitting a parent node \(P\) into \(L, R\) improves the objective by $$ \mathrm{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma. $$
The objective factorizes per leaf: \(\sum_j [\,w_j G_j + \tfrac{1}{2} w_j^2 (H_j + \lambda)\,] + \gamma T\) (the \(\lambda\) completes the square). Each term is a trinomial in \(w_j\) with minimum \(w_j^\star = -G_j/(H_j + \lambda)\), of value \(-\tfrac{1}{2}G_j^2/(H_j + \lambda)\). Hence \(\mathcal{L}^\star\). A split replaces leaf \(P\) (sums \(G_P = G_L + G_R\), \(H_P = H_L + H_R\)) with two leaves: the gain is \(\mathcal{L}^\star_P - \mathcal{L}^\star_{\{L,R\}}\), i.e. the stated expression; one splits only if Gain > 0, i.e. beyond the "price" \(\gamma\). ∎
The other pieces of XGBoost Weighted quantile sketch: proposing split thresholds via quantiles weighted by the \(h_i\) (points with high curvature count more), with an approximation guarantee. Sparsity-aware: each node learns a "default direction" for missing values. Column/row sampling: random-forest-style bagging applied to boosting. The whole made XGBoost (2016) the reference engine of tabular competitions from 2015 onward.

3.2. LightGBM: histograms, GOSS, EFB

Definition 3.3 — Histogram-based splits Each numerical feature is pre-discretized into \(B\) bins (typically 255); the \((g_i, h_i)\) are accumulated per bin, and the candidate thresholds are the bin boundaries. Cost per feature: \(O(n)\) to build the histogram, instead of \(O(n \log n)\) for sorting; in memory, the child histogram is derived from the parent by subtraction. The approximation error is bounded by the bin width — imperceptible in practice.
Definition 3.4 — GOSS (Gradient-based One-Side Sampling) Examples with small gradients are well predicted; one keeps the \(a \cdot n\) examples with the largest \(|g_i|\) (set \(A\)), randomly samples \(b \cdot n\) examples from the rest (\(B'\)), and amplifies their gradients by a factor \((1-a)/b\). The split gain is computed on this weighted selection.
Proposition 3.5 — GOSS (approximately) preserves the gain With the weights \(\tilde{g}_i = \frac{1-a}{b}\, g_i\) on \(B'\), the estimator \(\tilde{G}_j = \sum_{i \in A \cap j} g_i + \sum_{i \in B' \cap j} \tilde{g}_i\) is an (approximately) unbiased estimator of \(G_j = \sum_{i \in j} g_i\), and likewise for the \(H_j\) — hence the gain of theorem 3.2 is estimated consistently.
Write \(G_j = G_j^{A} + G_j^{\bar{A}}\). Uniform sampling of \(B'\) in \(\bar{A}\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i \in B' \cap j} \tilde{g}_i] = \frac{1-a}{b}\, \mathbb{E}[\sum_{i \in B' \cap j} g_i] = \frac{1-a}{b} \cdot \frac{b}{1-a} G_j^{\bar{A}} = G_j^{\bar{A}}\), hence the expectation \(G_j^{A} + G_j^{\bar{A}} = G_j\). The rest follows by replacing the sums with their estimators in the gain formula (second-order bias, negligible compared to the gain itself). ∎
Definition 3.6 — EFB (Exclusive Feature Bundling) In sparse data (massive one-hot), two columns are rarely nonzero simultaneously: they can share a "merged" bin. One builds a conflict graph (edge if frequent co-nonzero), greedily colors it to form groups of exclusive features; each bundle becomes a single histogram column. Combined with GOSS, this is the pair that gives LightGBM its speed gains.

3.3. CatBoost: oblivious trees and ordered statistics

Definition 3.7 — Oblivious trees An oblivious tree applies the same split to all nodes of a given level: depth \(d\) gives exactly \(d\) tests \((j_1, t_1), \dots, (j_d, t_d)\) and the result is indexed like an array of size \(2^d\). Advantages: evaluation in \(O(d)\), trivially vectorized execution, and strong regularization (higher bias, lower variance — the same logic as the limited depth of ch. 2).
Definition 3.8 — Ordered target statistics For a categorical \(c\) and a random permutation \(\sigma\) of the rows, the encoded value of row \(i\) uses only earlier rows: $$ \hat{x}_i = \frac{\sum_{\sigma(k) < \sigma(i)} \mathbf{1}[c_k = c_i]\, y_k + a\, p}{\sum_{\sigma(k) < \sigma(i)} \mathbf{1}[c_k = c_i] + a}, $$ with \(p\) a global prior (target mean) and \(a\) a smoothing weight. This is target encoding (ch. 4) without leakage: row \(i\) is never encoded with its own target. Ordered boosting generalizes the idea to residuals: each example is scored only by models that have not seen it — correcting the "prediction shift" of classical GBDTs (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018).

4. Encoding columns: categorical and numerical

On tables, a massive share of the performance is decided before the model: GBDTs barely digest massive one-hots, networks require clean numerical inputs. This chapter surveys the encodings, with the central pitfall: target leakage.

Definition 4.1 — Target encoding (mean encoding) For a high-cardinality categorical \(c\) (postal codes, SKUs, users), one replaces the category with a statistic of the target; smoothed version of Micci-Barreca (2001): $$ \mathrm{enc}(v) = \frac{n_v\, \bar{y}_v + m\, \bar{y}}{n_v + m}, $$ where \(\bar{y}_v\) is the target mean within category \(v\), \(n_v\) its count, \(\bar{y}\) the global mean and \(m\) a pseudo-count: rare categories are pulled toward the global prior.
Example 4.2 — Leakage, demonstrated Suppose a column "identifier" unique per row (\(n_v = 1\) everywhere) and a target independent of everything (pure noise). Naive target encoding (computed on the whole train set, row included) gives \(\mathrm{enc}(v_i) = y_i\): the feature contains exactly the target. Any model reaches a training AUC of 1.0, while the test AUC falls back to 0.5. The model has learned nothing: it copies. With intermediate cardinality, the leakage is partial (\(\mathrm{enc}(v)\) mixes \(y_i\) and \(\bar{y}_v\)) — even more dangerous, because invisible on naive metrics.
Proposition 4.3 — Anti-leakage: out-of-fold and random order Two standard repairs: (i) K-fold out-of-fold: split the train set into \(K\) blocks, encode each block with the statistics of the other \(K-1\); (ii) random order (CatBoost, def. 3.8): each row encoded using only earlier rows. In both cases, \(\mathrm{enc}(v_i)\) becomes independent of \(y_i\) conditionally on the category — the leakage disappears at the cost of a slight estimation noise, absorbed by the smoothing \(m\).
Definition 4.4 — One-hot vs embeddings One-hot: \(K\) binary columns: no similarity assumption between categories, but dimension \(K\) (disastrous for large \(K\), and cf. EFB). Entity embeddings (Guo & Berkhahn, 2016): each category \(v\) receives a learned vector \(z_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) (\(d \ll K\)), via a table embedded in the network: categories that behave similarly with respect to the target move closer — the embeddings of "Bavaria" and "Baden-Württemberg" (Rossmann competition) end up neighbors. This is the natural bridge between categoricals and networks; GBDTs, for their part, prefer native target encoding or splits by category partitions.
Definition 4.5 — Numerical feature embeddings Three families to "textualize" a scalar in the eyes of a network (Gorishniy et al., NeurIPS 2022):
  • Linear: \(e_j = b_j + x_j w_j\) (the FT-Transformer tokenizer, ch. 5);
  • Periodic: \(e_j = [\sin(2\pi c_j x_j), \cos(2\pi c_j x_j)]_{c_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)}\), parameters \(\sigma\) (or the \(c_j\)) learned — a kernel-type building block;
  • Piecewise (piecewise-linear binning): \(x_j \to\) interpolation coordinates over \(B\) bins defined by quantiles — a controlled return to the "histogram logic" of GBDTs.
Massive empirical result: a simple MLP with good numerical embeddings rivals sophisticated architectures.
Engineering, not magic Networks inherit the M1 reflexes: standardize per column (never globally), transform heavy distributions (rank/quantile), extract dates (day of week, holidays, \(\sin/\cos\) cyclicity of the hour), cross business columns (income/spending), and handle missing values by imputation + indicator (networks have no XGBoost-style default direction — GBDTs do; cf. ch. 3). Proposition 1.3 reminds us why all this only benefits networks: trees do not care.

5. Deep tabular: regularized MLPs and FT-Transformer

Two deep answers to the dominance of trees: better regularize MLPs, and treat the columns as tokens of a transformer — attention playing the role of a differentiable interaction engine.

Definition 5.1 — Feature tokenizer (FT-Transformer) Each column becomes a token of dimension \(d\): for a numerical one, \(e_j = b_j + x_j\, w_j\) (\(b_j, w_j \in \mathbb{R}^d\) learned); for a categorical one, a row of an embedding table. To this one adds a \([\mathrm{CLS}]\) token. The following block is a standard transformer (multi-head attention, cf. Deep Learning module): $$ \mathrm{Att}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V, $$ computed between features of the same row. The prediction is read from \([\mathrm{CLS}]\) after \(L\) blocks.
Why feature-wise attention changes things An MLP must sample interactions via its weights; a tree builds them greedily; the transformer learns, for each pair of columns, a conditional interaction weight (the heads saturate on useful pairs and "switch off" useless columns — a partial answer to the sensitivity to uninformative features of study 1.2). Gorishniy et al. (NeurIPS 2021) show that the FT-Transformer is the most consistent tabular network, at an equal tuning budget — and that the tokenizer/def. 4.5 accounts for much of it.
Example 5.2 — Two other families TabNet (Arik & Pfister, AAAI 2021): per-instance selection of a small group of features (sparse masks learned via a sparsemax) at each step — a differentiable echo of the trees' "column selection", with the masks offered as interpretability. NODE (Popov et al., 2020): differentiable oblivious trees (entmax splits over thresholds) trained end-to-end — boosting rewritten as a network. Regularized MLPs (Kadra et al., 2021): a well-tuned "cocktail" of regularizations suffices to hold the comparison — confirming that on tables, the essence of the game is played on inductive biases and tuning.
Peculiarities of tables (summary)
  • Heterogeneous columns: mixed types, scales, semantics — no "natural" convolution kernel;
  • No translation invariance nor locality: permuting pixels changes nothing in an image, permuting columns changes everything; the rotation experiment of ch. 1 shows that this absence of invariance is a richness (axis-aligned structure) that MLPs do not exploit;
  • Irregular distributions: skewness, heavy tails, multimodality, "round" regulatory thresholds;
  • Many uninformative features and informative missing values;
  • Small/medium \(n\) by vision standards: the risk of overfitting the architecture is real (cf. TabZilla, ch. 9).

6. TabPFN: learning tables in-context

The 2023-2025 paradigm shift: no longer train a model on the table, but train a transformer once and for all on millions of synthetic tables, then predict in-context — the tabular analogue of LLM few-shot learning.

Definition 6.1 — Prior-fitted network (PFN) Let a prior \(\mathfrak{p}\) over datasets: a law over functions \(f\) (and noises, features, sizes) from which synthetic datasets \(\mathcal{D}_{sim}\) are drawn. A PFN is a transformer \(q_\theta\) trained to predict, for an example \(x\) presented next to a set \(\mathcal{D}_{train}\) inside its input window, the law \(p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_{train})\). Müller et al. (ICLR 2022) show that such a network approximates the Bayesian posterior predictive $$ p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y \mid x, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\theta $$ in a single forward pass: the transformer's in-context learning is a form of amortized Bayesian inference. Cf. LLMs module (M2) ch. 3 for in-context learning on the language side.
Definition 6.2 — TabPFN's causal prior TabPFN (Hollmann et al., ICLR 2023; v2: Nature 2025) draws its synthetic tables from random structural causal models: random DAG over \(p\) variables, nonlinear mechanisms (networks, piecewise transformations), heterogeneous noises, then monotone marginal transformations — mimicking the "irregular distributions" of ch. 5 — and adding uninformative features and missing values. By training on millions of such tables, the model learns to learn: in use, one deposits train + the row to predict in the context; no gradient descent is run on the user's table.
Proposition 6.3 — What in-context buys (i) Zero pipeline hyperparameters (no trees, rates, or depths): the "tuning" is amortized in pre-training; (ii) seconds of inference for tables up to \(\sim\!10,000\) rows (v2), classification and regression, missing values and outliers tolerated; (iii) free uncertainty: \(q_\theta(y \mid x, \mathcal{D})\) is a predictive law — usable for abstention. Trade-offs: a context window (beyond \(\sim\!10,000\) rows, fall back to "classical" training minibatches), limited types (no rich textual columns; cf. ch. 7), and a dependence on the proximity between the real table and the prior of def. 6.2.
Example 6.4 — A shifting benchmark On the reference suites of medium-sized datasets, v2 (Nature 2025) beats tuned GBDTs on average — the first credible "deep" victory in this regime, three years after Grinsztajn et al. Ch. 9 nonetheless requires reading these results with a symmetric protocol (GBDT tuning budget, same folds, same seeds): the "trees vs foundations" debate remains open for large \(n\), textual columns, and industrial constraints.

7. LLMs and tables: serialization, text-to-SQL

An LLM does not read a table: it reads text. Everything goes through a serialization — and this engineering choice is as decisive as the prompt itself.

Definition 7.1 — Usual serializations of a row For a row \((x_1, \dots, x_p)\) with named columns \(c_1, \dots, c_p\): CSV aligned on a header; JSON key-value; Markdown; textual template ("The patient is \(x_{age}\) years old, receives \(x_{dose}\) mg…"); the "schema" (column names, types, description) can precede the series. Two critical properties: the column order influences the prediction (the model is sensitive to token positions), and the column names activate pre-training knowledge ("country", "dosage") — a semantic prior that no GBDT has.
Study 7.2 — TabLLM (Hegselmann et al., 2023) In few-shot (typically \(\leq 64\) examples), an LLM (T0/GPT-3.5, textual serializations) predicts tables better than dedicated deep methods and rivals GBDTs; the advantage reverses as soon as data abounds — the per-row token cost becomes prohibitive and the semantic prior no longer compensates for the statistical capacity. The LLM wins where every example is expensive (medical annotation, industry); the GBDT where data is massive.
Definition 7.3 — Text-to-SQL and schema linking When the table is a database, the LLM becomes an interface: producing SQL from a question in natural language. Schema linking denotes the step of aligning question ↔ schema: entities of the question to cells of the tables, terms to candidate columns/tables, then plausible joins. Benchmarks: Spider (Yu et al., 2018; clean schemas, academic questions) then BIRD (Li et al., 2023; 95 large "dirty" databases, dirty values, external knowledge, query efficiency requirement) — on which the performance drop of models between the two worlds is precisely measured. 
# Three postures of the LLM facing a table
# 1) Predict: serialized row -> label (TabLLM, few-shot)
prompt = f"Column 'dosage_mg': {x['dose']}; 'age': {x['age']}; ...\nAnswer (0/1)?"
# 2) Engineer: propose features / clean / document the columns
#    (the LLM writes the feature engineering code, the GBDT predicts)
# 3) Query: natural language question -> SQL (schema linking + BIRD)
question = "top 5 clients by margin in 2025, excluding refunds"
# -> SELECT c.id, SUM(o.marge) FROM ... WHERE ... GROUP BY ... ORDER BY ... LIMIT 5;
Structural limits Tokenization of numbers (scalars are chopped into pieces — fine arithmetic remains fragile); context window (millions of rows will never fit); confidentiality (sending customer rows to an API; cf. the Explainability, Security and Privacy of LLMs module); cost and non-determinism in production (cf. ML Ops module). Hence the dominant posture: hybrid — the LLM prepares, summarizes, links, programs; the tabular model (GBDT/TabPFN) predicts.

8. Synthetic tabular data

Definition 8.1 — The fidelity-utility-privacy triangle A tabular generator \(\mathcal{D}_{syn} \sim G(\mathcal{D}_{real})\) is judged on three axes in tension: fidelity (marginals and dependencies reproduced; KS/TV distances per column, correlations, detectability by a real-vs-synthetic classifier), utility (does a model trained on the synthetic perform on the real? the train-synthetic-test-real protocol, TSTR), and privacy (no synthetic row should copy an individual; nearest-neighbor distance metrics and membership inference attacks — cf. ch. 8 of the Security and Robustness module).
Example 8.2 — Three families of generators GAN/VAE: CTGAN/TVAE (Xu et al., 2019) — "mode-based" normalization of multimodal columns (GMM) and a conditional classifier for categoricals. Diffusion: TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., ICML 2023) — Gaussian diffusion for numericals, multinomial diffusion for categoricals (cf. Image Synthesis module for the diffusion foundation), with missing-value imputation as a bonus. Language models: GReaT (Borisov et al., ICLR 2023) — one fine-tunes an LM on rows serialized as text with random column order; generating = sampling conditioned completions — the most faithful on dependencies, the most exposed to memorization (cf. LLMs module for LM memorization).
Proposition 8.3 — Synthetic ≠ anonymous A generator trained by maximum likelihood memorizes: rare rows (atypical individuals) can be reproduced almost verbatim. The formal safeguard is differential privacy (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 5): training the generator in DP (Gaussian mechanism on gradients) guarantees an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) — at the price of a measurable fidelity loss. In practice one combines: DP when the guarantee is required, membership inference audits and nearest-record distances otherwise, and documentation of both (regulation; cf. ML Ops module).
Secondary uses Data augmentation to rebalance rare classes, pipeline testing ("twin datasets" for ML regression tests), cross-entity sharing (banks/suppliers without exchanging real rows), and — back to ch. 6 — synthetic generation is also the training technology of PFNs: TabPFN's prior is nothing other than a controlled causal table generator. The loop is closed between "learning from synthetic" and "generating synthetic".

9. Honest benchmarking

The "trees vs deep vs foundations" debate has produced more contradictory conclusions than any other area of ML — almost always because of asymmetric protocols. This chapter sets the method required in all the module's labs (an extension of ch. 9 of the Machine Learning module).

Protocol rules
  1. Same folds, same preprocessing for everyone: seeded splits, encodings computed only inside the CV loop (the OOF target encoding of prop. 4.3 is inside the CV, never before);
  2. Symmetric tuning budget: same number of trials, same search, same selection metric — TabZilla shows that the tuning budget dominates the family choice (ch. 1);
  3. Multiple seeds (≥ 5) and report mean ± standard deviation: on medium tables, the inter-seed std of a GBDT can approach the gap between families;
  4. Counted cost: training + inference + tuning time (TabPFN's "zero-tuning" is compared to a tuned XGBoost — but also to default XGBoost);
  5. Dataset deduplication: OpenML suites share variants of the same dataset — a "win by average" benchmark may count the same dataset three times;
  6. Test reuse: forbidden — a test set that served to select a family no longer measures anything (benchmark overfitting).
Example 9.1 — Calibrating an "X% better" On 20 OpenML datasets: mean GBDT AUC 0.812 ± 0.004 (5 seeds), FT-Transformer 0.807 ± 0.006, TabPFN 0.818 ± 0.000 (deterministic). Before writing "TabPFN wins": (i) paired per-dataset test (permutation or corrected t); (ii) check that the GBDT had its budget; (iii) look at the distribution — gaining 0.006 everywhere is not gaining 0.06 on half the datasets and losing on the other half; (iv) report the total time to prediction. The honest conclusion is often "statistically tied, choose by cost and constraint" (ch. 10).
