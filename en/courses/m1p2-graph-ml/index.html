0. Refresher: graph theory

L3 prerequisites — the "graph" object (cf. Refresher CS)
  • Definitions: \(G = (V, E)\); directed/undirected, weighted; degree \(d_v\), neighbors \(\mathcal{N}(v)\), adjacency matrix \(A\), degree matrix \(D = \mathrm{diag}(d_v)\). Paths, connectivity, components, cycles, DAGs.
  • Euler and Königsberg (1736): crossing the 7 bridges is impossible because more than two vertices have odd degree — the first theorem of graph theory; the abstraction (nodes + edges) founded the discipline.
  • Linear algebra: \((A^k)_{ij}\) counts the paths of length \(k\); eigenvalues of \(A\) = spectrum of the graph; symmetric matrices diagonalizable in an orthonormal basis (algebra reminder).
  • Stochastic matrices: \(P = D^{-1}A\) = random walk; stationary distribution, PageRank \(\pi \propto (I - \alpha P)^{-1}\mathbf{1}\).
Definition 0.1 — Isomorphism, automorphism, subgraph Two graphs \(G = (V, E)\) and \(G' = (V', E')\) are isomorphic if there exists a bijection \(\pi : V \to V'\) such that \((u, v) \in E \iff (\pi(u), \pi(v)) \in E'\). An automorphism is an isomorphism of \(G\) onto itself; their set forms a group \(\mathrm{Aut}(G)\). A subgraph of \(G\) is formed of \(S \subseteq V\) and a subset of \(E \cap (S \times S)\); the induced subgraph keeps all the internal edges. Deciding isomorphism is a problem known neither NP-complete nor polynomial; the Weisfeiler-Lehman test (M2 module, ch. 1) is its fundamental heuristic — and the expressivity bound of GNNs.
Proposition 0.2 — Connectivity and shortest paths The relation "\(u \sim v\) if there exists a path from \(u\) to \(v\)" is an equivalence relation; its classes are the connected components. Between two vertices of the same component, every path of minimal length (shortest path) is a simple path (without repeated vertex).
Reflexivity (path of length 0), symmetry (undirected graph: reverse the path) and transitivity (concatenate) are immediate. If a path from \(u\) to \(v\) visits a vertex \(w\) twice, we delete the cycle between the two visits: the path strictly shortens. A path of minimal length therefore cannot contain a cycle, so every vertex appears there only once: it is simple, of length \(\leq n - 1\). ∎
Theorem 0.3 (spectral theorem) and counting by the trace Every real symmetric matrix \(S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) writes \(S = U \Lambda U^\top\) with \(U\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda\) real diagonal. For the adjacency of a simple graph, three counting identities follow: $$\mathrm{tr}(A) = 0, \qquad \mathrm{tr}(A^2) = 2|E|, \qquad \mathrm{tr}(A^3) = 6\,|\{\text{triangles}\}|.$$
\(\mathrm{tr}(A^2) = \sum_i (A^2)_{ii} = \sum_i d_i\), each edge contributing to two degrees. \((A^3)_{ii}\) counts the closed paths of length 3 through \(i\): these are exactly the triangles containing \(i\), each traversed in both directions from \(i\). Each triangle \(\{i, j, k\}\) thus produces 2 closed paths for each of its 3 vertices, i.e. 6 countings; conversely a closed path of length 3 \(i \to j \to k \to i\) visits three pairwise distinct, pairwise adjacent vertices. ∎
Immediate application in learning: counting triangles (motifs) by matrix products — the basic ingredient of the structural features of ch. 9 and of the expressivity of the M2 module (ch. 1).
Definition 0.4 — The Laplacian of a graph $$L = D - A, \qquad \text{or normalized: } L_{\mathrm{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2} A\, D^{-1/2}.$$ For every vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) (\(n = |V|\)): $$x^\top L x = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (x_u - x_v)^2 \ \geq\ 0$$ — \(L\) is positive semi-definite; its eigenvalues \(0 = \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_n\) encode connectivity; \(\lambda_2\) (the spectral gap, Fiedler 1973) measures the difficulty of cutting the graph, and the number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components.
\(x^\top D x = \sum_v d_v x_v^2\) and \(x^\top A x = 2 \sum_{(u,v) \in E} x_u x_v\) (symmetric matrix); hence \(x^\top L x = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (x_u^2 + x_v^2 - 2 x_u x_v)\). For the multiplicity of 0: reordering the vertices by components makes \(A\) (and \(L\)) block-diagonal; each block \(L_C\) has the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_C\) as kernel, since \(x^\top L_C x = 0\) forces \(x\) constant on each connected component (the sum of squares cancels edge by edge, and connectivity propagates the constant — Prop. 0.2). ∎
Example 0.5 — The spectrum tells the story Path of \(n\) vertices: \(\lambda_k = 2(1 - \cos\frac{k\pi}{n})\); complete graph: \(0\) then \(n\) (multiplicity \(n-1\)); cycle: eigenvalues of \(2\cos\). A "two-community" graph has a small \(\lambda_2\); the Fiedler eigenvector separates the communities — this is the heart of spectral clustering, developed in ch. 4 (direct bridge with the PCA of the Machine Learning module).
Proposition 0.6 — Random walks: stationarity and ergodicity Let \(G\) be connected undirected, \(P = D^{-1}A\), \(m = |E|\). The distribution \(\pi_v = d_v / (2m)\) is stationary: \(\pi^\top P = \pi^\top\). If moreover \(G\) is not bipartite, the chain is irreducible and aperiodic, hence ergodic: $$P^t(x, \cdot) \xrightarrow[t \to \infty]{} \pi \qquad \text{whatever the starting point } x.$$
Detailed balance: \(\pi_u P_{uv} = \frac{d_u}{2m} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{1}[(u,v) \in E]}{d_u} = \frac{\mathbb{1}[(u,v) \in E]}{2m}\), a quantity symmetric in \(u, v\); summing over \(u\), \((\pi^\top P)_v = \pi_v\). Irreducibility: connectivity makes every vertex reachable. Periodicity: on a bipartite graph, every walk has a fixed parity (\(P^t(x, x) = 0\) for odd \(t\)); conversely, every edge \((u,v)\) of a non-bipartite graph belongs to an odd cycle, providing returns of periods \(d(u,v)+1\) and \(d(u,v)-1\), coprime — period 1. The convergence theorem for Markov chains (cf. Refresher Statistics) concludes. ∎
Practical consequence: the walks of DeepWalk (ch. 3) "mix" the distribution toward \(\pi \propto\) degrees — hence the bias of embeddings toward hubs, partly corrected by node2vec and by NetMF.
Definition 0.7 — Modularity (Newman & Girvan, 2004) For a partition \(\mathcal{C} = \{C_1, \dots, C_k\}\) of the vertices: $$Q(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \Big[ A_{ij} - \frac{d_i\, d_j}{2m} \Big]\, \delta(c_i, c_j),$$ where \(c_i\) is the community of \(i\) and \(\delta\) the indicator. \(Q\) compares the observed density of intra-community edges to the expected one under the configuration model (edges redistributed while preserving degrees, term \(d_i d_j / 2m\)). Maximization sketch: maximizing \(Q\) exactly is NP-hard; Louvain does it greedily — iteratively merging the (community, neighbor) pairs that most increase \(\Delta Q = \frac{1}{2m}[\Sigma_{in} - \frac{(\Sigma_{tot} + d_i)^2}{4m}]\dots\), then contracting — hence hierarchies of communities used as-is by GraphRAG (ch. 11). Known limit: resolution — \(Q\) cannot "see" communities smaller than ~\(\sqrt{2m}\) edges; SBM (ch. 9) offers a better-founded statistical alternative.

1. Why graphs?

Real-world data is relational: the structure carries information that i.i.d. methods ignore. Social networks (friendships), molecules (bonds), transaction networks (fraud!), enterprise knowledge graphs (customers, products, processes), computer networks, citation clusters, legal ontologies… All are naturally graphs, often heterogeneous (typed nodes/edges: "buys", "owns", "works with") and dynamic (timestamped edges: transactions second by second). Also typically: few labels (a few dozen annotated nodes out of millions) — the semi-supervised regime of ch. 5 — and distributions biased by sampling (the observed edges are not a uniform sample of the interactions).

The LLGA positioning LLMs excel at text but struggle with: structured relations, factual fidelity (hallucinations), and non-textual data. Graphs fill exactly these gaps:
  • GraphRAG: connecting the LLM to structured proprietary data — today the most effective approach for making an LLM reliable in the enterprise (ch. 11);
  • GNN + LLM: generative AI on structured data (generating molecules, plans, recommendations);
  • Industrial applications: pharma/biotech (molecules = graphs), finance (transaction networks, systemic risk), recommendation, supply chain, multi-agent systems (agent workflows are DAGs — RL module).
Common thread of the master's program Three places of the graph in generative AI: inside the model (the attention of transformers is a complete dynamic graph — Deep Learning module), for the model (GraphRAG, structural encoding — ch. 11), between models (agent orchestration = DAG programming). This module lays the foundations of all three; the M2 module "Advanced GNN" extends each one.

Definition 2.1 — Permutation invariance and equivariance A graph has neither a fixed size nor a canonical order of the nodes: the model must be permutation-invariant (graph output): \(f(P X, P A P^\top) = f(X, A)\), or permutation-equivariant (per-node output): \(f(PX, PAP^\top) = P f(X, A)\), for every permutation matrix \(P\). CNNs (grid), RNNs (sequence) violate these constraints — hence dedicated architectures (ch. 5) and the whole expressivity theory of the M2 module (ch. 1).
Transductive vs inductive — a distinction that structures the whole module
  • Transductive: training and testing on the same graph (embeddings ch. 3); impossible to predict for a node arriving after training;
  • Inductive: the model (its weights) reapplies to new graphs (GraphSAGE, GNNs ch. 5) — indispensable in production;
  • Structural semi-supervised: on Cora (2,708 nodes), 140 labels suffice — the structure relays the information (homophily hypothesis: neighbors share the label; when it fails, one needs "virtual" edges or heterophilous models).

3. Node embeddings

Objective Learn \(z_v \in \mathbb{R}^d\) such that the similarity of the embeddings reflects proximity in the graph: \(\mathrm{sim}(z_u, z_v) \approx P(u \text{ neighbor of } v)\) — an implicit factorization of the adjacency matrix (or of a matrix of derived proximities), in contrast with the GNNs of ch. 5 which condition the embedding on the features \(X\).
DeepWalk (Perozzi et al., 2014) and node2vec (Grover & Leskovec, 2016)
  1. generate random walks from each node (length \(\ell\), \(r\) walks per node); node2vec biases the walk with \((p, q)\) — developed below;
  2. apply word2vec skip-gram to the walks: the "sentences" are sequences of nodes, the window \(b\) defines the (center, context) pairs;
  3. afterwards: any vector method (k-means, classification…).
The random walk defines proximity: two often co-visited nodes get close embeddings — the NetMF theory (Qiu et al., 2018) shows retrospectively that DeepWalk factorizes a deterministic matrix of walks (Theorem 3.3).
Derivation 3.1 — Skip-gram with negative sampling: the loss and its gradient For a (center \(c\), context \(o\)) pair and \(K\) negatives \(v_1, \dots, v_K\) drawn from a noise distribution \(P_n\) (unigram law raised to the power 3/4, as in NLP): $$\mathcal{L}(c, o) = -\log \sigma(z_o^\top z_c) \; - \; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-z_{v_k}^\top z_c),$$ with \(\sigma(s) = 1/(1 + e^{-s})\). Writing \(s_k = z_{v_k}^\top z_c\) and differentiating \(\frac{d}{ds}[-\log\sigma(s)] = \sigma(s) - 1\): $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_c} = \big(\sigma(z_o^\top z_c) - 1\big)\, z_o \; + \; \sum_{k=1}^K \sigma(s_k)\, z_{v_k}, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_o} = \big(\sigma(z_o^\top z_c) - 1\big)\, z_c.$$ Reading: the term \(-z_o\) (since \(\sigma < 1\)) attracts the context toward the center, each negative repels with a weight \(\sigma(s_k) \in ]0,1[\) — strong if the negative is already too close. Stochastic gradient descent: cost per pair \(\mathcal{O}(K d)\), independent of \(n\) — this is what made word2vec (and hence DeepWalk) scalable; the hierarchical softmax trick replaces the \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) of the full softmax by \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\).
Derivation 3.2 — node2vec: the biases \(p\) and \(q\) via transition probabilities At step \(i\), the walk comes from \(t\), is at \(v\); for the candidate \(x \in \mathcal{N}(v)\): $$\pi_{tx} = \frac{w_{tx}}{Z}\, \alpha_{pq}(t, x), \qquad \alpha_{pq}(t, x) = \begin{cases} 1/p & \text{if } d_{tx} = 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } d_{tx} = 1 \\ 1/q & \text{if } d_{tx} = 2 \end{cases}$$ (\(d_{tx}\) = distance in the graph; \(Z\) normalizes over \(\mathcal{N}(v)\); in a simple graph, \(d_{tx} \leq 2\) for every \(x \in \mathcal{N}(v) \cup \{t\}\) if \(t\) and \(v\) are adjacent). Three regimes:
  • \(p \lt 1\): return toward \(t\) favored — the walk stays in the close neighborhood (BFS): two nodes with analogous structural roles (hubs, bridges) get close embeddings — structural equivalence;
  • \(q \lt 1\): vertices at \(d_{tx} = 2\) are favored — the walk moves away (DFS): two nodes of the same community/cluster are close — homophily;
  • \(p = q = 1\): uniform random walk — DeepWalk is a special case of node2vec.
The qualitative proof: with \(p \to 0\), the probability of returning to \(t\) dominates and the walk samples the 2-hop neighborhood of \(t\) almost uniformly; with \(q \to 0\), the chain avoids \(t\) and behaves like a large-scale exploratory walk — find these behaviors again in exercise 3.
Theorem 3.3 — NetMF: DeepWalk as matrix factorization (Qiu et al., 2018) As the window \(b\) and the number of walks grow, DeepWalk converges to the (truncated, element-wise) factorization of the matrix: $$M = \log\Big( \frac{\mathrm{vol}(G)}{b\, T} \Big( \sum_{r=1}^{T} P^r \Big) D^{-1} \Big), \qquad \mathrm{vol}(G) = \sum_v d_v = 2m,$$ that is, we look for \(Z Z^\top \approx M\); \(T\) plays the role of the effective horizon of the walks. Consequences: (i) a theoretical bridge — DeepWalk, LINE, PTE and node2vec are all factorizations of variants of \(M\) (hence the name "NetMF", Network Embedding as Matrix Factorization); (ii) a deterministic algorithm — factorizing \(M\) directly (truncated SVD) matches or beats DeepWalk without drawing a single walk; (iii) a diagnostic — the target matrix is built on \(P = D^{-1}A\): the embeddings encode the walks, not the features, and remain transductive (a new node requires recomputing everything — direct motivation for the inductive GNNs of ch. 5).
The "word2vec on the graph" intuition — and its converse This is the historical NLP ↔ graphs bridge: a corpus of walks ≈ a corpus of sentences. The teaching team pushed the reverse idea — Graph of Words (ch. 10): representing a document by a graph of co-occurring terms and classifying it with graph tools. Both directions converge today in LLMs on graphs (ch. 11). To go beyond walks (with repetitions) toward paths (simple), see the Path Neural Networks (ch. 10; M2, ch. 1 and 8).
Example 3.4 — Karate Club: complete code Zachary's graph (34 nodes, 78 edges, 2 factions at the club split) is the "MNIST of graphs". 
import numpy as np, networkx as nx
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import normalized_mutual_info_score

G = nx.karate_club_graph()
y = np.array([0 if G.nodes[v]["club"] == "Mr. Hi" else 1 for v in G])

def biased_walk(G, v, p, q, length):
    walk, prev = [v], None
    for _ in range(length):
        cand = list(G.neighbors(walk[-1]))
        probs = []
        for x in cand:
            d = 0 if x == prev else nx.shortest_path_length(G, prev, x) if prev is not None else 1
            probs.append({0: 1/p, 1: 1.0, 2: 1/q}[d])
        probs = np.array(probs) / sum(probs)
        prev, nxt = walk[-1], np.random.choice(cand, p=probs)
        walk.append(nxt)
    return walk

walks = [ [str(u) for u in biased_walk(G, v, p=1, q=0.25, length=30)]
          for _ in range(80) for v in G.nodes() ]
model = Word2Vec(walks, vector_size=64, window=5, min_count=0, sg=1, epochs=10)
Z = np.array([model.wv[str(v)] for v in sorted(G.nodes())])
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Z)
print("NMI :", normalized_mutual_info_score(y, km.labels_))   # ~0.5-0.8 depending on p, q, seed

4. Spectral filters and spectral clustering

Definition 4.1 — Graph Fourier transform Let \(L = U \Lambda U^\top\) (spectral decomposition, \(U\) orthogonal, columns \(u_1, \dots, u_n\)). For a signal \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) carried by the nodes: $$\hat x = U^\top x \quad (\text{analysis}), \qquad x = U \hat x \quad (\text{synthesis}).$$ The "frequencies" are the eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) — small = slow variations over the graph (low frequencies), large = local alternations (high frequencies). This is the convolution of the Signal Processing module, with the Laplacian in place of \(-i\frac{d}{dt}\): \(L\) plays the role of the differential operator there.
Theorem 4.2 — Variational characterization of \(\lambda_2\) (Fiedler) For a connected graph: $$\lambda_2 \;=\; \min_{x \neq 0,\ x \perp \mathbf{1}} \frac{x^\top L x}{x^\top x},$$ attained at the Fiedler vector \(u_2\). The smaller \(\lambda_2\), the more there exists a quasi-constant signal "almost orthogonal" to \(\mathbf{1}\) — that is, two large blocks of weakly connected opposite values.
Write \(x = \sum_i c_i u_i\) (orthonormal basis); \(x \perp \mathbf{1}\) forces \(c_1 = 0\) since \(u_1 = \mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}\) (the kernel of \(L\) is exactly spanned by \(\mathbf{1}\), Prop. 0.2 of the multiplicity computation). Then \(x^\top L x = \sum_{i \geq 2} \lambda_i c_i^2\) and \(x^\top x = \sum_{i \geq 2} c_i^2\); the quotient is a weighted average of the \(\lambda_i, i \geq 2\), hence lower-bounded by \(\lambda_2\), with equality for \(x = u_2\). ∎
Example 4.3 — Fiedler vector on two linked cliques (exact computation) Two \(K_5\)s (\(\{u, x_1, \dots, x_4\}\) and \(\{v, y_1, \dots, y_4\}\)) linked by the bridge \((u, v)\). By symmetry, look for an antisymmetric eigenvector: \(x_i = a\) on the 4 non-bridge nodes of the left, \(u \mapsto b\), and opposite values on the right. The equations \(L x = \lambda x\) at a non-bridge node (degree 4) and at \(u\) (degree 5) give: $$\lambda a = a - b \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lambda b = 6b - 4a \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 2 = 0.$$ Hence \(\lambda_2 = \frac{7 - \sqrt{41}}{2} \approx 0.298\) and \(b = (1 - \lambda_2)\, a \approx 0.70\, a\); the Fiedler vector equals, up to normalization: $$(\underbrace{1, 1, 1, 1}_{x_i}, \underbrace{0.70}_{u}, \underbrace{-0.70}_{v}, \underbrace{-1, -1, -1, -1}_{y_j})$$ — the sign exactly separates the two communities, and the bridge node takes an intermediate value. This is the picture to remember about spectral clustering.
Theorem 4.4 — Spectral clustering: complete algorithm and justification (RatioCut relaxation) Algorithm (for \(k\) communities):
  1. compute the first \(k\) eigenvectors \(u_1, \dots, u_k\) of \(L\); form \(F = [u_1 | \dots | u_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\);
  2. normalize each row of \(F\);
  3. apply k-means to the \(n\) rows of \(F\) (spectral descriptor).
Justification: the RatioCut of a partition \(\{C_1, \dots, C_k\}\) is \(\mathrm{RatioCut} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{\mathrm{cut}(C_j, \bar{C}_j)}{|C_j|}\) (cutting a small community is costly — unlike the plain minimization of the cut). Let \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\) denote the renormalized indicators \(H_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[v_i \in C_j] / \sqrt{|C_j|}\).
(i) Exactness on the discrete class. \(H^\top H = I\) (orthogonal columns of norm 1). Moreover \(h_j^\top L h_j = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} (h_j(u) - h_j(v))^2 = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \times |\{(u,v) \in E : |\{u, v\} \cap C_j| = 1\}| = \frac{\mathrm{cut}(C_j, \bar{C_j})}{|C_j|}\). So \(\mathrm{tr}(H^\top L H) = \mathrm{RatioCut}\): minimizing RatioCut = minimizing \(\mathrm{tr}(H^\top L H)\) over orthonormal indicators. (ii) Relaxation. We allow any \(F \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\) with \(F^\top F = I\); the relaxed problem \(\min_{F^\top F = I} \mathrm{tr}(F^\top L F)\) has as solution the first \(k\) eigenvectors (Ky Fan's theorem: shown by writing \(F^\top L F = \sum_{ij} (F^\top u_i)\,\lambda_i\,(u_j^\top F)\) and exchanging the order of summation, then minimizing the diagonalized coefficients). (iii) Back to the discrete. The rows of \(F\) remain close to the rows of renormalized indicators; k-means groups them. ∎
Normalized Cut variant (Shi & Malik): weighting by the volumes \(d(C_j)\) leads to the generalized problem \(L y = \lambda D y\), solved by the eigenvectors of \(L_{\mathrm{sym}}\) — this is the version used in practice (and by scikit-learn). Links: PCA (Machine Learning module — the matrix \(F\) is the spectral counterpart of the projection); SBM (ch. 9) for the statistical approach; sign ambiguities of the eigenvectors — a real problem for GNNs, solved by SignNet (M2, ch. 3).
Theorem 4.5 — ChebNet: Chebyshev polynomials (Defferrard et al., 2016) Filtering exactly costs \(y = U\, g_\theta(\Lambda)\, U^\top x\) — a full decomposition (\(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\)) specific to each graph. Trick: take \(g_\theta\) a polynomial of degree \(K\) in the Chebyshev basis. Set \(T_0(x) = 1\), \(T_1(x) = x\), \(T_{k+1}(x) = 2x T_k(x) - T_{k-1}(x)\), and \(\tilde\Lambda = \frac{2\Lambda}{\lambda_{\max}} - I\) (rescaling to \([-1, 1]\)); then $$y = \sum_{k=0}^K \theta_k\, T_k(\tilde L)\, x, \qquad \tilde L = \frac{2L}{\lambda_{\max}} - I,$$ without eigenvectors: \(K\)-localized filter, weights \(\theta\) shared by all graphs.
(i) Chebyshev recursion. For \(x = \cos\theta\), \(T_k(x) = \cos(k\theta)\); the addition formula \(\cos((k{+}1)\theta) = 2\cos\theta \cos(k\theta) - \cos((k{-}1)\theta)\) gives the recursion (it propagates to all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) by polynomial identity). (ii) Functional calculus. Let us show \(T_k(\tilde L) = U T_k(\tilde\Lambda) U^\top\): this is true for the monomials by recursion — \(\tilde L^m = (U \tilde\Lambda U^\top)^m = U \tilde\Lambda^m U^\top\), the inner products \(U^\top U\) canceling — hence for every polynomial. Thus \(y = U\, g_\theta(\tilde\Lambda)\, U^\top x = g_\theta(\tilde L)\, x\): the spectral filter is expressed as a polynomial of \(L\) applied directly to \(x\). (iii) Locality. \(\tilde L x\) at a node only combines \(x_v\) and \(\{x_u : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\); by recursion on the Chebyshev recursion (linear combination of \(\tilde L^k x\), \(k \leq K\)), the output at \(v\) depends only on the \(K\)-neighborhood. ∎
Cost: \(\mathcal{O}(K |E|)\) per application — linear in the size of the graph.
Proposition 4.6 — From spectral to spatial: the equality demonstrated Every filter that is a polynomial of degree \(K\) in \(L\) is exactly a \(K\)-localized operator, and conversely every message passing operator with \(K\) layers whose propagation is a polynomial of \(A\) realizes a polynomial spectral filter.
Forward direction: developed in (iii) above. Conversely: a local layer \(y_v = \sum_{u \in \{v\} \cup \mathcal{N}(v)} c_{uv} x_u\) with uniform weights by symmetry writes in matrix form \(y = \sum_{k \leq 1} c_k\, \Pi_k(A) x\) for polynomials \(\Pi_k\) of degree \(\leq 1\) (the diagonal term is \(\Pi_0\), the edges \(\Pi_1\)); composing \(K\) layers without non-linearity, \(y = \Pi(A) x\) with \(\deg \Pi \leq K\), and since \(L = D - A\) is itself a polynomial in \(A\) (with \(D\) fixed by the graph), \(\Pi(A) = g(L)\) for a polynomial \(g\) of the same degree. The non-linearities between layers break the exact equality — this is precisely the gap between the spectral "CNN on graph" and the spatial GNN: the GNN generalizes the filter by inserting point-wise non-linearities, like a 1D CNN. ∎

5. GNNs and message passing

Definition 5.1 — The message passing layer (MPNN, Gilmer et al., 2017) $$h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \phi\Big(h_v^{(\ell)},\ \underset{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}{\mathrm{AGG}}\ \big\{\psi\big(h_v^{(\ell)}, h_u^{(\ell)}\big)\big\}\Big)$$ message \(\psi\) (computed by the neighbor), aggregation \(\mathrm{AGG}\) (permutation-invariant: sum, mean, max, attention), update \(\phi\). One layer = one round of message passing = one convolution = one diffusion step on the graph. The choice of \(\mathrm{AGG}\) is not innocent: mean/max lose cardinality ({a, a} and {a} have the same mean and the same max); this is the subject of the GIN theorem (M2, ch. 1).
Derivation 5.2 — GCN from spectral convolution (Kipf & Welling, 2017) Start from the Chebyshev filter truncated at \(K = 1\) on the renormalized Laplacian \(L_{\mathrm{sym}} = I - \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}\), \(\tilde A = A + I\), whose eigenvalues live in \([0, 2]\) (hence the rescaling \(\tilde\Lambda \approx \Lambda - I\) by approximating \(\lambda_{\max} \approx 2\)): $$g_\theta(\tilde\Lambda) \approx \theta_0\, T_0 + \theta_1\, T_1(\tilde\Lambda) = \theta_0 I + \theta_1 (\Lambda - I).$$ Fixing the renormalization trick \(\theta_0 = -\theta_1 = \theta\) (a single parameter, filter increasing toward the low frequencies, self-loops stabilizing the dynamics): $$g_\theta \approx \theta\,(2I - L_{\mathrm{sym}}) = \theta\, \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}.$$ Adding the non-linearity, the hidden dimension and the features \(X\), we obtain the GCN layer: $$H^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big(\tilde D^{-1/2}\, \tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2}\, H^{(\ell)}\, W^{(\ell)}\Big), \qquad H^{(0)} = X,$$ a renormalized mean of the transformed representations of the neighbors; \(L\) layers = a receptive field of \(L\) hops. The renormalization (i) cancels the zero eigenvalue (no constant component injected), (ii) keeps the gains in \([-1, 1]\) — a stability condition for depth, related to the over-smoothing below.
GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) — algorithm and pseudo-code For each node, aggregate a sample of neighbors then concatenate with the current representation: 
input : G, features X, depth K, sample sizes s_1..s_K
for v in V : h_v^(0) = x_v
for l = 1 .. K :
    for v in V :
        S_v = uniform sample of N(v) of size s_l               # K neighbors in the paper
        a_v^(l) = AGG({ h_u^(l-1) : u in S_v })                 # mean / LSTM / pool
        h_v^(l) = sigma( W_l . [ h_v^(l-1) || a_v^(l) ] )       # concatenation!
        h_v^(l) = h_v^(l) / ||h_v^(l)||                          # L2 normalization
output : z_v = h_v^(K)
The concatenation preserves the information specific to the node (otherwise the mean of the neighbors crushes the local signal); the fixed per-layer sampling bounds the minibatch (ch. 7); the whole scheme is inductive: the weights \(W_l\) are re-applied to unseen nodes/graphs. Special cases: AGG = mean ≈ GCN without renormalization; AGG = pool (MLP + max); AGG = LSTM over neighbors randomly ordered (the ordering breaks invariance… an accepted approximation; the ordering problem is addressed by GOAT, ch. 10).
GAT (Veličković et al., 2018) — equations and complexity Edge weights learned by attention: $$e_{uv} = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(a^\top [\,W h_u \| W h_v\,]\big), \qquad \alpha_{uv} = \frac{\exp(e_{uv})}{\sum_{u' \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \exp(e_{uv'})}, \qquad h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \alpha_{uv}\, W h_u^{(\ell)}\Big)$$ with \(T\) parallel attention heads (T projections \(W^{(t)}\)) concatenated or averaged. Per-layer complexity: \(\mathcal{O}(|V| F F' + |E| F')\) — linear in edges, comparable to GCN, but with weights tailored to each edge: useful when the graph is noisy or some relations matter more (suspicious transactions!). Structurally identical to the attention of transformers (Deep Learning module): here the attention mask is given by the graph, there it is computed — the graph transformers of M2 (ch. 4) close the loop by learning the attention and biasing it with structure.
Beyond the homogeneous graph: R-GCN, edge features
  • R-GCN (Schlichtkrull et al., 2018): relational graph (edge types \(r\) in \(\mathcal{R}\)); \(h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\big(W_0 h_v^{(\ell)} + \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}_r(v)} \frac{1}{c_{v,r}} W_r\, h_u^{(\ell)}\big)\). To avoid the parameter explosion (\(|\mathcal{R}| \times F \times F'\)): basis decomposition \(W_r = \sum_b a_{rb} V_b\) or block-diagonal. Typical use: encoding knowledge graphs for link prediction (ch. 6).
  • Edge features: (a) line graph (each edge becomes a node); (b) convolution over edges (NNConv): \(\psi(h_u, e_{uv})\) instead of \(\psi(h_u)\) — the chemical bond carries a type; (c) conditioned attention (GAT can inject \(e_{uv}\) into the \(e_{uv}\)-score).
  • Hierarchical pooling: DiffPool (Ying et al., 2018) learns a soft assignment matrix \(S^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{GNN}_A(H^{(\ell)}))\) and contracts the graph: \(H^{(\ell+1)} = S^{(\ell)\top} H^{(\ell)}\), \(A^{(\ell+1)} = S^{(\ell)\top} A^{(\ell)} S^{(\ell)}\) — neural coarse-graining, costly but hierarchical; alternatives: sort/TopK pooling, pyramidal readout.
Theorem 5.3 — Over-smoothing: proof on a regular graph On a connected \(d\)-regular graph, GCN propagation without weights/non-linearity satisfies \(H^{(\ell)} = P^\ell X\) with \(P = \frac{A + I}{d + 1}\). Then $$H^{(\ell)} \xrightarrow[\ell \to \infty]{} \frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top}{n}\, X,$$ the harmonic solution (global mean), at geometric rate \(\big(\frac{\lambda_2(A) + 1}{d + 1}\big)^\ell\).
On a \(d\)-regular graph, \(D = (d+1)I\) for \(\tilde A\), hence \(\tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2} = (A + I)/(d+1) =: P\). Since \(A\) is real symmetric, write \(X\) in its eigenbasis: \(A = \sum_i \lambda_i u_i u_i^\top\) with \(u_1 = \mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}\), \(\lambda_1 = d > \lambda_2 \geq \dots\), and \(\lambda_i < d\) for \(i \geq 2\) (equality only if there is a distinct connected component). Then \(P^\ell = \sum_i \big(\frac{\lambda_i + 1}{d+1}\big)^\ell u_i u_i^\top\); the \(i = 1\) term equals exactly \(\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top}{n}\); all the others have a ratio \(\frac{\lambda_i + 1}{d + 1} < 1\) (and \(\geq 0\) if the graph is not bipartite; otherwise \(> -1\) in absolute value) and decay geometrically. All the information of the non-constant components is erased: every node converges to the same mean row vector — the embeddings collapse. ∎
With weights and non-linearities, the phenomenon persists (rank collapse; Oono & Suzuki, ICLR 2020): this is a structural limit of GCN depth. Remedies:
  • Jumping Knowledge (Xu et al., 2018): the readout aggregates all the depths \(h_v = \mathrm{JK}(h_v^{(1)}, \dots, h_v^{(L)})\) (concat / max / LSTM-attention): each node chooses its scale;
  • residuals and normalizations (GCNII, APPNP — developed in M2, ch. 2);
  • in practice: 2-4 layers suffice, and often — the useful depth is that of the statistical dependence of the task.
A second structural limit — over-squashing (topological bottlenecks) — is different: it concerns the channel rather than the representation, and is addressed by rewiring; the whole topic is in M2, ch. 2. A third limit: expressivity bounded by 1-WL (M2, ch. 1). 
import torch
from torch_geometric.nn import GCNConv, GATConv
from torch_geometric.datasets import Planetoid

dataset = Planetoid(root="/tmp/Cora", name="Cora")   # 2708 nodes, 5429 edges, 7 classes

        self.c1, self.c2 = GCNConv(d_in, d_hid), GCNConv(d_hid, n_classes)
    def forward(self, x, edge_index):

6. Link prediction and graph auto-encoders

Definition 6.1 — Link prediction Predicting the existence (or the type) of a missing edge \((u, v)\): the most widespread task in industry — recommendation ("who will like what?"), completion of knowledge graphs ("does this drug target this protein?"), prediction of future interactions (M2 ch. 5 for the temporal case). Protocol: mask a fraction of the edges (10%), sample as many "negatives" (non-adjacent pairs), score all the pairs, measure AUC / Hits@k.
GAE / VGAE (Kipf & Welling, 2016) — graph auto-encoders GAE: encoder \(Z = \mathrm{GCN}(X, A)\); decode by dot product — the reconstruction probability of an edge: $$p(A_{uv} = 1 \mid z_u, z_v) = \sigma(z_u^\top z_v).$$ Training: maximize the likelihood of the observed edges and minimize that of the negatives — the cross-entropy of the negative sampling of ch. 3, transposed: $$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{(u,v) \in E^+} \log \sigma(z_u^\top z_v) \; - \sum_{(u,v') \in E^-} \log \big(1 - \sigma(z_u^\top z_{v'})\big).$$ VGAE: variational Gaussian encoder \(q(Z | X, A) = \prod_i \mathcal{N}(z_i;\ \mu_i, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_i^2))\) with \(\mu = \mathrm{GCN}_\mu(X, A)\), \(\log\sigma = \mathrm{GCN}_\sigma(X, A)\), prior \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\); one maximizes the ELBO (Deep Learning module): $$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(A | Z)] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q(Z|X,A)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big),$$ reparameterization \(z_i = \mu_i + \sigma_i \odot \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). Results: on Cora, AUC \(\approx 0.91\) (GAE) and \(\approx 0.92\) (VGAE) — the prior regularizes the dot product (cf. the connection with SBM inference, ch. 9).
Scoring pairs: the choice of the decision function
  • dot product \(z_u^\top z_v\) — rank-\(d\) decomposition, symmetric, related to NetMF (ch. 3);
  • MLP over \((z_u \odot z_v, z_u - z_v)\) or concatenation — asymmetric (important in recommendation: "A buys B" ≠ "B buys A");
  • relational distances (KGC, below);
  • negatives: uniform (easy) vs hard — pairs sharing a neighbor (popularity-biased); the choice of negatives changes the learned ranking (Deep Learning module, contrastive; NLP module for the word2vec analogy).
PinSage (Ying et al., 2018) — recommendation at industrial scale The Pinterest deployment of GraphSAGE: a user-pin graph of 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges, trained by minibatches. Two key differences from GraphSAGE:
  1. importance sampling: instead of uniform neighbors, the \(T\) neighbors with the highest PageRank score normalized per node — the same "gateways" serve all the minibatches, which maximizes the reuse of the cache;
  2. ranking loss (hinge margin): \(\max(0, m - s(u, v^+) + s(u, v^-_h))\) with \(v^-_h\) a hard negative (a pin popular but not clicked); production requires candidates ranked by approximate score (vector search — NLP/Multimodal module).
LLGA lesson: at this scale, the architecture matters less than the system (sampling, cache, GPU) — this is the bridge to ch. 7.
Knowledge graph completion (KGC) Link prediction on a typed heterogeneous graph (heads \(h\), relations \(r\), tails \(t\)):
  • TransE (Bordes et al., 2013): embeddings in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with the energy score \(f(h, r, t) = -\|h + r - t\|\) — the relation is a translation; margin-based training (positives vs corrupted negatives); illuminating but limited (1-N, symmetric relations);
  • R-GCN + score (ch. 5): the encoder propagates over the typed graph, a decoder (DistMult: \(\langle h, \mathrm{diag}(r), t \rangle\)) scores the triples — the "encode-decode" architecture dominates;
  • GraphRAG link: an enterprise KG completed by KGC becomes queryable by an LLM (ch. 11); the completion deficits guide the collection of data — a data/model loop specific to the LLGA trade.

7. GNNs at scale

Why full-batch does not fit The gradient of a GCN layer mobilizes the whole graph: activation memory \(\mathcal{O}((|V| + |E|) d)\) per layer, not to mention the densification of the deep layers. On ogbn-products (2.4 million nodes, 61.9 million edges), the full-batch does not fit on a GPU; three families of solutions, all grounded on the same observation: the dependence of a node over \(L\) layers is limited to its \(L\)-neighborhood.
7.1 — Neighborhood minibatching (GraphSAGE / NeighborSampler) For a batch of target nodes \(\mathcal{B}\) and a depth \(L\) with sampling sizes \((s_L, \dots, s_1)\) (fanouts): 
for l = L .. 1 :                                    # from the deep layer toward the surface
    N_l = union of the sampled neighbors (s_l per node) of the nodes of N_{l+1}
    N_{l+1/2} = N_l sampled                          # computation subgraph
compute the representations layer by layer on the subgraph
update the parameters with the loss on the outputs of B only
Subgraph size: \(|\mathcal{B}| \times (1 + s_L (1 + s_{L-1}(\dots)))\); with \(L = 2\), \(s_1 = s_2 = 10\), a batch of 1 node touches at most \(1 + 10 + 100 = 111\) nodes — bounded, independent of \(n\). Two subtleties: (i) the estimator is unbiased in expectation for the mean over neighbors, but the variance explodes at the hubs (hence decreasing fanouts); (ii) the nested samplings create redundant computation across batches (the "expansive neighborhood"; PyG's NeighborLoader implements exactly this).
7.2 — Cluster-GCN (Chiang et al., 2019): partitioning Partition the graph into \(k\) clusters (METIS), each batch = one whole cluster: GCN training on the induced subgraph costs \(\mathcal{O}(|E_c| d + |V_c| d^2)\) and each full epoch costs \(\mathcal{O}(|E|)\) — like the full-batch, but in constant memory, and deep GCNs become trainable again. The price: the partition bias — the inter-cluster edges are invisible during the step; correction: block adjacency matrix, \(\bar A = A + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(A)\) (linking each cluster to itself) and re-sampling the partitions from time to time. Even better: link the clusters by a "virtual graph" of clusters — a precursor of the GraphRAG hierarchies (ch. 11).
7.3 — GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2020): sampling subgraphs, unbiasedly Flip the perspective: first sample a subgraph \(\mathcal{G}_s\) (by nodes, by edges, or by random walks), then compute a corrected gradient on it. The key is the normalization by coverage probability: if the subgraph includes node \(v\) with its edges covered with probabilities \(p_v, p_{uv}\), the reweighted estimator $$\hat\nabla = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{G}_s} \frac{1}{p_v} \nabla_v + \sum_{(u,v) \in \mathcal{G}_s} \Big(\frac{1}{p_{uv}} - \frac{1}{p_u} - \frac{1}{p_v}\Big) \nabla_{uv}$$ is unbiased for the full-batch gradient — the paper proves it by expanding the expectation over the sampling scheme. Hidden benefit: the batches are "dense" (no expansive neighborhood), and walk-based sampling captures the community structure. The general picture: bias-variance-memory — neighbor-sampling (high variance, zero bias); cluster-GCN (partition bias, zero variance); GraphSAINT (unbiased by reweighting, variance controlled by RW sampling). These trade-offs are the daily bread of M2, ch. 6 (and of the Emerging ML module for the federated parallel: partitions = clients).

9. Graph generation

Problem Sample \(G \sim p_\theta(G)\) where \(p\) is learned over a family of graphs (active molecules, road networks…) — possibly conditioned on a target property ("generate a molecule with this activity") or on text (text-to-graph). This is the "Generative AI" side of the title of the master. Three difficulties specific to graphs: (i) variable size (\(n\) random); (ii) discrete outputs (atom types, edge types); (iii) a space quotiented by isomorphism — two permuted adjacencies are the same graph: one needs exchangeable models (theory below).
9.1 — The limiting model: graphons and exchangeability A graphon is a measurable function \(W : [0,1]^2 \to [0,1]\). Given \(W\) and \(n\): draw \(x_i \sim \mathcal{U}([0,1])\), then each edge \((i, j)\) independently with probability \(W(x_i, x_j)\) — a graph \(G_n\) on \(n\) vertices. The Aldous-Hoover theorem guarantees that any sequence of exchangeable graphs (distribution invariant under permutation of the vertices) admits a limit graphon in the sense of the cut metric (Lovász, Large Networks and Graph Limits, 2012). The learning connection:
  • estimation: estimating \(W\) from a large graph = approximating it by a step function constant per block — partition the \(n\) vertices into \(k\) blocks of sizes \(n_b\), set \(\hat W = \frac{e_{bb'}}{n_b n_{b'}}\) on the block \((b, b')\) (empirical densities); the partition is the problem — and SBM/clustering solves it;
  • generation: a learned graph model implicitly targets a \(\hat W\) (large molecules: large \(n\), structured target distribution);
  • theory of deep generative models of graphs: the exchangeable architectures (invariant to permutations of the nodes) are those that approximate graphons — this is the consistency criterion of the models below.
9.2 — Classical random models: ER and SBM (with the math)
  • Erdős-Rényi \(G(n, p)\): all the edges iid with probability \(p\); degrees \(\sim \mathrm{Bin}(n-1, p)\), no community; threshold phenomena (at \(\frac{\log n}{n}\)) for connectivity — the "null" reference against which to test structure (Refresher Statistics module);
  • SBM (Holland et al., 1983): each node has a latent block \(z_i \in \{1..k\}\) (uniform), and $$P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid z_i = a, z_j = b) = B_{ab}.$$ Likelihood: \(\log P(A | z, B) = \sum_{a,b} n_{ab} \log B_{ab} + (n_a n_b - n_{ab}) \log(1 - B_{ab})\) (with \(n_{ab}\) = observed edges between blocks \(a, b\)); inference by maximum of likelihood (EM) or Bayesian (MCMC sampling); detectability: below the Kesten-Stigum threshold, no algorithm recovers the blocks — the phase where the spectral clustering (ch. 4) sits exactly at the boundary. Generation: sample \(z\) then \(A\); DCSBM variant (corrected degrees). The SBM is used in both directions: generating realistic synthetic graphs (benefit: total control) and inferring the blocks (statistically grounded clustering, without a resolution limit unlike the modularity of ch. 0); topic modeling by SBM (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018) treats a document-term corpus as a bipartite SBM — the "statistical" generalization of the Graph of Words of ch. 10.
9.3 — Graph VAEs: GraphVAE and NeVAE GraphVAE (Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018): a GNN encoder, decoder in one block \(\hat A, \hat X, \hat E\) (full adjacency); the mismatch of the outputs to the ground truth requires a matching matrix (Hungarian matching) — the node ordering problem is paid for here (cf. GOAT, ch. 10). NeVAE (Samanta et al., AAAI 2019): a granular VAE designed for chemistry:
  1. sparse encoder: one embedding per atom (no full adjacency — memory \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\)), MPNN messages between atoms;
  2. sequential decoder constrained by valence: at each step, a new atom is added and its bonds drawn by probability heads conditioned on the remaining valence — a carbon cannot receive 5 bonds; aromaticity is handled by dedicated heads;
  3. noise correction: local resampling of the invalid bonds during training — the penalty of forbidden solutions is softened;
  4. result: chemical validity > 90% before post-processing, where unconstrained GraphVAEs produce very few.
Moral: injecting domain knowledge (valence) into the architecture beats the post-processing (RDKit sanitization) — reused by all the following methods.
9.4 — Autoregressive models: GraphRNN (You et al., 2018) Generating a graph = generating a sequence. Order the nodes \(1, 2, \dots\) (any order: the model must be robust to this choice — in practice canonical BFS orders); the graph is then the sequence of adjacency rows \(S^i = (A_{i1}, \dots, A_{i,i-1}) \in \{0,1\}^{i-1}\). Two levels: $$\text{node level: } h_i = \mathrm{RNN}_{node}(h_{i-1}, s_i), \qquad \text{edge level: } P(S^i | h_i) = \prod_{j < i} \mathrm{RNN}_{edge}(s_{ij}),$$ where the state \(h_i\) conditions the edge RNN which emits \(S^i\) bit by bit (Bernoulli categorical per position); training maximizes the sequential likelihood (teacher forcing). Properties: exchangeability approximated by the use of BFS orders (the prefix is invariant to intra-level permutations); complexity \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) decoding steps, \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) per step → quadratic — very reasonable for \(n \leq 100\). Limits: sensitivity to order (aromatic rings depend on the starting point); conditioning on properties is awkward (hence the models below). This "sequentialize the graph" scheme is exactly that of the LLM on text — the text-to-graph convergence is natural (ch. 11).
9.5 — Discrete diffusion: DiGress (Vignac et al., 2023) The molecular state of the art 2023-2026 transposes the diffusion of the Multimodal GenAI module to categories (atom types, bond types). Forward process (noising): at each step, each node and edge type is re-drawn $$q(X_t | X_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(X_t;\ Q_t X_{t-1}\big), \qquad q(E_t | E_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(E_t;\ \bar Q_t E_{t-1}\big),$$ where \(Q_t\) are transition matrices (one row = law of the new type given the old one); after \(T\) steps, the stationary law is the marginal of the datasets (distribution of the atoms/bonds). Backward process (denoising): a GNN \(\theta_\phi\) (MPNN + attention) predicts \(X_0\) and \(E_0\) clean from \((X_t, E_t)\), then a Bayesian denoising step \(q(X_{t-1}|X_t, \hat X_0)\) is derived in closed form; perturbations: edges are also added/removed during noising (discrete topology). Three winning choices: auxiliary losses (valence, bond lengths) for chemistry; guaranteed exchangeability — the model never sees a node order (unlike GraphRNN); conditioning by graph (inpainting): freeze a skeleton and generate the rest — "target molecule" conditional generation. 3D cousin: EDM/EDT (equivariant positions — M2, ch. 9). The team's NGG paper (ch. 10) transposes this recipe into latent space to condition on graph features.
9.6 — Evaluating generated graphs: distributional fidelity A generator is not graded image by image but distribution against distribution:
  • validity / uniqueness / novelty (chemistry): % of syntactically valid, distinct molecules, absent from training — a model that memorizes has a novelty of 0;
  • structural MMDs: maximum mean discrepancy \(\mathrm{MMD}^2 = \big\| \mathbb{E}_{G \sim p}[\phi(G)] - \mathbb{E}_{G \sim q}[\phi(G)] \big\|^2\) with \(\phi\) = degree histograms, clustering coefficients, motif/orbit counts (the triangles of ch. 0!) — the remark: these features are exactly what 1-WL does not always capture (M2, ch. 1);
  • FCD (Fréchet ChemNet Distance): Fréchet distance between the Gaussians of the ChemNet embeddings of the generated vs real molecules — the exact analogue of the FID of images (Multimodal GenAI module);
  • OT distances: comparing graphs without node correspondence = Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport (Optimal Transport module); the MMDs above are kernel special cases of them.
9.7 — Conditional generation: the real industrial product Rank by target property (solubility, docking), condition on text ("an antibiotic against X"), on a skeleton (R-group optimization), on a partial graph (inpainting); three mechanisms: (i) input conditioning (concat of the descriptor — NGG); (ii) gradient guidance through a property predictor during sampling (the classifier guidance of images); (iii) search in latent space (Bayesian optimization — the classic QSAR pipeline). This is where the GNN × LLM encounter is most fertile (ch. 11): the LLM proposes, the GNN evaluates, the loop optimizes.

This chapter presents four research directions of the laboratory in direct connection with the program; the papers are to be read and presented as a project — this is the module's "paper" exercise (and the daily bread of M2, ch. 8).

10.1 — Graph of Words: from document to graph (Rousseau & Vazirgiannis; Tixier, Malliaros & Vazirgiannis) The "bag-of-words" representation ignores the order of words; the graph of words partially restores it:
  1. construction: sliding window of size \(w\) (typically 2-10); nodes = unique terms (weighted by frequency); edge \((u, v)\) if \(u\) and \(v\) co-occur in a window, weighted by the number of co-occurrences; a document = one graph;
  2. keywords: extract the "central" nodes of the graph; Rousseau & Vazirgiannis (ECIR 2015) keep the main k-core (the maximal k-core: the largest subgraph with min degree \(\geq k\)), Tixier et al. (EMNLP 2016) classify by degeneracy (the core dominates classification);
  3. document classification: a corpus = a set of graphs → graph classification (ch. 2); moving from bag-of-words → graph-of-words enriches the co-occurrence features and improves the classic text classifiers.
Positioning: this is the symmetric twin of ch. 3 — DeepWalk applies word2vec to graphs, Graph of Words applies graphs to text. Posterity: graph-of-words + graph CNNs for hierarchical classification; topic modeling by bipartite SBM (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018); and the GraphRAG indexes (ch. 11) that index entities and co-occurrences — an enterprise graph of words.
10.2 — Path Neural Networks (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Idea: the GNN layer of ch. 5 aggregates walks of length 1 (the neighbors); paths of length \(\ell\) carry more structural information. The path operator: for each node \(v\), consider the set \(\mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)}\) of simple paths of length \(\ell\) starting from \(v\); each path \(p = (v, u_1, \dots, u_\ell)\) is encoded by an RNN (GRU): $$m_p = \mathrm{GRU}\big(h_{u_\ell}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{GRU}(\dots \mathrm{GRU}(h_{u_1}^{(0)}, h_v^{(0)})\dots)\big), \qquad h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\big(\{m_p : p \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)}\}\big).$$ Three variants depending on \(\mathcal{P}_v\): shortest paths (PathNN-s), random walks (PathNN-r), paths sampled in the neighborhood (PathNN-n) — sampling bounds the cost, since the number of simple paths grows exponentially. Results: accuracies greater than or equal to the canonical GNNs on the classification benchmarks (nodes and graphs, including ZINC for molecules), with controlled complexity; the expressivity analysis (what the paths see that the walks miss — cycles, distances) is developed in M2, ch. 1 and 8. Positioning in the course: the "natural" generalization of ch. 3 (DeepWalk walks) and of ch. 5 (1-hop neighborhoods).
10.3 — Graph Ordering Attention Networks (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) — J. Lutzeyer's work on ordering and expressivity Invariant aggregators (sum/mean/max) throw away all ordering information — but the local order of the neighbors carries signal (think of a text: the bag of words loses the syntax). GOAT learns a local order:
  1. an attention orders the neighbors of \(v\) (scores learned on \((h_v, h_u)\), sorting);
  2. the ordered sequence is processed by an RNN — a local sequence model, hence strictly more expressive than invariant aggregation;
  3. the GOAT levels (order at increasing radius) compose layer after layer.
The double positioning: in expressivity, GOAT distinguishes graphs that 1-WL confuses (joins M2, ch. 1); in generation, the order choice is THE Gordian knot of autoregressive models (GraphRNN, ch. 9.4; GraphVAE, ch. 9.3) — learning the order rather than suffering it is a transverse contribution. It is also an elegant answer to the GraphSAGE-LSTM question of ch. 5 (the neighborhood there was ordered randomly).
10.4 — Two recent bridges to GenAI: NGG and pseudo-code prompting
  • NGG — Neural Graph Generator (Evdaimon et al., with Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024): latent diffusion conditioned on graph features (spectrum, degrees…): a GNN encoder compresses graphs into latent vectors, a diffusion model learns the distribution and the conditioning there; one then generates graphs exhibiting the requested features — ch. 9.5 transposed into latent space, with the spectrum (ch. 4) as descriptor; applications: synthetic data, anonymization, distributional anomaly detection;
  • Pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024): make the LLMs reason about graphs (connected components, shortest paths…) by giving them instructions in pseudo-code rather than in natural language — systematic gains across all the LLM sizes tested; detailed in ch. 11.
These four directions (text↔graph, paths, order, generation/diffusion, LLM) cover exactly the map of the master — M2, ch. 8, develops them on the theory side.

11. Graphs × LLMs: GraphRAG and the hybrid

11.1 — The complete industrial GraphRAG pipeline (after Edge et al., 2024)
  1. ingestion: splitting the corpus into chunks; LLM extraction of the entities (NER) and relations (typed relation extraction, cf. NLP module) in the triplet format (subject, relation, object, source passage); entity resolution (aliases); the graph is built incrementally — each edge keeps its source or sources;
  2. communities: community detection (Leiden — a Louvain variant, ch. 0) on the entity graph; for each community (at each level of the hierarchy), an LLM summarizes the entities, relations and themes; the summaries aggregate bottom-up;
  3. hybrid index: the graph plus a vector index of the chunks and the summaries (classic RAG remains useful for local queries);
  4. global queries ("what are the major themes of the corpus?"): map-reduce over the community summaries — each community answers partially, an LLM synthesizes; local queries ("who works with whom on X?"): expanding the neighborhood of entity X (linked entities, source chunks) then a grounded answer;
  5. evaluation: comparison of vector-text RAG vs GraphRAG on the completeness and diversity of the answers; cost: LLM ingestion is expensive — amortized over thousands of queries.
LLGA reading: each stage is an object of the course — clustering (ch. 0), link prediction for the completion of the KG (ch. 6), embeddings for the vector index (ch. 3), exchangeability of the summaries (generation, ch. 9).
11.2 — Making the LLM reason over the graph: encoding and pseudo-code
  • serialization: describing the (sub)graph as text (edge lists, adjacency, incidences); the NLGraph benchmark (Wang et al., 2023) shows that LLMs fail on elementary tasks (cycles, connectivity, shortest paths) as soon as size grows, even few-shot; the "Talk like a Graph" study (Fatemi et al., 2024) compares the encodings — none dominates everywhere;
  • pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024 — the master's team): give the LLM the algorithm in pseudo-code rather than a description in natural language; 
    input : edge list E, node s
visited = {s} ; stack = [s]
while stack not empty :
    v = stack.pop()
    for u in neighbors(v) in E :
        if u not in visited : visited.add(u) ; stack.push(u)
output : visited   # connected component of s
    the LLM executes the trace — systematic gains over natural instructions, across all LLM families; the lesson: on discrete structures, the programming language is a better "prompt" than natural language;
  • GNN + LLM hybrids: the GNN encodes the structure (ch. 5) and injects: (a) soft tokens into the prompt (projecting the GNN embeddings into the LLM space); (b) structural recall — the GNN retrieves the connected passages (like the KG of 11.1, but learned); (c) cross-distillation — the annotated LLM trains the GNN, the GNN filters the LLM's hallucinations.
11.3 — GraphRAG limits and hygiene
  • extraction hallucinates (non-existent entities): control with a double pass and anchoring on chunks;
  • the graph is an approximation — queries depend on its quality; harden with KGC (ch. 6) before querying;
  • the ingestion cost vs the gain on relational queries: for a single-document FAQ, vector RAG is enough; GraphRAG pays off when the questions are multi-hop, aggregative or global;
  • privacy: the graph aggregates scattered facts — re-identification risk (M2, ch. 7).
  • Agents on graphs: the plan of a multi-agent system is a graph (orchestration DAG); "graph engineering" (2026) = programming the topology of the agents — the master loop: the graph in the model (attention), the graph for the model (RAG), the graph between the models (agents).
Final lab Build a mini-GraphRAG: 1) extract an entity graph from an enterprise corpus (reports, simulated e-mails) with an LLM; 2) detect the communities (Leiden) and summarize with an LLM; 3) answer relational questions ("who works with whom on X?") by graph traversal vs. by vector RAG vs. by pseudo-code prompting (11.2); 4) compare fidelity, token cost and latency. Deliverable: report + reproducible notebook.

Solution (sketch): expand \(x^\top D x - x^\top A x = \sum_v d_v x_v^2 - 2\sum_{(u,v)} x_u x_v\) and group by edges; \(\lambda_2 = 0\) is equivalent to the existence of a non-zero \(x \perp \mathbf{1}\) constant per component (thm. 0.3 of the multiplicity computation), hence \(\lambda_2 > 0\) \(\iff\) a single component.   2. Triangles by the trace. From \(\mathrm{tr}(A^3)\), count the triangles of the "two \(K_4\)s sharing an edge" graph. Generalize: what does \(\mathrm{tr}(A^4)\) count? Solution: the two \(K_4\)s contain 4 triangles each, none goes through the shared edge alone; total 8, hence \(\mathrm{tr}(A^3) = 48\). \(\mathrm{tr}(A^4)\) counts the closed walks of length 4 (squares, back-and-forths…) — not only the squares; one must subtract the degree contributions (formula with \(\sum_v d_v^2\) for the back-and-forths).
