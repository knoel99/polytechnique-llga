1. Why graphs?

Real-world data is relational: the structure carries information that i.i.d. methods ignore. Social networks (friendships), molecules (bonds), transaction networks (fraud!), enterprise knowledge graphs (customers, products, processes), computer networks, citation clusters, legal ontologies… All are naturally graphs, often heterogeneous (typed nodes/edges: "buys", "owns", "works with") and dynamic (timestamped edges: transactions second by second). Also typically: few labels (a few dozen annotated nodes out of millions) — the semi-supervised regime of ch. 5 — and distributions biased by sampling (the observed edges are not a uniform sample of the interactions).

The LLGA positioning LLMs excel at text but struggle with: structured relations, factual fidelity (hallucinations), and non-textual data. Graphs fill exactly these gaps:
  • GraphRAG: connecting the LLM to structured proprietary data — today the most effective approach for making an LLM reliable in the enterprise (ch. 11);
  • GNN + LLM: generative AI on structured data (generating molecules, plans, recommendations);
  • Industrial applications: pharma/biotech (molecules = graphs), finance (transaction networks, systemic risk), recommendation, supply chain, multi-agent systems (agent workflows are DAGs — RL module).
Common thread of the master's program Three places of the graph in generative AI: inside the model (the attention of transformers is a complete dynamic graph — Deep Learning module), for the model (GraphRAG, structural encoding — ch. 11), between models (agent orchestration = DAG programming). This module lays the foundations of all three; the M2 module "Advanced GNN" extends each one.
