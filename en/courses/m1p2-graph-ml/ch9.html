9. Graph generation

Problem Sample \(G \sim p_\theta(G)\) where \(p\) is learned over a family of graphs (active molecules, road networks…) — possibly conditioned on a target property ("generate a molecule with this activity") or on text (text-to-graph). This is the "Generative AI" side of the title of the master. Three difficulties specific to graphs: (i) variable size (\(n\) random); (ii) discrete outputs (atom types, edge types); (iii) a space quotiented by isomorphism — two permuted adjacencies are the same graph: one needs exchangeable models (theory below).
9.1 — The limiting model: graphons and exchangeability A graphon is a measurable function \(W : [0,1]^2 \to [0,1]\). Given \(W\) and \(n\): draw \(x_i \sim \mathcal{U}([0,1])\), then each edge \((i, j)\) independently with probability \(W(x_i, x_j)\) — a graph \(G_n\) on \(n\) vertices. The Aldous-Hoover theorem guarantees that any sequence of exchangeable graphs (distribution invariant under permutation of the vertices) admits a limit graphon in the sense of the cut metric (Lovász, Large Networks and Graph Limits, 2012). The learning connection:
  • estimation: estimating \(W\) from a large graph = approximating it by a step function constant per block — partition the \(n\) vertices into \(k\) blocks of sizes \(n_b\), set \(\hat W = \frac{e_{bb'}}{n_b n_{b'}}\) on the block \((b, b')\) (empirical densities); the partition is the problem — and SBM/clustering solves it;
  • generation: a learned graph model implicitly targets a \(\hat W\) (large molecules: large \(n\), structured target distribution);
  • theory of deep generative models of graphs: the exchangeable architectures (invariant to permutations of the nodes) are those that approximate graphons — this is the consistency criterion of the models below.
9.2 — Classical random models: ER and SBM (with the math)
  • Erdős-Rényi \(G(n, p)\): all the edges iid with probability \(p\); degrees \(\sim \mathrm{Bin}(n-1, p)\), no community; threshold phenomena (at \(\frac{\log n}{n}\)) for connectivity — the "null" reference against which to test structure (Refresher Statistics module);
  • SBM (Holland et al., 1983): each node has a latent block \(z_i \in \{1..k\}\) (uniform), and $$P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid z_i = a, z_j = b) = B_{ab}.$$ Likelihood: \(\log P(A | z, B) = \sum_{a,b} n_{ab} \log B_{ab} + (n_a n_b - n_{ab}) \log(1 - B_{ab})\) (with \(n_{ab}\) = observed edges between blocks \(a, b\)); inference by maximum of likelihood (EM) or Bayesian (MCMC sampling); detectability: below the Kesten-Stigum threshold, no algorithm recovers the blocks — the phase where the spectral clustering (ch. 4) sits exactly at the boundary. Generation: sample \(z\) then \(A\); DCSBM variant (corrected degrees). The SBM is used in both directions: generating realistic synthetic graphs (benefit: total control) and inferring the blocks (statistically grounded clustering, without a resolution limit unlike the modularity of ch. 0); topic modeling by SBM (TopSBM, Gerlach et al., 2018) treats a document-term corpus as a bipartite SBM — the "statistical" generalization of the Graph of Words of ch. 10.
9.3 — Graph VAEs: GraphVAE and NeVAE GraphVAE (Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018): a GNN encoder, decoder in one block \(\hat A, \hat X, \hat E\) (full adjacency); the mismatch of the outputs to the ground truth requires a matching matrix (Hungarian matching) — the node ordering problem is paid for here (cf. GOAT, ch. 10). NeVAE (Samanta et al., AAAI 2019): a granular VAE designed for chemistry:
  1. sparse encoder: one embedding per atom (no full adjacency — memory \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\)), MPNN messages between atoms;
  2. sequential decoder constrained by valence: at each step, a new atom is added and its bonds drawn by probability heads conditioned on the remaining valence — a carbon cannot receive 5 bonds; aromaticity is handled by dedicated heads;
  3. noise correction: local resampling of the invalid bonds during training — the penalty of forbidden solutions is softened;
  4. result: chemical validity > 90% before post-processing, where unconstrained GraphVAEs produce very few.
Moral: injecting domain knowledge (valence) into the architecture beats the post-processing (RDKit sanitization) — reused by all the following methods.
9.4 — Autoregressive models: GraphRNN (You et al., 2018) Generating a graph = generating a sequence. Order the nodes \(1, 2, \dots\) (any order: the model must be robust to this choice — in practice canonical BFS orders); the graph is then the sequence of adjacency rows \(S^i = (A_{i1}, \dots, A_{i,i-1}) \in \{0,1\}^{i-1}\). Two levels: $$\text{node level: } h_i = \mathrm{RNN}_{node}(h_{i-1}, s_i), \qquad \text{edge level: } P(S^i | h_i) = \prod_{j < i} \mathrm{RNN}_{edge}(s_{ij}),$$ where the state \(h_i\) conditions the edge RNN which emits \(S^i\) bit by bit (Bernoulli categorical per position); training maximizes the sequential likelihood (teacher forcing). Properties: exchangeability approximated by the use of BFS orders (the prefix is invariant to intra-level permutations); complexity \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) decoding steps, \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) per step → quadratic — very reasonable for \(n \leq 100\). Limits: sensitivity to order (aromatic rings depend on the starting point); conditioning on properties is awkward (hence the models below). This "sequentialize the graph" scheme is exactly that of the LLM on text — the text-to-graph convergence is natural (ch. 11).
9.5 — Discrete diffusion: DiGress (Vignac et al., 2023) The molecular state of the art 2023-2026 transposes the diffusion of the Multimodal GenAI module to categories (atom types, bond types). Forward process (noising): at each step, each node and edge type is re-drawn $$q(X_t | X_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(X_t;\ Q_t X_{t-1}\big), \qquad q(E_t | E_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(E_t;\ \bar Q_t E_{t-1}\big),$$ where \(Q_t\) are transition matrices (one row = law of the new type given the old one); after \(T\) steps, the stationary law is the marginal of the datasets (distribution of the atoms/bonds). Backward process (denoising): a GNN \(\theta_\phi\) (MPNN + attention) predicts \(X_0\) and \(E_0\) clean from \((X_t, E_t)\), then a Bayesian denoising step \(q(X_{t-1}|X_t, \hat X_0)\) is derived in closed form; perturbations: edges are also added/removed during noising (discrete topology). Three winning choices: auxiliary losses (valence, bond lengths) for chemistry; guaranteed exchangeability — the model never sees a node order (unlike GraphRNN); conditioning by graph (inpainting): freeze a skeleton and generate the rest — "target molecule" conditional generation. 3D cousin: EDM/EDT (equivariant positions — M2, ch. 9). The team's NGG paper (ch. 10) transposes this recipe into latent space to condition on graph features.
9.6 — Evaluating generated graphs: distributional fidelity A generator is not graded image by image but distribution against distribution:
  • validity / uniqueness / novelty (chemistry): % of syntactically valid, distinct molecules, absent from training — a model that memorizes has a novelty of 0;
  • structural MMDs: maximum mean discrepancy \(\mathrm{MMD}^2 = \big\| \mathbb{E}_{G \sim p}[\phi(G)] - \mathbb{E}_{G \sim q}[\phi(G)] \big\|^2\) with \(\phi\) = degree histograms, clustering coefficients, motif/orbit counts (the triangles of ch. 0!) — the remark: these features are exactly what 1-WL does not always capture (M2, ch. 1);
  • FCD (Fréchet ChemNet Distance): Fréchet distance between the Gaussians of the ChemNet embeddings of the generated vs real molecules — the exact analogue of the FID of images (Multimodal GenAI module);
  • OT distances: comparing graphs without node correspondence = Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport (Optimal Transport module); the MMDs above are kernel special cases of them.
9.7 — Conditional generation: the real industrial product Rank by target property (solubility, docking), condition on text ("an antibiotic against X"), on a skeleton (R-group optimization), on a partial graph (inpainting); three mechanisms: (i) input conditioning (concat of the descriptor — NGG); (ii) gradient guidance through a property predictor during sampling (the classifier guidance of images); (iii) search in latent space (Bayesian optimization — the classic QSAR pipeline). This is where the GNN × LLM encounter is most fertile (ch. 11): the LLM proposes, the GNN evaluates, the loop optimizes.
