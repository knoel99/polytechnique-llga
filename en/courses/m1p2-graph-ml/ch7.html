7. GNNs at scale

Why full-batch does not fit The gradient of a GCN layer mobilizes the whole graph: activation memory \(\mathcal{O}((|V| + |E|) d)\) per layer, not to mention the densification of the deep layers. On ogbn-products (2.4 million nodes, 61.9 million edges), the full-batch does not fit on a GPU; three families of solutions, all grounded on the same observation: the dependence of a node over \(L\) layers is limited to its \(L\)-neighborhood.
7.1 — Neighborhood minibatching (GraphSAGE / NeighborSampler) For a batch of target nodes \(\mathcal{B}\) and a depth \(L\) with sampling sizes \((s_L, \dots, s_1)\) (fanouts): 
for l = L .. 1 :                                    # from the deep layer toward the surface
    N_l = union of the sampled neighbors (s_l per node) of the nodes of N_{l+1}
    N_{l+1/2} = N_l sampled                          # computation subgraph
compute the representations layer by layer on the subgraph
update the parameters with the loss on the outputs of B only
Subgraph size: \(|\mathcal{B}| \times (1 + s_L (1 + s_{L-1}(\dots)))\); with \(L = 2\), \(s_1 = s_2 = 10\), a batch of 1 node touches at most \(1 + 10 + 100 = 111\) nodes — bounded, independent of \(n\). Two subtleties: (i) the estimator is unbiased in expectation for the mean over neighbors, but the variance explodes at the hubs (hence decreasing fanouts); (ii) the nested samplings create redundant computation across batches (the "expansive neighborhood"; PyG's NeighborLoader implements exactly this).
7.2 — Cluster-GCN (Chiang et al., 2019): partitioning Partition the graph into \(k\) clusters (METIS), each batch = one whole cluster: GCN training on the induced subgraph costs \(\mathcal{O}(|E_c| d + |V_c| d^2)\) and each full epoch costs \(\mathcal{O}(|E|)\) — like the full-batch, but in constant memory, and deep GCNs become trainable again. The price: the partition bias — the inter-cluster edges are invisible during the step; correction: block adjacency matrix, \(\bar A = A + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(A)\) (linking each cluster to itself) and re-sampling the partitions from time to time. Even better: link the clusters by a "virtual graph" of clusters — a precursor of the GraphRAG hierarchies (ch. 11).
7.3 — GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2020): sampling subgraphs, unbiasedly Flip the perspective: first sample a subgraph \(\mathcal{G}_s\) (by nodes, by edges, or by random walks), then compute a corrected gradient on it. The key is the normalization by coverage probability: if the subgraph includes node \(v\) with its edges covered with probabilities \(p_v, p_{uv}\), the reweighted estimator $$\hat\nabla = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{G}_s} \frac{1}{p_v} \nabla_v + \sum_{(u,v) \in \mathcal{G}_s} \Big(\frac{1}{p_{uv}} - \frac{1}{p_u} - \frac{1}{p_v}\Big) \nabla_{uv}$$ is unbiased for the full-batch gradient — the paper proves it by expanding the expectation over the sampling scheme. Hidden benefit: the batches are "dense" (no expansive neighborhood), and walk-based sampling captures the community structure. The general picture: bias-variance-memory — neighbor-sampling (high variance, zero bias); cluster-GCN (partition bias, zero variance); GraphSAINT (unbiased by reweighting, variance controlled by RW sampling). These trade-offs are the daily bread of M2, ch. 6 (and of the Emerging ML module for the federated parallel: partitions = clients).
