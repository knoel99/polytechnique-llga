5. Stochastic gradient descent

When \(n = 10^9\), computing \(\nabla f\) costs \(10^9\) evaluations — per iteration. The founding gesture of modern learning (Robbins–Monro, 1951): accept a noisy but cheap gradient.

Definition 5.1 — SGD and assumptions Draw \(i_k\) uniformly from \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) (or from the batch): $$x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta_k\, g_k, \qquad g_k = \nabla \ell(h_{x_k}(x_{i_k}), y_{i_k}) + \nabla\Omega\text{ (when applicable).}$$ Assumptions: (H1) unbiasedness \(\mathbb{E}[g_k \mid x_k] = \nabla f(x_k)\); (H2) bounded variance \(\mathbb{E}\|g_k - \nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \leq \sigma^2\); (H3) Robbins–Monro \(\sum \eta_k = \infty\), \(\sum \eta_k^2 < \infty\) for decreasing steps.
Theorem 5.2 — What can be proved simply (proof) Let \(f\) be convex \(L\)-smooth, (H1)–(H2), constant step \(\eta \leq 1/L\). Then $$\mathbb{E}\big[f(x_{k+1})\big] \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}\big[f(x_k)\big] - \frac{\eta}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \frac{\eta^2 L \sigma^2}{2}.$$ Consequences: (i) \(\liminf \mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 \leq L\sigma^2\eta\); (ii) at constant step, SGD converges to a neighborhood of the solution of radius \(O(\eta\sigma^2)\) — not to the solution.
Conditionally on \(x_k\), the mean step equals \(-\eta\nabla f(x_k)\) (unbiasedness) and the noise \(\zeta_k = g_k - \nabla f(x_k)\) is centered. The descent lemma 2.1 applied to \(x_k - \eta g_k\), then expanding the squares: $$\mathbb{E}_k[f(x_{k+1})] \leq f(x_k) - \eta\big(1 - \tfrac{L\eta}{2}\big)\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \tfrac{L\eta^2}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_k\|\zeta_k\|^2 \leq f(x_k) - \tfrac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \tfrac{L\eta^2\sigma^2}{2}.$$ (i) follows by summing and using the bounded decrease of the errors; (ii) by injecting back into the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality of ch. 2. ∎

Minibatching: buying variance with compute A mini-batch \(B\) of size \(b\) replaces \(g_k\) with the average over \(B\): the variance drops to \(\sigma^2/b\) (independent draws), the cost rises to \(b\) gradients. At a fixed compute budget, large \(b\) = less noise but fewer steps — the optimal trade-off depends on the regime: in the convex case, steps of modest size \(b\) often dominate (Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal 2018, §5.3); in deep learning, very large batches require correcting the step (LARS, ch. 10). Hence the question of the next chapter: can we kill the variance without paying \(b\) or \(n\) at every step?
Proposition 5.3 — Noise is not (only) an evil The stationary regime \(O(\eta\sigma^2)\) of theorem 5.2 has a positive side: the fluctuations around the minimum sweep the "passes" of the landscape and experience (see the Deep Learning module, ch. 3) shows that the "flat" minima found by SGD often generalize better than the narrow valleys reached by deterministic methods. The very sigma² that sets the stationary error acts as an implicit regularizer — an active research theme (see ch. 11, A. Dieuleveut's work on constant-step SGD). 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, d = 20_000, 50
X = rng.normal(size=(n, d)) / np.sqrt(d)
y = X @ rng.normal(size=d) + 0.3*rng.normal(size=n)

def sgd_logreg(eta, epochs=20, b=32):
    w = np.zeros(d); losses = []
    sig = lambda z: 1/(1+np.exp(-z))
    for ep in range(epochs):
        idx = rng.permutation(n)
        for s in range(0, n, b):
            B = idx[s:s+b]
            p = sig(X[B] @ w)
            g = X[B].T @ (p - y[B]) / b      # mini-batch gradient
            w = w - eta * g
        losses.append(np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -(2*y-1)*(X@w))))  # exact risk
    return losses

for eta in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0):
    print(eta, "->", sgd_logreg(eta)[-1])
# expected: step too small = slow; too large = high plateau (eta*sigma^2 term)
