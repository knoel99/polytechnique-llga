7. Newton and quasi-Newton

Changing markets: pay for second-order information (Hessian) in exchange for quadratic local convergence — the number of correct digits doubles at every iteration.

Theorem 7.1 — Local quadratic convergence (proved) Let \(f\) be twice differentiable, \(\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI\) everywhere (\(f\) therefore strongly convex), Hessian \(M\)-Lipschitz. Newton's method \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \nabla^2 f(x_k)^{-1} \nabla f(x_k)\) satisfies, as soon as \(\|x_0 - x^\star\| \leq \mu/M\): $$\|x_{k+1} - x^\star\| \;\leq\; \frac{M}{2\mu}\,\|x_k - x^\star\|^2.$$
Write \(e_k = x_k - x^\star\) and expand \(\nabla f\) at \(x_k\) around \(x^\star\) (where \(\nabla f(x^\star) = 0\)): $$0 = \nabla f(x^\star) = \nabla f(x_k) - \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x^\star + s e_k)\, e_k \, ds \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla f(x_k) = H_k e_k - \rho_k,\quad \|\rho_k\| \leq \frac{M}{2}\|e_k\|^2,$$ where \(H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)\) and \(\rho_k = \int_0^1 (H_k - \nabla^2 f(x^\star+se_k))e_k\,ds\) collects the Hessian gap (\(\leq M \|e_k\|\) in operator norm, hence the \(\tfrac{M}{2}\|e_k\|^2\) after integration). Then $$e_{k+1} = e_k - H_k^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) = H_k^{-1}\rho_k \;\Rightarrow\; \|e_{k+1}\| \leq \|H_k^{-1}\| \,\frac{M}{2}\|e_k\|^2 \leq \frac{M}{2\mu}\|e_k\|^2. \;\blacksquare$$ If \(\|e_0\| \leq \mu/M\), the error at least halves at every step — and then decreases quadratically once below 1. ∎

Newton is not global: far from the minimum, the direction can be bad — hence damped Newton (step by line search) and the "self-concordant" analysis (Nesterov–Nemirovski) which gives a globally convergent version; this is the heart of interior-point LP/QP solvers (see the Responsible AI module, ch. 4).
Definition 7.2 — BFGS in three ideas (1) Goal: replace \(\nabla^2 f\) with \(B_k \succ 0\) updated at cost \(O(d^2)\). (2) Secant equation: impose \(B_{k+1} s_k = y_k\) with \(s_k = x_{k+1}-x_k\), \(y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_k)\) — the only second-order information extractable from gradients. (3) Rank-2 update (closest to \(B_k\) in the Frobenius sense under these constraints): $$B_{k+1} = B_k - \frac{B_k s_k s_k^\top B_k}{s_k^\top B_k s_k} + \frac{y_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}.$$ L-BFGS stores only the last \(m\) pairs \((s_k, y_k)\) and reconstructs the product \(B_k^{-1}\nabla f\) by recursion: it is the standard weapon for smooth convex losses in high dimension (and the default solver of scipy.optimize.minimize(method="L-BFGS-B")).
Example 7.3 — Quadratic speed, experienced Minimize \(f(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} + e^{-x}\)... hmm let us take \(f(x)=x\log x\) (\(x>0\), convex): Newton is exactly second-order there: \(f''(x) = 1/x\) and the iterate \(x_{k+1} = x_k - f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)\) can be computed by hand. Exercise 7: show that for \(f(x) = x^4\), starting from \(x_0 = 2\), Newton gives \(x_k = 2^{(1-(-1/3)^k \cdot \ldots)}\)... — the simplest is to observe it numerically: count the correct digits at each iteration, watch them double, then compare L-BFGS via SciPy on the smoothed LASSO of ch. 4 (in dimension \(d = 200\)).
