2. Duality and the Wasserstein distance

Theorem 2.1 — Kantorovich duality (complete proof in the discrete case) $$W_c(\mu, \nu) \;=\; \max_{(f, g) \in \Phi_c} \ \int f\, d\mu + \int g\, d\nu, \qquad \Phi_c = \big\{(f, g) \ :\ f(x) + g(y) \leq c(x, y)\ \forall (x,y)\big\}.$$ In the discrete case: \(\max_{f,g} \langle a, f\rangle + \langle b, g\rangle\) s.t. \(f_i + g_j \leq C_{ij}\).
Step 1 (the discrete problem is an LP). The constraints \(\Gamma \geq 0\), \(\Gamma\mathbf{1} = a\), \(\Gamma^\top\mathbf{1} = b\) are affine; the primal is feasible (e.g. \(\Gamma = ab^\top\)) and bounded (\(\langle C,\Gamma\rangle \geq 0\)). Step 2 (the dual). Write the dual of th. 0.1 by associating a multiplier \(f_i\) with the row constraint \(\sum_j \Gamma_{ij} = a_i\) and \(g_j\) with the column constraint \(\sum_i \Gamma_{ij} = b_j\): the dual of \(\min_{\Gamma \geq 0}\{\langle C, \Gamma\rangle\}\) under these equalities is \(\max_{f,g}\{\langle a, f\rangle + \langle b, g\rangle\ :\ f_i + g_j \leq C_{ij}\ \forall i,j\}\) — each variable \(\Gamma_{ij} \geq 0\) yields one dual inequality constraint. Step 3 (strong duality). The primal is feasible and bounded ⇒ strong duality (th. 0.1): the optima exist and coincide. Step 4 (reading the complementarity). At the optimal triple \((\Gamma^\star, f^\star, g^\star)\): $$\Gamma^\star_{ij} > 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ f^\star_i + g^\star_j = C_{ij},$$ i.e. mass travels only on saturated routes — it is this condition that ties the plan to the potentials (corollary 2.3).∎
Definition 2.2 — c-transform and Kantorovich potentials For a potential \(f\): \(f^c(y) = \inf_x\, \big[c(x,y) - f(x)\big]\) (c-transform); \(f\) is c-concave if \(f = (f^c)^{\bar c}\) (bitransform). At the optima of theorem 2.1, one may always assume \(g^\star = (f^\star)^c\) and \(f^\star = (g^\star)^{\bar c}\): replacing \(g\) by \(f^c \geq g\) can only increase the dual objective while remaining admissible. The optimal pair then reduces to one c-concave potential \(f^\star\) (and its dual).
Corollary 2.3 — Brenier's theorem (precise statement) and the quadratic case If \(\mu \ll \mathrm{Leb}\) and \(c(x,y) = \tfrac12\|x-y\|^2\):
  • the optimal plan is unique and induced by a map: \(\gamma^\star = (\mathrm{Id}, T)_\#\mu\);
  • \(T = \nabla\varphi\), the gradient of a convex function \(\varphi\); conversely, every admissible map of this form is optimal (cyclical monotonicity);
  • link with the dual: \(\varphi(x) = \tfrac12\|x\|^2 - f^\star(x)\), i.e. \(\ T(x) = x - \nabla f^\star(x)\) on the transported support.
Reading: optimal transport is a gradient — it deforms space continuously, without tearing; in 1D, \(T = F_\nu^{-1} \circ F_\mu\) (monotone rearrangement, prop. 2.6). For two Gaussians, \(T\) is affine (th. 2.7). In ML: \(T\) = the "mapping" we learn to adapt a domain (ch. 4) — cf. the Deep Learning module for the networks that approximate it.
Definition 2.4 — Wasserstein distance of order \(p\) With \(c = d^p\) on a metric space \((E, d)\): $$W_p(\mu, \nu) = \Big( \min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int d(x,y)^p\, d\gamma \Big)^{1/p}$$ on the set of probabilities with finite moment of order \(p\).
Theorem 2.5 — \(W_p\) is a distance (proved: separation and triangle inequality by gluing)
Symmetry: if \(\gamma\) is admissible for \((\mu,\nu)\), its transpose is admissible for \((\nu,\mu)\), at the same cost. Separation: \(W_p(\mu,\nu) = 0\) ⇒ there exists an optimal \(\gamma^\star\) with zero cost, supported on \(\{x = y\}\) (since \(d^p > 0\) off the diagonal); its marginals are then equal: \(\mu = \nu\). Triangle inequality (discrete): let \(\gamma^{12}\) be optimal between \(\mu^1, \mu^2\) and \(\gamma^{23}\) optimal between \(\mu^2, \mu^3\). Gluing. Set $$\pi_{i\ell} \;=\; \sum_j\ \frac{\gamma^{12}_{ij}\,\gamma^{23}_{j\ell}}{\mu^2_j},$$ the law of the pair \((X, Z)\) where we first draw \(Y \sim \mu^2\), then \(X \sim \gamma^{12}(\cdot|Y)\) and \(Z \sim \gamma^{23}(Y, \cdot)\). Marginals: \(\sum_\ell \pi_{i\ell} = \sum_j \gamma^{12}_{ij}\frac{\sum_\ell\gamma^{23}_{j\ell}}{\mu^2_j} = \sum_j \gamma^{12}_{ij} = \mu^1_i\), and likewise \(\sum_i \pi_{i\ell} = \mu^3_\ell\). So \(\pi\) is admissible for \((\mu^1, \mu^3)\), and by conditional Minkowski (the inequality \(\big(\mathbb{E}[d(X,Z)^p]\big)^{1/p} \leq \big(\mathbb{E}[d(X,Y)^p]\big)^{1/p} + \big(\mathbb{E}[d(Y,Z)^p]\big)^{1/p}\) is proved by applying Minkowski conditionally on \(Y\), then integrating): $$W_p(\mu^1,\mu^3) \leq \Big(\sum_{i\ell} \pi_{i\ell}\, d(x_i, z_\ell)^p\Big)^{1/p} \leq W_p(\mu^1,\mu^2) + W_p(\mu^2,\mu^3).\qquad \square$$∎
Narrow topology: what \(W_p\) "sees" On probabilities with finite moment of order \(p\), \(W_p(\mu_k \to \mu)\) ⇔ narrow convergence (against every bounded continuous test function) + convergence of the moments of order \(p\). Comparison with the usual divergences: \(\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu) = \infty\) as soon as the supports move apart, whereas \(W_p\) stays finite and continuous — two clouds shifted by \(\varepsilon\) are at distance \(\varepsilon\) (not "infinity"). It is this "flat" geometry (no support jump) that makes \(W\) usable in ML: informative gradients, interpolation \(\mu_t = ((1-t)\,\mathrm{Id} + tT)_\#\mu\) in the physical sense of displacement.
Proposition 2.6 — \(W_p\) in dimension 1: coupling of quantiles (proved in the discrete case) $$W_p(\mu, \nu) = \Big(\int_0^1 \big|F_\mu^{-1}(u) - F_\nu^{-1}(u)\big|^p\, du\Big)^{1/p},$$ where \(F^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x \ : \ F(x) \geq u\}\) is the quantile. The optimal plan matches the \(u\)-quantile of \(\mu\) with the \(u\)-quantile of \(\nu\); computable by sorting in \(\mathcal{O}(n\log n)\).
Discrete case, \(n\) equal masses. Step 1 (uncrossing). Consider a plan putting mass on \((x_1, y_2)\) and \((x_2, y_1)\) with \(x_1 \leq x_2\) and \(y_1 \leq y_2\) ("crossed routes"); move this mass to \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\): the marginals are preserved and the cost does not increase, since $$|x_1 - y_2|^p + |x_2 - y_1|^p \ \geq\ |x_1 - y_1|^p + |x_2 - y_2|^p$$ — setting \(a = x_1 - y_1 \leq b = x_2 - y_2\) and \(d = y_2 - y_1 \geq 0\), the right-hand side reads \(|a|^p + |b|^p\), the left one \(|a - d|^p + |b + d|^p\): moving two real numbers further apart when one is already the smaller one can only increase the sum of the \(p\)-th powers (convexity of \(|{\cdot}|^p\): the function \(t \mapsto |t-d|^p + |t+d|^p\)… one checks that the derivative vanishes at the midpoint and that the expression is minimal when the two quantities are "brought closer together"): a convex rearrangement inequality. Step 2. By iterating the uncrossing, an optimal plan exists that crosses no pair: it is the monotone coupling \(x_i \leftrightarrow y_{\sigma(i)}\) with increasing \(\sigma\) — the quantile matching. Step 3 (general case, statement). For arbitrary laws, the plan \((F_\mu^{-1}(U), F_\nu^{-1}(U))\) with \(U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]\) has the right marginals and is monotone, hence optimal (1D Brenier).∎
Theorem 2.7 — \(W_2\) between Gaussians: the Bures metric (proved) $$W_2^2\big(\mathcal{N}(m_1,\Sigma_1), \mathcal{N}(m_2,\Sigma_2)\big) = \|m_1 - m_2\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\Big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2\big(\Sigma_1^{1/2}\,\Sigma_2\,\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big).$$
Step 1 (centering). For every coupling \((X, Y)\), \(\mathbb{E}\|X - Y\|^2 = \|m_1 - m_2\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\tilde X - \tilde Y\|^2\) with \(\tilde X, \tilde Y\) centered (expand the square, the cross terms cancel): means and covariances transport separately, and the plan \((m_1 + \tilde X,\ m_2 + \tilde Y)\) attains the bound. Assume \(m_1 = m_2 = 0\). Step 2 (an affine candidate). Set \(M = \Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2\Sigma_1^{1/2}\) and $$A \;=\; \Sigma_1^{-1/2}\, M^{1/2}\, \Sigma_1^{-1/2}.$$ \(A\) is symmetric positive definite (a symmetric conjugate of \(M^{1/2}\)). The coupling \((X, Y = AX)\) with \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_1)\) is admissible: \(Y\) is a centered Gaussian with covariance \(A\Sigma_1A^\top = \Sigma_1^{-1/2}M\Sigma_1^{-1/2} = \Sigma_2\). Step 3 (cost of the candidate). \(\mathbb{E}\|X - AX\|^2 = \mathrm{tr}\big((I - A)\Sigma_1(I - A)^\top\big) = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(A\Sigma_1) + \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_2)\). Now \(\mathrm{tr}(A\Sigma_1) = \mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma_1^{-1/2}M^{1/2}\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big(M^{1/2}\big)\) (cyclicity): cost \(= \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(M^{1/2})\) — the announced formula. Step 4 (optimality via Brenier). \(T(x) = Ax = \nabla\big(\tfrac12 x^\top A x\big)\) is the gradient of a convex function (A symmetric ≥ 0) that sends \(\mu\) onto \(\nu\); by the converse of corollary 2.3 (sufficiency of cyclical monotonicity), this coupling is optimal. In 1D, \(A = \sigma_2/\sigma_1\): cost \((\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2\), consistent with prop. 2.6.∎
Corollary 2.8 — Wasserstein barycenter of 1D Gaussians (proved) The barycenter \(\displaystyle\arg\min_\nu \sum_k w_k\, W_2^2(\nu, \mu_k)\) of \(\mu_k = \mathcal{N}(m_k, \sigma_k^2)\), \(\sum w_k = 1\), is the Gaussian $$\mathcal{N}\Big(\sum_k w_k m_k,\ \ \big(\sum_k w_k \sigma_k\big)^{2}\Big).$$
By prop. 2.6, minimizing over \(\nu\) amounts to minimizing \(\sum_k w_k \int_0^1 |F_\nu^{-1}(u) - F_k^{-1}(u)|^2du\) in the quantile function \(F_\nu^{-1}\). Pointwise in \(u\), the optimization is a simple regression: the optimum is \(F_\nu^{-1}(u) = \sum_k w_k F_k^{-1}(u)\) (zero derivative — strictly convex function). Now \(F_k^{-1}(u) = m_k + \sigma_k\,\Phi^{-1}(u)\): $$F_\nu^{-1}(u) = \underbrace{\sum_k w_k m_k}_{m} + \underbrace{\sum_k w_k\sigma_k}_{\sigma}\,\Phi^{-1}(u),$$ which is exactly the quantile of \(\mathcal{N}(m, \sigma^2)\). Note that the "barycenter variance" \(\sigma^2 = (\sum w_k\sigma_k)^2\) is the square of the mean of the standard deviations — not the mean of the variances.∎
Bridge with the Signal Processing module The Bures formula of theorem 2.7 is exactly the Fréchet distance (FID) used to evaluate image generators and audio models: cf. the Signal Processing module M1P1, chapter 8, which prepares this reading — and whose "spectral loss" is a Gaussian \(W_2\) loss on spectrograms. The same mathematical object runs through both modules.
