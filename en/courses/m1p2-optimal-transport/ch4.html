4. OT and machine learning

Definition 4.1 — Domain adaptation, covariate shift A domain adaptation problem provides a labeled source \((x_i, y_i)_{i \leq n} \sim \mu_S\) on \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}\) and an unlabeled target \((z_j)_{j \leq m} \sim \mu_T\) on \(\mathcal{X}\). Working assumption (covariate shift): the labeling rule \(P(Y\,|\,X)\) is the same in both domains, but the marginals \(\mu_S \neq \mu_T\) (sensors, populations, image styles…). A classifier trained on the source suffers from the distribution shift; the OT idea: transport the source onto the target before learning — exactly Monge's problem of ch. 1, applied to clouds of features.
OTDA algorithm — transport, propagate the labels, predict (Courty, Flamary et al., IEEE TPAMI 2016)
  1. Costs: \(C_{ij} = \|x_i - z_j\|^2\) (centered-scaled features, possibly kernel or deep features, cf. the Deep Learning module M1P1, chapter 8 "transfer learning").
  2. Class regularization: we additionally know that the \(x_i\) carry labels — we penalize plans that scatter a single class, via a convex group-lasso-type penalty on the rows of \(\Gamma\) (the points of a same class are transported to the same place). Final cost: \(\langle C, \Gamma\rangle\) + class penalty, solved by LP (ch. 1) or by regularized Sinkhorn (ch. 3).
  3. Barycentric transport: each source point is sent to the plan-weighted average of its images: $$\hat T(x_i) \;=\; \frac{1}{a_i}\sum_j\ \Gamma_{ij}\, z_j,$$ and its label \(y_i\) follows (the plan is a soft coupling, not a map: this is the Monge barycentric map, optimal for \(c = \|\cdot\|^2\) by corollary 2.3). Label propagation follows: the label predicted for \(z_j\) is the (plan-weighted) majority of the classes arriving at \(z_j\).
  4. Learning: train the classifier on the transported pairs \((\hat T(x_i),\, y_i)\) — which live in the target space — then predict on the target.
  5. Out-of-sample: a new point \(z\) is predicted directly (it is already in the target space); to transport a new source point without recomputing the plan, interpolate the plan row of the \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(x\) in the source, or learn a map \(T_\theta \approx \hat T\) (a network, Deep Learning module).
Positioning: the module's instructor is a co-author of the paper — detailed in ch. 7. The JDOT extension (Courty, Flamary et al., NeurIPS 2017) transports the joint law \((X, Y)\) with a cost \(c\big((x,y),(z,y')\big) = \alpha\|x - z\|^2 + \mathcal{L}(y, y')\) that includes the label loss: unsupervised and semi-supervised adaptation in the same framework.
Proposition 4.2 — Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, or why GANs are called "Wasserstein" (proved sketch) For \(c = d\) (a distance), $$W_1(\mu, \nu) \;=\; \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}} \leq 1}\ \int f\, d\mu - \int f\, d\nu,$$ the sup being taken over the 1-Lipschitz functions.
Start from the duality of th. 2.1 with \(c = d\) and replace \(g\) by the c-transform \(g = f^c\) (def. 2.2: this does not decrease the objective). For a distance cost, \(f^c(y) = \inf_x\,[\,d(x,y) - f(x)\,]\). If \(f\) is 1-Lipschitz, taking \(x = y\) gives \(f^c(y) = -f(y)\) (the infimum is attained at \(x = y\) since \(f(x) \geq f(y) - d(x,y)\)), and the dual equals \(\int f\,d\mu - \int f\, d\nu\). Conversely, the constraint \(f(x) + f^c(y) \leq d(x,y)\) with \(y \to x\) forces \(|f(x) - f(x')| \leq d(x, x')\): every admissible dual pair can be written (up to normalization) with a 1-Lipschitz \(f\) — hence the formula (complete proof: Villani 2009, th. 5.10).∎
The WGAN (Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou, ICML 2017) minimizes precisely this quantity between the law of the data and that of a generator \(G_\theta\): $$\min_\theta\ \max_{D_\phi\ \mathrm{1\!-\!Lip}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathrm{data}}[D_\phi(x)] \ -\ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}[D_\phi(G_\theta(z))],$$ the "critic" \(D_\phi\) playing the role of a parametrized Kantorovich potential (the dual of th. 2.1 makes the loss differentiable in \(\theta\)). The 1-Lipschitz constraint in practice: weight clipping, gradient penalty (Gulrajani et al., 2017), spectral normalization (Miyato et al., 2018) — cf. the Deep Learning module M1P1, chapter 7 (generative models). A modern computable alternative: the debiased Sinkhorn loss of ch. 3 (th. 3.4: we backpropagate the potential), available with PyTorch/JAX backends in POT.
Definition 4.3 — Wasserstein barycenter (Agueh & Carlier, 2011) For measures \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K\) and weights \(w \in \Delta_K\) (the simplex): $$\mathrm{Bar}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K; w) \;=\; \arg\min_{\nu}\ \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, W_2^2(\nu, \mu_k).$$ The 1D Gaussian case is solved explicitly by corollary 2.8 (barycentric quantiles). General cases:
  • Fixed support (a grid of \(p\) points): the problem is convex in the barycenter weights — quadratic programming (Agueh-Carlier) or iterative Sinkhorn;
  • Iterated Bregman projections (IBP): the entropic version (Benamou et al., 2015): initialize \(\Gamma_k\) independently for each \(k\), then repeat for each \(k\) a Sinkhorn scaling step (th. 3.2) toward the common barycenter mass, then a rescaling toward the marginal \(\mu_k\) — a "squared" Sinkhorn iteration, parallelizable;
  • Free support (Cuturi & Doucet, 2014): optimize positions and weights of the barycenter points by alternating: (i) plans \(\Gamma_k\) toward each measure (OT), (ii) moving the barycenter points toward the barycenter of their images — the multi-measure extension of the barycentric transport formula of the OTDA algorithm.
Applications: interpolation of shapes and distributions, color transfer (histograms), atlases and averages in medical imaging, signal normalization (ch. 7: Convolutional Monge Mapping), barycenters of graphs (ch. 5, FGW). POT: ot.bregman.barycenter, ot.lp.free_support_barycenter.
Example 4.4 — Word Mover's Distance: the distance between documents as OT (Kusner et al., ICML 2015) A document is a distribution over the embeddings of its words: \(a_i \propto\) frequency (or frequency × idf) of word \(i\), point \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) its embedding (word2vec/GloVe — cf. the Text Mining and NLP module M1P2, chapter 3). The WMD between two documents is simply \(W_1\) between these two discrete distributions, with the Euclidean cost between embeddings: the transport plan "explains" the semantic proximity: "Obama" speaks to "president", "France" to "Germany". For large-scale nearest-neighbor search, lower bounds (relaxed distances between centroids) are precomputed so that exact OT is solved only on the candidates. 
import numpy as np, ot
# two partially shared "documents", fictitious embeddings
X = np.array([[0., 0.], [1., 0.], [10., 0.]])     # roi, reine, chat (source)
Y = np.array([[0.1, 0.], [1.2, 0.], [10.4, 0.]])  # queen, king, dog  (target)
a = np.array([.5, .3, .2]); b = np.array([.4, .4, .2])
C = ot.dist(X, Y)                                  # squared euclidean costs
print("WMD :", ot.emd2(a, b, C) ** 0.5)            # exact plan: ot.emd(a, b, C)
Spectral loss: Gaussian \(W_2\) as a closed-form, differentiable loss Compare two batches of generative data (images, audio) by fitting a Gaussian to their features and evaluating \(W_2^2\) with the closed formula of theorem 2.7 (Bures metric): this is the FID of image generators and the "Fréchet audio distance" of audio generators — a loss without a solver, differentiable, usable as a training loss (spectral loss). This is exactly the announced bridge with the Signal Processing module M1P1: chapter 8 (FID and Bures are presented there) and chapter 9 (bridge lab). In 1D (spectra, signals), return to quantiles (prop. 2.6); ot.gaussian.wass2 provides the Gaussian empirical estimate.
Theorem 4.5 — Statistical estimation of \(W\): the curse of dimensionality, and the Sinkhorn exception (statement) Let \(\mu_n\) be the empirical measure of \(n\) i.i.d. points \(\sim \mu\) on \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Then:
  • Exact OT: \(\mathbb{E}\,W_p(\mu_n, \mu) \asymp n^{-1/d}\) for \(d \geq 3\) (Dudley; sharp bounds by Fournier & Guillin, 2015). In high dimension, the empirical \(W_p\) distance between two samples of the same law remains of constant order — the "noise" does not decrease: using exact \(W_p\) as a loss in high dimension is statistically fragile;
  • Debiased Sinkhorn: the divergence \(S_\varepsilon\) of ch. 3 (debiased) is estimated at the parametric rate \(n^{-1/2}\) under regularity assumptions (Genevay, Chizat, Bach, Peyré & Rigollet, AISTATS 2019); the same holds for the sliced distance (def. 3.5, 1D projections).
