Introduction and overview

ModuleComputational Optimal Transport for ML and Fairness
CodeOption P2 (APM)
Team and workloadInstructor: Rémi Flamary (École Polytechnique, CMAP; co-creator of the POT library) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · from Monge and Kantorovich to Sinkhorn, with the ML applications: Wasserstein losses, domain adaptation, graphs (Gromov-Wasserstein, FGW, COOT) and fairness. Status in the programRecommended — LLGA track

Comparing two probability distributions — two point clouds, two datasets, two populations — is an omnipresent problem in machine learning. Optimal transport answers it by looking for the minimum-cost displacement plan, from which the Wasserstein distance derives. This module builds its theory (Monge, Kantorovich, duality) and its practice (the Sinkhorn algorithm), then tours the applications: Wasserstein losses, domain adaptation, Gromov-Wasserstein for graphs, and fairness. It is taught by Rémi Flamary, co-creator of the POT library.

What you will be able to do

  • Formulate Monge's transport problem and its Kantorovich relaxation, and relate them to linear programming.
  • Exploit duality to interpret the Wasserstein distance and its potentials.
  • Implement the Sinkhorn algorithm and understand the role of entropic regularization.
  • Use transport as a machine learning tool: Wasserstein losses, domain adaptation, barycenters.
  • Understand Gromov-Wasserstein to compare objects with no direct correspondence, typically graphs.
  • Apply transport to fairness questions, acting on distributions rather than on predictions.

Where this course fits in the program

Module of period 2 of M1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS): an option explicitly recommended for the LLGA track. It is the course that gives the master's program its common language for talking about distances between distributions.

  • Prerequisites — Machine Learning for the learning framework and Refresher in Statistics for handling distributions; the Signal Processing module (P1, same instructor) is useful without being a blocker, as this course extends its "signal for ML" chapter.
  • Downstream modules — the Gromov-Wasserstein chapter directly prepares Graph Generative Models and Applications (M2), which aligns relational structures by transport; the FID that evaluates the generative models of the Multimodal Generative AI module (same period) is a Wasserstein distance between distributions; finally, the fairness approach crosses paths with the master's concerns in responsible AI.

Course outline

  • Review: convexity and LP duality — the two reading keys for the rest of the module.
  • The transport problem — from Monge to Kantorovich: moving mass at minimum cost, and why we relax.
  • Duality and the Wasserstein distance — the dual viewpoint, and the geometry the distance induces on distributions.
  • Entropic regularization: Sinkhorn — making transport computable, iteration by iteration.
  • OT and machine learning — Wasserstein losses, domain adaptation, barycenters: the toolbox in action.
  • Gromov-Wasserstein and beyond — transporting between spaces with no correspondence, up to the FGW and COOT variants.
  • Fairness through transport — correcting distributional disparities between populations.
  • The instructor's research — a tour of the research work of Rémi Flamary.
  • Exercises and References — systematic training and sources to go further.

How to study this module

  • First absorb convexity and duality (the review section): almost every theorem of the course is a duality theorem in disguise.
  • Solve tiny discrete cases by hand (two or three points): the optimal transport plan takes shape there, and the intuition generalizes.
  • Experiment with the POT library, created by the instructor: varying the entropic regularization and observing the transport plan is the best way to understand Sinkhorn.
  • Relate each application to the generic problem "compare two distributions": a loss, domain adaptation or fairness are just different instances of this same question.
  • Take this module alongside Multimodal Generative AI (same period): the Wasserstein-GAN and the FID make full sense there.
  • Redo the exercises of each section before moving on to the next; the hand calculations prepare the software implementation.
The LLGA positioning Optimal transport is a cross-cutting language of the master's program: it ties together image generation (FID in Multimodal Generative AI), graph alignment (Gromov-Wasserstein, up to Graph Generative Models and Applications in M2) and questions of equity between groups. This versatility makes it a sought-after differentiator, both in industrial data science and in research — a profile the LLGA track explicitly encourages.
