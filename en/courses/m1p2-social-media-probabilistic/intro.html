This module offers a probabilistic theory of social networks: rather than analyzing an observed graph, networks are modeled as random processes — Erdős–Rényi graphs, small worlds, scale-free networks, epidemics, rumors, stochastic block models. This generational view explains why networks have the shape they have, and grounds the algorithms — community detection, influence — deployed on them. It is, literally, the course where statistical physics meets the platforms.

What you will be able to do

  • Handle the major random graph models — Erdős–Rényi, prescribed degrees, small worlds, scale-free — and their emergent properties.
  • Analyze epidemic processes spreading on a graph.
  • Study rumor spreading (push-pull) and influence mechanisms.
  • Understand the stochastic block model as a generative model of communities, and use it to detect them.
  • Connect these models to the large-scale distributed systems that run the platforms.
  • Simulate random graphs and processes with networkx, and confront simulations with real data.

Where this course fits in the program

An option of period 2 of M1, outside the recommended track — and a course unit not carried over into the 2026-2027 catalog: the last official sheet (2025-2026) lists no prerequisites. The module builds on the two P1 refreshers: Refresher in Statistics for probabilistic calculus and Refresher in Computer Science for graphs, both reactivated in chapter 0. Downstream, it provides the generative foundation of Graph Machine and Deep Learning for Generative AI — the stochastic block model and community detection are central there — and prepares Advanced Graph Neural Networks (M2), where these structures meet deep learning.

Course outline

  • Background: probability and graphs — the two languages of the module, put back in hand before any use.
  • Random graphs: Erdős–Rényi — the founding model and its threshold phenomena.
  • Prescribed degrees, small worlds, scale-free — bringing the models closer to real networks, and measuring what each one misses.
  • Epidemic processes on graphs — how a spread depends on the structure of the graph that carries it.
  • Rumors, push-pull and influence — the dissemination protocols and the influence questions that follow.
  • The stochastic block model — the stochastic block model: communities that are generated, and therefore detectable.
  • Community detection and applications — the algorithms that exploit the SBM and their uses.
  • Large-scale distributed systems — what these models say about the systems that run the platforms.
  • Research of the teaching team — the team's papers, read with the tools of the module.
  • Hands-on: networkx simulations — going from theorems to numerical experiments.
  • Exercises — threshold computations, spreads, and detections to carry out yourself.
  • References — the founding papers of each chapter.

How to study this module

  • Work through chapter 0 in full: discrete probability and graph vocabulary are needed from the very first theorem.
  • Simulate early and often: a random graph model is better understood plotted than stated — the networkx chapter is made for that.
  • For each model, ask yourself which real property it captures and which one it misses: that is the question that structures the module.
  • Plot the distributions (degrees, component sizes) instead of relying on averages.
  • Connect epidemics and rumors to platform news: the same models describe widely publicized phenomena.
  • Cross-reference with the Graph ML module of the same period: the SBM returns there as the foundation of learning on graphs.
The module in the LLGA master Networks are, along with text, the master's great structured data source: here they are treated as random processes, which prepares both for the M2 GNNs and for a critical reading of the platforms. The targeted skills — generative graph models, spreading, distributed systems — are at the heart of online platform jobs and of an active research field, at the crossroads of randomness and large-scale networks.
