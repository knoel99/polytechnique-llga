This module offers a probabilistic theory of social networks: rather than analyzing an observed graph, networks are modeled as random processes — Erdős–Rényi graphs, small worlds, scale-free networks, epidemics, rumors, stochastic block models. This generational view explains why networks have the shape they have, and grounds the algorithms — community detection, influence — deployed on them. It is, literally, the course where statistical physics meets the platforms.

  • Understand the stochastic block model as a generative model of communities, and use it to detect them.
  • Connect these models to the large-scale distributed systems that run the platforms.

0. Background: probability and graphs

The module's guiding thread: a social network is a random graph (who follows whom?), traversed by stochastic processes (what spreads, and how fast?). The two mathematical pillars are therefore discrete probability — distributions, moments, convergence — and graph theory. This chapter puts back in place the definitions and the three basic tools: the first and second moment methods, branching processes, and random walks.

L3 review — probabilistic toolbox (cf. Refresher Statistics module)
  • Standard distributions (ch. 3): binomial \(\mathrm{Bin}(n, p)\) with expectation \(np\); Poisson \(\mathrm{Pois}(c)\) with \(\mathbb{P}(X = k) = e^{-c} c^k / k!\), variance = mean; exponential with parameter \(\lambda\), memoryless — the waiting time of Poisson processes.
  • Convergence and concentration (ch. 4): weak law of large numbers, CLT, Markov's inequality \(\mathbb{P}(X \geq a) \leq \mathbb{E}[X]/a\) and Chebyshev's; this is the machinery behind the thresholds of ch. 1.
  • Estimation (ch. 5): maximum likelihood, bias, consistency — it will be used on the SBM (ch. 5) and on power laws (ch. 2).
  • Testing (ch. 6): hypothesis testing, type I/II errors — the detection threshold of ch. 5 is exactly the boundary where every test becomes powerless.
L3 review — graph toolbox (cf. Graph ML module, ch. 0)
  • Vocabulary: \(G = (V, E)\), degrees \(d_v\), neighbors \(\mathcal{N}(v)\), adjacency matrix \(A\), paths, connected components, diameter, clustering coefficient \(c_v = \frac{2\,|\text{edges in } \mathcal{N}(v)|}{d_v(d_v - 1)}\).
  • Spectrum (Graph ML module, ch. 4): eigenvalues of \(A\), spectral radius \(\rho(A)\) = largest eigenvalue in absolute value (connected graph: simple, positive, Perron–Frobenius vector with strictly positive coordinates).
  • Random walks (Graph ML module, ch. 3): \(P = D^{-1}A\), stationary distribution \(\pi_v = d_v / 2m\), mixing time — the underpinning of rumor protocols (ch. 4) and distributed algorithms (ch. 7).
  • Graph algorithms (Refresher CS module, ch. 5): BFS/DFS in \(O(n + m)\), connected components — the basic building block of the simulations in ch. 9.
Definition 0.1 — Random graph A random graph is a random variable taking values in the set of graphs (simple, undirected, unless stated otherwise) on a fixed set of \(n\) vertices. Two equivalent specifications dominate: by measure (a distribution over the \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) possible graphs) or by generative procedure (a randomized algorithm that builds the graph edge by edge, or vertex by vertex). Chapters 1, 2 and 5 give the three canonical generative models: Erdős–Rényi, configuration/Barabási–Albert, and the stochastic block model.
Definition 0.2 — Branching process (Galton–Watson) Starting from one initial individual, each individual has an i.i.d. random offspring with distribution \((p_k)\), of mean \(m = \sum_k k\, p_k\). If \(Z_t\) denotes the size of generation \(t\): \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t] = m^t\). The process dies out almost surely if \(m \leq 1\) and survives with strictly positive probability if \(m > 1\) (the survival event has as its probability the smallest nonzero solution of \(s = G(s)\), where \(G\) is the generating function of the offspring). This is the universal tool for "early stages of processes": giant component (ch. 1), epidemic (ch. 3), rumor spreading (ch. 4).
Proposition 0.3 — First moment method For any nonnegative integer random variable \(X\): \(\mathbb{P}(X \geq 1) \leq \mathbb{E}[X]\) (Markov). Consequently, if \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \to 0\) then \(X_n = 0\) with probability tending to 1; and if \(\mathrm{Var}(X_n) = o(\mathbb{E}[X_n]^2)\) with \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \to \infty\), then \(X_n > 0\) with probability tending to 1 (second moment, via Chebyshev: \(\mathbb{P}(X_n = 0) \leq \mathbb{P}(|X_n - \mathbb{E}X_n| \geq \mathbb{E}X_n) \leq \mathrm{Var}(X_n)/\mathbb{E}[X_n]^2 \to 0\)). All of ch. 1 rests on this pair.

1. Random graphs: Erdős–Rényi

The \(G(n, p)\) model of Gilbert–Erdős–Rényi is the primitive atom: \(n\) vertices, each pair connected independently with probability \(p\). It has no structure — and that is precisely what makes it the touchstone: a phenomenon observed on a real network is "significant" only if it is improbable under \(G(n, p)\) at the same size and density (this is the role of the null model in modularity, ch. 6).

Definition 1.1 — The two Erdős–Rényi models \(G(n, p)\): each of the \(\binom{n}{2}\) edges is present independently with probability \(p\). \(G(n, m)\): a uniform choice among graphs with exactly \(m\) edges. For \(m \approx p \binom{n}{2}\), the two are interchangeable to within \(o(m)\) edges (we will move from one to the other without notice). Immediate expectations: $$\mathbb{E}[|E|] = p \binom{n}{2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{deg}(v)] = (n-1) p =: d \quad (\text{mean degree }).$$
Proposition 1.2 — Degree distribution: binomial, then Poisson In \(G(n, p)\), the degree \(D_v\) follows the law \(\mathrm{Bin}(n-1, p)\). In the sparse regime \(p = c/n\) (mean degree \(c\) constant), \(D_v\) converges in law to \(\mathrm{Pois}(c)\): $$\binom{n-1}{k} \Big( \frac{c}{n} \Big)^k \Big( 1 - \frac{c}{n} \Big)^{n-1-k} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} e^{-c}\, \frac{c^k}{k!}.$$
First factor: \(\binom{n-1}{k} \sim \frac{(n-1)^k}{k!}\), and \((n-1)^k (c/n)^k \to c^k\). Second: \((1 - c/n)^{n-1-k} = \exp\big( (n - 1 - k) \ln(1 - c/n) \big) = \exp\big( -(n-1-k) (c/n + O(1/n^2)) \big) \to e^{-c}\), since \(k\) is fixed and \((n-1-k)/n \to 1\). ∎
Theorem 1.3 (emergence of the giant component, \(p = c/n\)) Let \(C_1\) be the largest connected component of \(G(n, c/n)\).
  1. if \(c \leq 1\): \(|C_1| = O(\log n)\) with high probability; all components are trees or "trees plus one edge";
  2. if \(c > 1\): \(|C_1| = \rho\, n + o(n)\) with high probability, where \(\rho \in (0, 1]\) is the unique solution of $$\rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho},$$ and all other components have size \(O(\log n)\).
We call \(c = 1\) the percolation threshold — the most famous phase transition of networks.
(Sketch by exploration and branching.) Let us explore the component of a vertex \(v\) by queue (BFS): each discovered vertex presents \(X\) not-yet-seen neighbors, where \(X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n - O(\text{seen}), c/n) \approx \mathrm{Pois}(c)\), and these laws remain nearly independent as long as the exploration stays small compared to \(n\). The component of \(v\) therefore behaves like a Galton–Watson process of mean \(c\) (Definition 0.2): almost-sure extinction if \(c \leq 1\) (components \(O(\log n)\); in the critical regime \(c = 1\) the size is \(n^{2/3}\); outside the syllabus); survival with probability \(\rho\) if \(c > 1\), where \(\rho\) is the survival probability of the branching: \(\rho = 1 - G(1 - \rho)\) with \(G\) the generating function of \(\mathrm{Pois}(c)\), that is, \(\rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho}\). The fraction of vertices in "infinite" components equals \(\rho\) — this is the relative size of \(C_1\). ∎
Theorem 1.4 (connectivity threshold) — proved in sketch form For \(p = (\ln n + \omega(1))/n\) (i.e. \(np = \ln n + \omega(1)\)), \(G(n, p)\) is connected with probability tending to 1. For \(p = (\ln n - \omega(1))/n\), it is disconnected with probability tending to 1. More precisely, for \(p = (\ln n + t)/n\) with \(t\) fixed: $$\mathbb{P}(G(n,p) \text{ connected}) \longrightarrow \exp\big( -e^{-t} \big).$$ The connectivity threshold coincides with the threshold for the disappearance of isolated vertices: at the moment the last isolated vertex disappears, the graph becomes connected.
(Sketch, first and second moments — cf. Proposition 0.3.) Let \(X\) be the number of isolated vertices: $$\mathbb{E}[X] = n (1 - p)^{n-1} = n \exp\big( (n-1)\ln(1 - p) \big) \approx n\, e^{-np} = e^{-t} \quad \text{for } np = \ln n + t.$$ Regime \(np = \ln n - \omega(1)\) (so \(t = -\omega(1)\), \(\mathbb{E}[X] = e^{-t} \to \infty\)): an isolated vertex exists. The events "\(v\) isolated" are not independent, but nearly: \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X](1 - o(1)) + \text{cross terms}\, o(\mathbb{E}[X]^2)\) (the covariances \(\mathbb{P}(u, v \text{ both isolated}) \leq (1-p)^{2(n-1)-1}\) contribute only \(O(\mathbb{E}[X]^2/n)\)). Chebyshev gives \(\mathbb{P}(X = 0) \leq \mathrm{Var}(X)/\mathbb{E}[X]^2 \to 0\): an isolated vertex exists, the graph is not connected.

Regime \(np = \ln n + \omega(1)\) (\(\mathbb{E}[X] \to 0\)): no more isolated vertices. By Markov, \(\mathbb{P}(X \geq 1) \leq \mathbb{E}[X] \to 0\). It remains to see that "no isolated vertex" \(\Rightarrow\) "connected" — this is the structural theorem: if all components have at least 2 vertices, each contains a path or a cycle, and the arithmetic deficit forces components of size \(O(\log n)\); their number is then \(O(n/\log n) \cdot (1-p)^{\Omega(\log n)} \to 0\) (each pair of components of size \(\geq 2\) not connected costs a factor \((1-p)^{s s'}\) that is huge when \(s, s' \geq 2\)): a single component remains. The limit \(\exp(-e^{-t})\) is the classical Gumbel law of extremes — the first isolated vertex to disappear is an extreme event. ∎
Proposition 1.5 — Diameter in the dense regime If \(np \geq \ln n \cdot \omega(1)\) (in particular \(np = n^{a}\), \(a > 0\)), the diameter of \(G(n, p)\) equals $$\mathrm{diam} = (1 + o(1)) \frac{\ln n}{\ln (np)}$$ with high probability. Intuition: the neighborhood of a vertex grows like \((np)^r\) after \(r\) BFS steps (each new vertex brings \(\approx np\) neighbors), and covers \(n\) vertices when \((np)^r \approx n\). For \(p = c/n\), \(c > 1\), the giant component has diameter \(\Theta(\log n)\) and typical distances \(\frac{\log n}{\log c}\) — the "six degrees of separation" phenomenon (ch. 2).
Theorem 1.6 (thresholds for subgraph appearance — triangles) For \(p = o(1/n)\): \(G(n,p)\) contains no triangle with probability tending to 1. For \(p = \omega(1/n)\): it contains at least one with probability tending to 1. More generally (Erdős–Rényi 1960), for \(H\) strictly balanced, the threshold for the appearance of a copy of \(H\) is \(n^{-1/m(H)}\), where \(m(H) = \max_{J \subseteq H} e_J / v_J\) (maximum density of a subgraph); for the triangle \(m = 1\), for \(K_4\), \(m = 3/2\).
(First moment, in full.) Let \(T\) be the number of triangles: \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \binom{n}{3} p^3 \sim \frac{(np)^3}{6}\). If \(np \to 0\), then \(\mathbb{E}[T] \to 0\) and Markov concludes: \(\mathbb{P}(T \geq 1) \to 0\).

(Second moment, sketch.) If \(np \to \infty\), we compute \(\mathrm{Var}(T) = \sum_{T_1, T_2} \big( \mathbb{P}(T_1 \wedge T_2) - p^6 \big)\): pairs of triangles that are disjoint or share a vertex are independent or nearly so (zero contribution, or \(\binom{n}{3}\binom{n}{2} p^5 = O(\mathbb{E}[T] \cdot n^2 p^2 / np) \ldots\)); only pairs sharing an edge contribute, in the amount \(\binom{n}{3} O(n) p^5 = o(\mathbb{E}[T]^2)\) as soon as \(np \to \infty\). Chebyshev concludes: \(T \geq 1\) with probability tending to 1. Exercise 3 asks for the complete computation. ∎

2. Prescribed degrees, small worlds, scale-free

Definition 2.1 — Configuration model (Bollobás; Molloy–Reed) Take a degree sequence \(d_1, \dots, d_n\) with even sum \(2m\). The configuration model attaches \(d_i\) half-edges (stubs) to vertex \(i\), then pairs the \(2m\) half-edges uniformly at random (\((2m - 1)!!\) possible pairings). The result is a multigraph: loops and multiple edges are possible, \(O(1)\) on average when the \(d_i\) remain \(o(\sqrt n)\); in practice one erases them. The point: the degree distribution is imposed — it can be fitted to a real network — while everything else remains "maximally random".
Theorem 2.2 (Molloy–Reed criterion, statement) Let \(D\) be the degree distribution (empirical limit \(p_k = \frac{1}{n}|\{i : d_i = k\}|\)). The configuration graph admits a giant component of strictly positive fraction if and only if $$\sum_{k} k (k - 2)\, p_k > 0 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \mathbb{E}[D^2] > 2\, \mathbb{E}[D].$$ The heuristic: when exploring the component of a vertex, each traversed edge leads to a new vertex whose excess degree follows the biased distribution \(\tilde p_k = \frac{(k+1) p_{k+1}}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\) (size bias: an edge arrives more often at a high-degree vertex); the exploration branching has mean \(\frac{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\), and the threshold is this mean crossing above 1. This same \(\frac{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\) will be the \(R_0\) of epidemics on graphs with heterogeneous degrees (ch. 3).
Example 2.3 — Power law: \(p_k \sim C k^{-\gamma}\) For \(\gamma \in (2, 3)\) (the regime of real social networks — Twitter, the web, collaborations): \(\mathbb{E}[D]\) is finite but \(\mathbb{E}[D^2]\) diverges — criterion 2.2 is widely positive: robust giant component. Cascading consequences (ch. 3): epidemic threshold tending to 0, robustness to random failures but fragility to targeted attacks on hubs. For \(\gamma > 3\), everything is finite and the world becomes Poissonian again to first approximation.
Definition 2.4 — Watts–Strogatz small world (1998) Start from a ring of \(n\) vertices, each connected to its \(K\) nearest neighbors (a highly clustered, very "wide" graph: diameter \(\Theta(n/K)\)). Each edge is rewired independently with probability \(\beta\), to a uniform destination (no duplicates). The model interpolates: \(\beta = 0\) gives the "circle" world, \(\beta = 1\) gives a graph close to Erdős–Rényi. The spectacular result of Watts and Strogatz: as soon as \(\beta \approx 0.01\), the diameter drops to \(\Theta(\log n)\) while the clustering coefficient stays close to its \(\beta = 0\) value — the two quantities become decoupled. This is the formalization of Milgram's experiments (1967): very local networks, crossed via a few random links — "six degrees of separation". Kleinberg (2000) shows the sharp condition for such a network to be navigable (finding a short path in a decentralized way, without a global map): the distribution of shortcut links must scale as \(r^{-2}\) — exactly what social networks rebuild.
Theorem 2.5 — Barabási–Albert: preferential attachment produces \(p_k \sim k^{-3}\) (master equation) The model: vertices are added one at a time; vertex \(t\) arrives with \(m\) edges, each connected to an existing vertex \(i\) with probability \(\Pi(i) = d_i(t) / \sum_j d_j(t)\) — "the rich get richer" (Matthew effect). Then the degree distribution converges to $$p_k \;\sim\; \frac{2 m^2}{k^{3}} \qquad (k \geq m),$$ with an exponent independent of \(m\).
(Continuous method — the master equation sketched.) Let \(d_i(t)\) denote the degree of vertex \(i\) (arrived at time \(t_i\)) at time \(t\), with \(\sum_j d_j(t) = 2mt\). The master equation reads, in unit time steps, $$\mathbb{E}[d_i(t+1) - d_i(t)] = m \cdot \frac{d_i(t)}{2 m t},$$ hence the differential equation \(\frac{\partial d_i}{\partial t} = \frac{d_i}{2t}\), with solution $$d_i(t) = m \Big( \frac{t}{t_i} \Big)^{1/2}.$$ Old vertices (\(t_i\) small) are thus proportionally richer — exactly the heterogeneity missing from Erdős–Rényi. Now for the distribution: for \(k \geq m\), $$\mathbb{P}(d_i(t) < k) = \mathbb{P}\Big( t_i > \frac{m^2 t}{k^2} \Big) = 1 - \frac{m^2 t}{k^2 (t - m)} \approx 1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2},$$ using that the arrival time \(t_i\) is (asymptotically) uniform on \([0, t]\). Differentiating: $$p_k = -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} k} \Big( 1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2} \Big) \Big/ 1 = \frac{2 m^2}{k^3},$$ exponent \(\gamma = 3\), natural cutoff \(k_{\max} \sim \sqrt n\) (the degree of the oldest vertex \(d_1(n) = m \sqrt n\)). ∎
Estimating an exponent — the pitfall Regressing \(\log p_k\) against \(\log k\) on a log-log plot is biased (noisy tails, empty bins); the reference is Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (SIAM Review 2009): Hill estimator on the threshold \(k_{\min}\) chosen by a KS test, then a likelihood-ratio test against the log-normal/exponential alternative (Refresher Statistics module, ch. 5). Many published "power laws" do not survive this protocol.

3. Epidemic processes on graphs

A meme, a virus, a rumor: the same mathematics. The canonical model is the SIR epidemic (Susceptible → Infectious → Recovered) or its relapse version SIS, posed no longer on a homogeneous population but on a contact graph. The central question: is there a threshold below which the epidemic dies out quickly — and what does this threshold become when the graph is heterogeneous?

Definition 3.1 — SIR and SIS on a graph Each vertex is in a state; the infection and recovery clocks are independent exponentials:
  • SIR: an infectious vertex transmits the infection to each healthy neighbor at rate \(\beta\), and recovers at rate \(\delta\) (permanent immunity). Key parameter: the ratio \(\tau = \beta / \delta\).
  • SIS: same transmission, but the vertex becomes susceptible again at rate \(\delta\) — the epidemic can become endemic (survive indefinitely).
  • Discrete-time version (TME): each edge \(uv\) with \(u\) infected transmits at each step with probability \(p = 1 - e^{-\beta}\), each infected vertex recovers with probability \(1 - e^{-\delta}\).
On a homogeneous population (perfect mixing, no graph), the classical SIR gives the basic reproduction number \(R_0 = \beta S_0 / \delta\) and the threshold \(R_0 > 1\) — this is the model of epidemiology courses; the graph replaces the mass term \(S_0\).
Theorem 3.2 — Branching approximation: the threshold on \(G(n, c/n)\) On \(G(n, c/n)\) with a single initial infective, if \(c \tau \leq 1\) (\(\tau = \beta/\delta\)), the epidemic reaches \(o(n)\) vertices; if \(c \tau > 1\), it reaches a strictly positive fraction of the vertices with strictly positive probability (a "massive" epidemic), and the final fraction is the size of the giant component of a \(G(n, c\tau/n)\) (cf. Theorem 3.4).
(Sketch.) At the very beginning, infection attempts on distinct edges are independent exponential races: on an edge \((u, v)\) with \(u\) infected, the infection of \(v\) wins the race against the recovery of \(u\) with probability \(\frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}\). Each infective therefore has, to first approximation (no collisions — the targets are all distinct), an infectious offspring \(\mathrm{Bin}\big( \mathrm{Pois}(c), \frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta} \big) \approx \mathrm{Pois}\big( c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta} \big)\) of mean $$R_0^{\text{graph}} = c\, \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta} = \frac{c \tau}{1 + \tau},$$ threshold \(R_0^{\text{graph}} = 1\), i.e. \(\tau = \frac{1}{c - 1}\) — up to constants, \(c\tau = 1\). In the sparse regime, the precision of the constant hardly matters: the subcritical branching extinguishes the epidemic with \(O(\log n)\) infectives; the supercritical one lets it survive long enough to see \(n\) vertices. ∎
Theorem 3.3 (Ganesh, Massoulié, Towsley, INFOCOM 2005) — the spectral threshold of SIS For SIS on a fixed graph \(G\) with adjacency matrix \(A\), spectral radius \(\rho(A)\):
  1. if \(\beta / \delta < 1 / \rho(A)\): the epidemic dies out in \(O(\log n)\) time with probability tending to 1 (fast extinction);
  2. conversely, as soon as \(\beta/\delta\) exceeds \(1/\rho(A)\) by a factor depending on the geometry of the graph, the survival time is exponential in \(n\): the epidemic becomes endemic. Later works (Chakrabarti et al., 2008) identify \(1/\rho(A)\) as the threshold of the mean-field approximation: the epidemic threshold of a network is spectral.
(Proof of (i), sketch — spectral supermartingale.) Let \(u\) be the Perron–Frobenius vector of \(A\) (\(u > 0\), \(A u = \rho u\)) and \(\Phi_t = \sum_i u_i x_i(t)\) where \(x_i(t) \in \{0, 1\}\) is the infection indicator. Each infective \(i\) recovers at rate \(\delta\) (drift \(-\delta u_i\)) and infects each neighbor \(j\) at rate \(\beta\) (drift \(+\beta u_j\)); writing \(x\) for the state vector: $$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathbb{E}[\Phi_t \mid x] \;\leq\; \sum_i x_i \big( -\delta u_i + \beta \sum_{j \sim i} u_j \big) = \big( \beta \rho(A) - \delta \big) \sum_i x_i u_i = \big( \beta \rho - \delta \big)\, \mathbb{E}[\Phi_t \mid x].$$ If \(\beta \rho < \delta\), \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi_t] \leq \Phi_0\, e^{-(\delta - \beta \rho) t}\); since \(\Phi_t = 0\) as soon as the epidemic is extinct and \(\Phi_t \geq (\min_i u_i) \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{at least one infective}\}}\), $$\mathbb{P}(\text{not extinct at } t) \;\leq\; \frac{\Phi_0}{\min_i u_i}\; e^{-(\delta - \beta \rho) t},$$ hence extinction in \(O(\log n)\) (the prefactor is polynomial in \(n\)). ∎
Theorem 3.4 — Final SIR = percolation: the final size is a giant component For SIR on \(G(n, c/n)\), the final size of the epidemic started from a random vertex has the same law as the size of the connected component of that vertex in the percolated graph \(G(n, c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}/n)\), where each edge is kept with probability \(\frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}\) (probability that transmission wins the race, cf. 3.2). In particular: massive epidemic \(\iff\) giant component of the percolated graph — Theorem 1.3 applies again with parameter \(c \tau' = c \frac{\beta}{\beta + \delta}\), and the final fraction of the infected population is \(\rho\) solving \(\rho = 1 - e^{-c \tau' \rho}\).
(Coupling.) Order the events: for each edge \((u, v)\), the "transmission vs recovery" race can be drawn in advance, independently of everything else: mark the edge "open" with probability \(\frac{\beta}{\beta+\delta}\) (transmission clock faster than the recovery clock, all independent). The strong Markov property of the process then shows that the final set of infectives is exactly the set of vertices reachable from the origin in the subgraph of open edges — the percolated connected component. This is the classic epidemic \(\leftrightarrow\) percolation bridge (Grassberger 1983; Newman 2002). ∎
Theorem 3.5 (Pastor-Satorras–Vespignani, 2001) — vanishing threshold on scale-free networks On a configuration graph whose degree distribution is \(p_k \sim C k^{-\gamma}\):
  1. the effective epidemic threshold equals \(\tau_c = \frac{\mathbb{E}[D]}{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}\) (the reciprocal of the \(R_0\) of Theorem 2.2);
  2. if \(2 < \gamma \leq 3\), \(\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]\) diverges with \(n\): \(\tau_c \to 0\) — every contagion becomes epidemic on an infinite scale-free network.
Engineering reading: vaccinating at random does not change the divergence; targeting hubs ("acquaintance" or degree-based vaccination) reintroduces a positive threshold. This is the quantitative argument behind the moderation of very high-audience accounts on platforms (and behind targeted vaccination strategies).
From model to data: \(R_0\) in practice Estimating \(\beta, \delta\) or \(R_0\) from cascade data (retweet timestamps): maximum likelihood on infection times (Hawkes processes / contagion chains — Refresher Statistics module, ch. 5); confidence intervals by bootstrap (ch. 4–6). The TME of ch. 9 runs the reverse experiment: simulate, measure the final size, and check the thresholds 3.2 and 3.5 on ER vs Barabási–Albert.

4. Rumors, push-pull and influence

Spreading information to \(n\) machines without a directory or a coordinator: the gossip protocol is the elegant solution — each node calls a random neighbor every round. It is the building block of rumors (Karp et al. 2000), of distributed aggregation (ch. 7) and the distant ancestor of blockchain blocks disseminated by "flooding". The quantitative question: how many rounds until everyone is informed?

Definition 4.1 — Push, pull, push-pull protocols (telephone call model) Discrete time; at each round, each node chooses an interlocutor uniformly at random among the other \(n - 1\) (complete graph):
  • push: informed nodes call to transmit;
  • pull: uninformed nodes call to ask;
  • push-pull: both at once — the call transmits as soon as either side knows.
Theorem 4.2 (Karp, Schindelhauer, Shenker, Vöcking, FOCS 2000) On the complete graph \(K_n\), the push-pull protocol informs all \(n\) nodes in $$\log_2 n + \ln n + o(\log n) \quad \text{rounds, with probability } 1 - o(1/n),$$ and the push-only protocol reaches the same asymptotic speed \(\log_2 n + \ln n + o(\log n)\); \(\log_2 n + \ln n\) is optimal in the model.
(Sketch in three phases — where the two constants come from.) Let \(k_t\) be the number of informed nodes and \(s_t = n - k_t\) the ignorant ones.
  1. Doubling (\(k \leq n/2\)): the term \(\log_2 n\). Each informed node pushes to a uniform target; the probability of hitting an uninformed node is \(1 - k/n \geq 1/2\), so \(\mathbb{E}[k_{t+1} \mid k_t] \geq k_t (1 + \frac{1}{2})\): geometric growth of ratio \(\approx 3/2\) per round, hence \(\log_2 n + O(1)\) rounds to go from 1 to \(n/2\) (the deviation from the expectation is controlled by a supermartingale).
  2. Exponential decay of ignorance (\(k \geq n/2\)): the term \(\ln n\). An ignorant node survives a push round if none of the \(k_t\) informed nodes has called it: \(\mathbb{P}(\text{survival}) = (1 - \frac{1}{n})^{k_t} \approx e^{-k_t/n}\). When \(k_t = n - s_t \approx n\), each ignorant node survives with probability \(\approx e^{-1}\) per round: \(s_t\) decays geometrically with ratio \(\approx e^{-1}\), and it takes \(\ln n\) rounds to go from \(\Theta(n)\) ignorants down to the last ones — the signature of the coupon collector (Refresher Statistics module, ch. 3): each round "covers" \(k_t\) random vertices, and covering all \(n\) vertices takes \(\ln n\) rounds.
  3. The role of pull. Pull does not change the two leading constants but speeds up the intermediate phase: an ignorant node that calls at random reaches an informed node with probability \(k_t/n\), which bounds survival by \(e^{-k_t/n} \cdot (1 - k_t/n)\) and smooths the transition. The technical difficulty of the paper — beyond this sketch — is controlling the deviations of both phases simultaneously ("forward-backward" analysis).
∎
General graphs, and communication costs On a graph of maximum degree \(\Delta\), push-pull takes \(\Theta(\Delta \log n)\) rounds in the worst case (the information must cross the bottlenecks); on the random graphs of ch. 1–2, \(\Theta(\log n)\) suffices. Karp et al. also show that push alone can be made message-efficient: \(O(n \log \log n)\) transmissions with a "lazy" variant — hence its use in peer-to-peer systems (ch. 7). Modern variants (pull with lists of new messages, protocols of Doerr et al.) achieve the same speed with robustness to failures.
Definition 4.3 — Influence maximization (Kempe, Kleinberg, Tardos, 2003) Independent cascade (IC) model: each edge \((u,v)\) is "active" independently with probability \(p_{uv}\); content starting from set \(S\) propagates along active edges (once per edge). The influence function is \(\sigma(S) = \mathbb{E}[|R(S)|]\), the expected size of the set reachable from \(S\). Problem: choose \(S\) of cardinality \(\leq k\) maximizing \(\sigma(S)\) — what "seeding" budget for a viral campaign? (The linear threshold model — each vertex activates when the sum of weights of active neighbors exceeds a random threshold — is the classic alternative, with the same complexity.)
Theorem 4.4 — \(\sigma\) is submodular, and greedy is \((1 - 1/e)\)-optimal
  1. \(\sigma\) is monotone (\(S \subseteq T \Rightarrow \sigma(S) \leq \sigma(T)\)) and submodular: \(\sigma(S \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(S) \geq \sigma(T \cup \{v\}) - \sigma(T)\) for \(S \subseteq T\) — marginal returns decrease;
  2. the greedy algorithm (iteratively add the vertex of maximal marginal gain) returns \(S_g\) such that $$\sigma(S_g) \;\geq\; \Big( 1 - \frac{1}{e} \Big) \sigma(S^\ast),$$ where \(S^\ast\) is the optimum (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher bound, 1978);
  3. the exact optimization problem is NP-hard (reduction from Set Cover); achieving \((1 - 1/e + \varepsilon)\) for any \(\varepsilon > 0\) is NP-hard as well.
Submodularity (live-edge coupling). Draw in advance, once and for all, the "live/dead" state of each edge (coupling of Theorem 3.4); in this world, \(R(S)\) is the set of vertices reachable from \(S\) in the live subgraph, and reachability is clearly monotone and submodular: if \(w\) is reachable from \(S \cup \{v\}\) but not from \(S\), then every live path from \(S \cup \{v\}\) to \(w\) goes through \(v\), so \(w\) is reachable from \(\{v\}\); hence \(R(S) \cup R(\{v\}) \supseteq R(S \cup \{v\})\) and the marginal gain in reached vertices is smaller on the larger set. Expectation preserves the order. \(\square\)

Greedy. By submodularity and monotonicity, the greedy marginal gains \(g_1 \geq g_2 \geq \dots\) satisfy \(\sigma(S^\ast) \leq \sigma(S_g) + \sum_{v \in S^\ast \setminus S_g} g_v \leq \sigma(S_g) + k\, g_t\) for any step \(t\) — choosing \(t = k\) and summing the recursion \(\sigma(S_{i+1}) \geq \sigma(S_i)(1 - 1/k)\) gives \(\sigma(S_g) \geq (1 - (1 - 1/k)^k) \sigma(S^\ast) \geq (1 - e^{-1}) \sigma(S^\ast)\). ∎
From theory to the platform Monte-Carlo estimators of \(\sigma\) are costly (10,000 simulations per candidate); the CELF trick (Leskovec et al., 2007) divides the cost by ~700 by exploiting the decrease of marginal gains to evaluate lazily. Applications: viral marketing (choice of seeders), early detection of contagions (sensors), vaccination (ch. 3). The dark symmetry: the same algorithms optimize disinformation — hence the ongoing research on inoculation (pre-bunking, "curative" bots) and models of competition between cascades.

5. The stochastic block model

Communities: groups of vertices better connected within themselves than between each other. The stochastic block model (SBM) is its canonical generative model — the equivalent, for graphs, of Gaussians for point clouds. It makes it possible to ask the right questions: when is the block structure detectable? Which algorithm achieves it? The answers involve exact thresholds — two of which are due to the module's instructor (ch. 8).

Definition 5.1 — SBM Parameters: \(n\) vertices, \(q\) blocks, proportions \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_q)\), connectivity matrix \(P = (p_{ab}) \in [0, 1]^{q \times q}\). Generation: each vertex receives a block \(\sigma(v) \sim \pi\) independently; each pair \((u, v)\) is connected with probability \(p_{\sigma(u)\sigma(v)}\), independently. The textbook case of "two symmetric blocks": \(q = 2\), \(\pi = (1/2, 1/2)\), \(p_{\mathrm{in}} = a/n\), \(p_{\mathrm{out}} = b/n\) (two communities of mean degree \(\frac{a+b}{2}\), all the more separated as \(a > b\)). The SBM with heterogeneous degrees within blocks (DC-SBM, Karrer–Newman 2011) corrects the bias "hubs become blocks".
Definition 5.2 — The three difficulty regimes
  • Detection (sparse, degrees \(O(1)\)): a partition \(\hat\sigma\) better correlated with \(\sigma\) than chance (more than 50% of correct pairs in \(q = 2\)). The realistic regime for large networks.
  • Partial recovery: error fraction \(\to 0\) asymptotically.
  • Exact recovery (dense, degrees \(\Theta(\log n)\)): \(\mathbb{P}(\hat\sigma = \sigma) \to 1\). For two symmetric blocks with \(p_{\mathrm{in}} = a \log n/n\), \(p_{\mathrm{out}} = b \log n / n\): the exact threshold is \(\sqrt a - \sqrt b > \sqrt 2\) (Abbe, Bandeira, Hall; Mossel, Neeman, Sly): above it, the maximum likelihood — NP-hard in general — is achievable there by semidefinite relaxation.
Theorem 5.3 — Kesten–Stigum and detection thresholds (statements) For two symmetric blocks \((a, b)/n\):
  1. Impossibility below KS. If \((a - b)^2 \leq 2(a + b)\), no method does better than chance (the model is "contiguous" to Erdős–Rényi of degree \(\frac{a+b}{2}\)) — conjectured by Decelle, Krzakala, Moore, Zdeborová (2011); impossibility is proved by Mossel, Neeman, Sly.
  2. Feasibility above. If \((a-b)^2 > 2(a+b)\), detection is possible — this is Massoulié's theorem (STOC 2014, ch. 8): random graphs there satisfy the weak Ramanujan property, and a non-backtracking spectral algorithm detects.
The quantity \(\frac{(a-b)^2}{2(a+b)}\) plays the role of a signal-to-noise ratio; the threshold is where information survives the spectral noise of the degree.
Definition 5.4 — The non-backtracking (Hashimoto) matrix Index the oriented edges \((u \to v)\) of the graph (there are \(2m\) of them). The non-backtracking matrix \(B \in \{0,1\}^{2m \times 2m}\) is defined by $$B_{(u \to v),\, (v \to w)} = \mathbf{1}\{ w \neq u \}, \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise: a path that does not retrace its steps.}$$ \(B\) encodes non-backtracking walks: the power \(B^r\) counts walks without U-turns of length \(r\). Connection with the course: it is the companion operator of the adjacency \(A\), insensitive to the "degree noise" that pollutes \(A\) — a walk without U-turns cannot go back and forth on a hub.
Theorem 5.5 (Bordenave, Lelarge, Massoulié, FOCS 2015 / Ann. Probab. 2018) — non-backtracking spectrum of the SBM For the SBM \((a, b)/n\) and its extension to \(q\) blocks, with high probability:
  1. the spectrum of \(B\) consists of a bulk contained in the disk of radius \(\sqrt{d}\) (where \(d\) is the mean degree), plus real eigenvalues carrying information;
  2. an informative eigenvalue escapes the bulk (\(|\lambda| > \sqrt d\)) if and only if the Kesten–Stigum threshold is crossed;
  3. consequently, the non-backtracking spectral algorithm — compute the leading eigenvectors of \(B\), run k-means on them — detects the blocks exactly down to the information threshold; the same matrix proves that random graphs are asymptotically "non-regular Ramanujan".
Why \(A\) fails where \(B\) succeeds: in the \(\log\)-free regime (degrees \(O(1)\)), the highest degree is \(\Theta(\frac{\log n}{\log \log n})\) and creates parasitic eigenvalues of the same order as the signal; the operator \(B\) precisely cancels the contribution of these local back-and-forths. This is the theoretical answer to the practical problem of spectral clustering on sparse networks with hubs (Graph ML module, ch. 4; Machine Learning module, ch. 6).
Example 5.6 — MLE and the EM connection Write the log-likelihood of the SBM (latent blocks unknown): $$\ell(\sigma) = \sum_{a, b} \Big[ m_{ab}(\sigma) \log \tfrac{a \vee b}{n} + (\text{non-edges}) \log(1 - \tfrac{a\vee b}{n}) \Big],$$ and the MLE is the partition maximizing \(\ell\) — a combinatorial problem (discretization of cuts). Variational EM (Machine Learning module, ch. 6) approaches this MLE: E step — block responsibilities; M step — re-estimation of \(P\) and \(\pi\); spectral initialization (5.5) to escape saddle points. This is the pipeline of the graph-tool / sbm libraries.

6. Community detection and applications

Definition 6.1 — Modularity (Newman–Girvan, 2004) For a partition \(\mathcal{C}\) of a graph \(G\) with \(m\) edges, modularity compares the observed internal density with that expected under the configuration model (ch. 2) — the null model "same degrees, edges at random": $$Q(\mathcal{C}) = \sum_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \Big( \frac{e_C}{m} - \Big( \frac{d_C}{2m} \Big)^2 \Big),$$ where \(e_C\) counts the internal edges of block \(C\) and \(d_C\) the sum of the degrees of its vertices. Maximizing \(Q\) = finding the partition most "over-connected" relative to the null. This is the objective function of Louvain (Blondel et al., 2008) — the industrial reference algorithm, \(O(m)\) per pass, deployed in networkx, Neo4j, Gephi.
Modularity vs SBM — the great debate
  1. Resolution limit (Fortunato–Barthélemy, 2007): \(Q\) cannot see communities smaller than roughly \(\sqrt{m/2}\) edges — merging small modules increases modularity. The SBM, by contrast, has no minimal granularity.
  2. Sparse overfitting: in the \(O(1)\) degree regime, maximizing \(Q\) finds communities even in pure Erdős–Rényi — the bias of modularity on sparse graphs is well documented (Peixoto; Newman); Bayesian inference of the SBM penalizes complexity and resists.
  3. Two philosophies: modularity is descriptive (an objective heuristic, no model); the SBM is generative (a model, hence tests, thresholds, intervals — ch. 5). In practice: Louvain/Leiden for scale, SBM for statistics.
  4. The lesson of Leskovec et al. (2009): in large real graphs, "natural" communities are small (up to ~100 vertices) and drowned in a dense core without structure — it is core-periphery, not partitioning, that dominates at large scale.
6.2 — Applications: virality, alignment, privacy
  • Virality. Predicting the final size of a cascade (retweets, shares): early features (depth, width, timing of the first shares — Cheng et al., WWW 2016) and Hawkes models; the connection to the course: a massive cascade \(\Leftrightarrow\) percolated giant component (3.4), and the block structure modulates \(\sigma(S)\) (4.3-4.4) — spreading between two communities costs the \(b\) edges, spreading inside benefits from the \(a\) edges.
  • Graph alignment and privacy. Re-identifying users across two platforms (anonymized): the correlated graph alignment problem — partial recovery guarantees by Hall and Massoulié (ch. 8). GDPR reading: "anonymizing" a social graph is mathematically fragile; pseudonymization is not anonymization.
  • Echo chambers. The homophilous SBM (\(a > b\)) is the minimal model of polarization; recommendation policies (Graph ML module, ch. 6 — link prediction) that optimize engagement decrease \(b\) and deepen polarization — a quantitative bridge between algorithm and society.
  • Anomaly detection. Blocks = normal behaviors; misclassified vertices (low likelihood under a fitted SBM) are the bot/spam candidates — the individual log-likelihood of Example 5.6 serves as a score.

7. Large-scale distributed systems

Behind every feed: millions of requests per second, caches everywhere, load balancers. These systems consume exactly the same mathematics as the previous chapters — balls into bins, Poisson processes, random walks — and part of the advances come from the French school (Massoulié, ch. 8). This chapter gives its three building blocks: load balancing, caching, and resource sharing.

Theorem 7.1 — Balls into bins: the power of two choices Throw \(n\) balls independently into \(n\) bins uniformly: the maximum load is \(\frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n}\big( 1 + o(1) \big)\) with high probability (Azar, Broder, Karlin, Upfal, 1999; Mitzenmacher, 2001). If each ball chooses two bins at random and goes to the less loaded one, the maximum load drops to $$\frac{\ln \ln n}{\ln 2} + O(1) \approx \log_2 \log_2 n + O(1),$$ an exponential gain for twice the information.
(Sketch — density differential equations, the mean-field method.) Let \(u_j(t)\) be the fraction of bins of load at least \(j\) after \(t\) balls. One choice: a ball lands on a bin of load \(j-1\) with probability \(u_{j-1} - u_j\) (first time at level \(j\)); \(\frac{\mathrm{d} u_j}{\mathrm{d} t} \approx u_{j-1} - u_j\), hence \(u_j \approx t^j / j!\) — and \(u_j \approx 1\) for \(j \approx \frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n}\). Two choices: the ball rises to level \(j\) only if both sampled bins are at least \(j - 1\): \(\frac{\mathrm{d} u_j}{\mathrm{d} t} \approx u_{j-1}^2 - u_j^2\); in the stationary regime \(u_j \approx u_{j-1}^2\) — doubling at each level: \(k\) levels reach \(u_k \approx 4^{-2^{k}} \cdot\)... and \(u_j\) reaches \(1/n\) as soon as \(2^{k} \approx \log_2 n\), i.e. \(k \approx \log_2 \log_2 n\). ∎
From the model to the data center The "supermarket" model (queues + choice of the least loaded) is the modeling of load balancers: at load \(\rho < 1\), the typical waiting delay goes from \(\Theta\big(\frac{1}{1 - \rho}\big)\) (one choice) to \(\Theta\big(\log \frac{1}{1 - \rho}\big)\) (two choices) — the tail behavior changes scale (Mitzenmacher; the JOIN-IDLE-QUEUE regime handles giant systems, the "power-of-d choices" variant with \(d > 2\)). The same idea serves hash tables (cuckoo hashing); the book by Mitzenmacher and Upfal covers it all.
Definition 7.2 — Caching: LRU, LFU, and the hierarchy of contents A cache of capacity \(k\) stores \(k\) objects; requests arrive according to a process (ideally Poisson), object \(i\) with probability \(\lambda_i\) (popularity, typically power law — ch. 2); a hit if the object is in the cache, a miss otherwise (cost: fetching it from further away). Classical policies: LRU (evict the least recently used — Che's approximation: the "characteristic time" \(t_C\) gives \(\mathbb{P}(\text{hit}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda_i t_C}\)); LFU (evict the least frequently requested); random and FIFO. Structural result: against an online adversary, LRU is \(k\)-competitive — its cost is at most \(k\) times that of the offline optimum (Sleator–Tarjan); under i.i.d. popularities, Che's approximation makes it analytic. The distributed question: who caches what, when users serve one another — work of the team in ch. 8.
7.3 — Bandwidth sharing and proportional fairness (Massoulié–Roberts, 1999-2002) Flows share links; \(x_r\) = rate of flow \(r\), capacity constraints \(A x \leq c\). \(\alpha\)-fairness objectives: maximize \(\sum_r \frac{x_r^{1 - \alpha}}{1 - \alpha}\) — \(\alpha = 1\) (limit) gives proportional fairness \(\max \sum_r \log x_r\), \(\alpha \to \infty\) gives max-min. The theorem of Massoulié and Roberts (INFOCOM 1999 / IEEE-ACM ToN 2002): networks stabilized by simple distributed algorithms (AIMD window à la TCP, load evolving at the RTT timescale) converge to the proportionally fair point — a bridge between the mechanics of protocols and convex optimization: "TCP does decentralized optimization without knowing it". This framework founded a whole literature (> 900 citations) on the stability of quality-of-service networks.
Gossip and distributed learning — the modern bridge The push-pull protocols of ch. 4 also compute aggregates: the mean of a field of values by random-walk iterations (asymptotic consensus), approximate quantiles, distinct counting (HyperLogLog). The revival: asynchronous SGD on graphs — workers exchange gradients via gossip instead of a central server; convergence guarantees depend on the spectral gap of the communication graph (recent work of the team, ch. 8). For an LLGA master: federated learning is a constrained gossip protocol — the module's theory applies to it directly.

8.4 — Distributed systems: caching, balancing, bandwidth Three works connected to ch. 7:
  • Bandwidth sharing — Massoulié, Roberts (INFOCOM 1999; IEEE/ACM ToN 2002): convergence of decentralized protocols (TCP-AIMD) to proportional fairness (7.3), the foundation of network stability — ~1,000 citations.
  • Distributed caching over heterogeneous mobile networks — Ioannidis, Massoulié, Chaintreau (SIGMETRICS 2010; Queueing Systems 2012): users cache contents and exchange them when they meet; which decentralized policy maximizes throughput? The model is that of the epidemics of ch. 3 — a usable rumor to save bandwidth.
  • Bipartite graph structures for efficient balancing of heterogeneous loads — Leconte, Lelarge, Massoulié (ACM, 2012): balancing heterogeneous loads as a matching on a random bipartite graph — the bridge between power-of-two-choices (7.1) and graph structures (ch. 2).
8.5 — Today: graph alignment and distributed learning
  • Graph alignment — Hall, Massoulié, Partial Recovery in the Graph Alignment Problem (Operations Research 71(1), 2023): two correlated graphs (two platforms), can we recover the vertex correspondence? Partial recovery guarantees depending on sparsity and correlation — the theory of de-anonymization (ch. 6), with the privacy limit that goes with it.
  • Asynchronous SGD on graphs — Even, Koloskova, Massoulié, Asynchronous SGD on Graphs (AISTATS 2024): a unified framework for decentralized and federated optimization via gossip — chapter 7 of the module projected onto today's federated learning.
  • Collective exploration — Cosson, Massoulié, Collective Tree Exploration via Potential Function Method (ITCS 2024): cooperating robots explore an unknown tree — distributed algorithms viewed through online analysis.
Guiding thread (for the exam) A single pattern runs through the whole body of work: take a system (network, platform, swarm), model it by a random process on a graph, identify a spectral or branching threshold, and derive from it a decentralized algorithm with guarantees. This is exactly the backbone of chapters 1 to 7 of this module — teaching one's own research field.

# TME 2 -- discrete-time SIR: does the threshold depend on the degree law?
def sir(G, p, patient0):
    """Discrete SIR: infection p per edge per step, recovery in 1 step.
    Returns the final size of the epidemic."""
    inf, seen = {patient0}, {patient0}
    while inf:
        new = set()
        for u in inf:
            new |= {v for v in G[u] if v not in seen and rng.random() < p}
        seen |= new
        inf = new
    return len(seen)

def outbreak(G, p, trials=200):
    n = G.number_of_nodes()
    sizes = [sir(G, p, int(rng.integers(n))) for _ in range(trials)]
    return np.mean(sizes) / n            # final fraction of the population

for tau in (0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.2, 2.0):   # tau = beta/delta; recovery in 1 step,
    p = 1 - np.exp(-tau)                 # infection per edge per step
    print(f"tau={tau:4.1f}  ER {outbreak(G_er, p):.3f}   BA {outbreak(G_ba, p):.3f}")
# guided exploration: plot the final fraction as a function of tau for ER and BA,
# and check theorem 3.5: the BA curve takes off well before the ER curve
# (vanishing threshold on scale-free networks).
