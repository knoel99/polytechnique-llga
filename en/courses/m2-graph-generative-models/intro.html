Generating novel molecules, candidate materials, realistic synthetic networks: that is what graph generative models do, the generative building block of machine learning on relational data. This module extends the "generation" chapter of the M1 core course all the way to the theorems: autoregressive models (DeepGMG, GraphRNN), VAEs (GraphVAE, NeVAE), normalizing flows (GraphAF) and discrete diffusion (DiGress), with constant attention to rigorous distributional evaluation and to applications. In the LLGA master — where it is one of the modules closest to the team's research — it embodies the "graph" side of the crossroads with foundation models, up to text-to-graph.

What you will be able to do

  • Formalize graph generation as a distributional problem (a distribution over a combinatorial space);
  • Explain the DeepGMG and GraphRNN autoregressive models in detail, from the stopping decision to the enumeration order;
  • Derive the ELBO of graph VAEs and handle valence constraints (GraphVAE, NeVAE);
  • Apply the change of variables of normalizing flows to mixed discrete/continuous variables (GraphAF);
  • Unroll the discrete diffusion of DiGress: noising processes, masking orders, denoising network;
  • Evaluate a generation rigorously: MMD on graph statistics, chemical validity, FCD;
  • Condition the generation (target properties) and generate graphs from text;
  • Situate the real applications — drugs, materials, networks — and read the recent research of the LLGA team.

Where this course fits in the program

An M2 module offered in period 2, outside the recommended track, with an explicitly research flavor: taught by the LLGA / DaSciM team, it prepares for internships and PhDs on the generation of discrete structures. Prerequisites: chapter 9 of Graph Machine and Deep Learning for Generative AI (first encounter with graph generation, revisited in ch. 0); the GNN architectures of Advanced Graph Neural Networks, which serve as denoisers and encoders everywhere here; and the optimal transport of Computational Optimal Transport for ML and Fairness, whose Gromov-Wasserstein distance allows graphs to be compared and underlies evaluation metrics.

Downstream: the module leads to the biological and industrial applications of generation (ch. 8) and to the open problems (ch. 10) — as many gateways to the research-oriented internship and, beyond, to the PhD; it also feeds the "LLMs × graphs" crossroads developed in Advanced Topics in Large Language Models (ch. 7).

Course outline

  • Recap: generating graphs in M1 — what was already covered in M1: introductive versions of graph VAEs and GANs, and the vocabulary of the problem.
  • The problem, formalized — graph space, sampling, combinatorial complexity: why generating graphs is hard.
  • Autoregressive models: DeepGMG and GraphRNN in depth — building the graph sequentially, decision by decision.
  • VAEs: GraphVAE, NeVAE and valence constraints — the graph encoder-decoder and the art of obtaining valid molecules.
  • Normalizing flows: change of variables and GraphAF — one-step generation by dense invertible flows, on mixed variables.
  • Discrete diffusion: DiGress in detail — noising then denoising edges and attributes: the state of the art explained step by step.
  • Evaluating rigorously: MMD, validity, FCD — why visual quality is not enough, and which distributional metrics to use.
  • Conditional generation and text-to-graph — targeting a property, and generating from a natural-language description.
  • Applications: drugs, materials, synthetic networks — three industrial and scientific territories examined seriously.
  • Research of the LLGA team — the lab's recent contributions, presented by their authors.
  • Open problems — what the community does not yet know how to do, and the internship topics hiding there.
  • Exercises / Labs — implement GraphRNN, train a small molecule VAE, evaluate by MMD.
  • References — the reference papers of each family, to be read in the original.

How to study this module

  • Read the indicated papers after the corresponding chapter: they are written for specialists, the chapter gives you the keys to read them.
  • Implement GraphRNN in a toy version (graphs with fewer than 20 nodes) before moving to frameworks: the sequential logic is easier to see there.
  • Always check the chemical validity of the molecules generated in the labs: it is the permanent reminder that combinatorics is not an image.
  • For each model family, fill in a small "sampling speed / quality / failure mode" table: it is the reading grid of the field.
  • Take notes on ch. 9 (the team's research): it is the best preparation for research internship interviews.
The LLGA perspective "Large Language Models, Graphs and Applications": this module is the heart of the graph side of that motto. Graph generation is the place where molecules, materials and networks meet foundation models — the text-to-graph of ch. 7 being the exact junction point between LLMs and discrete structures. For students aiming at research (the master's research internship, a PhD) as for those aiming at the life-science and materials industries, this is where the LLGA master is furthest ahead of neighboring programs.
