10. Open problems

The grid of ch. 1 (exchangeability, likelihood, constraints) closes here: four work sites where it still has no satisfactory answer — and a fifth, theoretical one. For each: the 2026 state, the promising direction, and the master's modules that contribute to it.

Problem 10.1 — Evaluation: a metric without a theory of the measure Chapter 6 showed the biases; the heart of the problem is that there is no canonical distance on \(\mathcal{G}\): the edit distance is NP-hard to compute, Gromov-Wasserstein costs \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) per pair (cf. Optimal Transport module), and MMD depends on an arbitrary choice of features and kernel — a generator can satisfy degrees + clustering + orbits and fail on every untested statistic. The FCD itself depends on a frozen bioactivity predictor (ChemNet, 2015): the metric ages with it. Promising directions: (i) task-based evaluation (do the generated graphs improve a downstream predictor, as augmentation data?); (ii) learned "critics" (a generated/real classifier trained to discriminate — if it succeeds, the distribution is betrayed); (iii) cheap surrogates of GW. Methodological reference: the lesson of healthy benchmarking (Dwivedi et al., JMLR 2023 — Advanced GNN module ch. 6).
Problem 10.2 — Global hard constraints The valence mask (ch. 3) is local: each decision only looks at the remaining valences — hence its tractability. The useful constraints are often global: connectivity, cycle sizes, total number of edges, quotas per type, stability. The automaton that would enforce them at decoding is exponential (the technical point of the Control module ch. 3, on text, carries over as is); the auxiliary losses of DiGress (ch. 5) penalize them without guaranteeing them; post-processing repairs them at the price of the bias of ch. 3. Direction: constrained generation with guarantee — projection at each diffusion step onto the admissible set (the discrete posterior of thm. 5.3 is no longer in closed form as soon as it is constrained: the whole challenge), solvers in the loop, learned rejection methods. This is the shared work site with the Control in Generative AI module (ch. 8).
Problem 10.3 — Scaling up All the complexities of the module are at best \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) per object: square adjacency, \(\Theta(n^2)\) sequential steps for GraphRNN, \(T\) GNN passes for DiGress — and \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) tokens to describe a graph to an LLM (ch. 7). Real networks reach 108-109 nodes. Leads: generation by fragments (junction trees, ch. 2 — the fragment as the decision unit divides the number of steps); latent generation (NGG, ch. 9: one denoises a fixed-size representation); training on subgraphs (mini-batch — at the price of a sampling bias); sparsification. Underlying open question: sample complexity — how many graphs are needed to learn a law at large \(n\)? (the generational counterpart of the generalization problem of the Statistical Learning Theory module).
Problem 10.4 — The integration of LLMs Chapter 7 showed four routes; their blind spots: (i) tokenization — no canonical serialization of graphs is satisfactory (SMILES is not canonical, the edge list is \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) tokens, cf. GOAT ch. 9 on the cost of orders); (ii) guarantee — the LLM hallucinates invalid adjacencies; the remedy is grammatical (SELFIES) or agentic (the tool verifies); (iii) memorization — an LLM can spit out training graphs (the privacy problem of ch. 8, displaced); (iv) evaluation — text-to-graph does not yet have its GuacaMol. Directions: graph foundation models (multi-family pre-training, adaptation by prompt — cf. Boosting and Foundation Models M2 module), LLM + GNN hybrids (propose/verify), and standardized evaluation (the "code" protocol of ch. 7 as a provisional standard).
Problem 10.5 — Theory: beyond exchangeability and likelihood Two frontiers. (i) Limits: the Aldous-Hoover theorem (ch. 1) describes exchangeable laws — now real networks grow (preferential attachment), evolve (temporal), and their limits require non-exchangeable extensions (graphexes): what is the theory of neural generators on these objects? (ii) Likelihood against quality: the ELBO of VAEs and diffusions (ch. 3, 5) is an uncontrolled bound — the gap between ELBO and log-likelihood can be enormous without the samples suffering, or the opposite; and for autoregressive models (ch. 2), the exact likelihood depends on an arbitrary order — what invariant quantity do they really optimize? These two questions condition any theory of learning for graph generators — the natural extension of the Statistical Learning Theory and Advanced Deep Learning modules.

The grid of ch. 1, closed Reread the table of ch. 1 (exchangeability / likelihood / constraints): each filled cell calls for the next empty one — VAEs trade exact likelihood for architectural exchangeability; flows buy likelihood at the price of sequential cost; diffusion buys exchangeability and parallelism at the price of hard constraints. Problem 10.2 is precisely the empty cell of the last column. Typical exam question: "place NGG, DiGress and GraphAF in the grid, and say which cell each leaves open" — if you can answer that, the module is acquired.
