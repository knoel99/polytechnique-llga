9. Research of the LLGA team

The LLGA master is driven by the DaSciM laboratory (Data Science and Mining, LIX, CNRS — École Polytechnique: M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer, G. Nikolentzos and their PhD students), the host laboratory of the internships and PhDs. This chapter presents four of its works, chosen because they touch directly on the objects of this module: the encoder of the generators (PathNN), the order question (GOAT), a latent-diffusion generator conditioned by features (NGG) and the LLM × graphs frontier (pseudo-code prompting). The other axes of the laboratory are covered by the Graph ML (ch. 10) and Advanced GNN (ch. 8) modules; here, each paper reads as the extension of a chapter.

Work 9.1 — PathNN: aggregating paths, not neighbors (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Standard message passing (ch. 0) aggregates immediate neighbors; PathNN aggregates paths starting from the node: $$h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\Big(\big\{\, \mathrm{LSTM}\big(h_v^{(0)}, h_{u_1}^{(0)}, \dots, h_{u_\ell}^{(0)}\big)\ :\ (v, u_1, \dots, u_\ell) \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} \,\big\}\Big),$$ where \(\mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)}\) is a set of paths of length \(\ell\) — three variants: shortest paths (to all destinations), random walks, sampled simple paths. Two reasons to be interested here: (i) expressivity: paths of length \(\ell\) capture multi-hop dependencies that \(\ell\) layers of MPNN irretrievably mix — strictly above 1-WL on chosen pairs (cf. Advanced GNN module ch. 1-2); since the number of simple paths grows exponentially, sampling \(\mathcal{P}_v\) bounds the cost: an expressivity/cost trade-off tuned in the encoder; (ii) role in this module: PathNN is an encoder — the natural candidate for the \(q_\phi(z \mid G)\) of the VAEs (ch. 3) and the backbone of the NGG below; replacing the MPNN with a more expressive encoder is the simplest improvement lever for latent-based generators.
Work 9.2 — GOAT: rehabilitating the order of neighbors (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) The whole module has run into order: autoregressive models impose a canonical order on it (BFS, ch. 2), the one-shot decoder must match its nodes (Hungarian matching, ch. 3). GOAT attacks the root: the usual aggregators (sum, mean, max, attention) are invariant — they throw away all order information between neighbors, while this order carries signal (synergies, redundancies between neighbors). The GOAT layer is a permutation-sensitive attention: each node learns a local order of its neighborhood (position scores) and encodes the neighbors in that learned order — global invariance is preserved (the order is a function of the graph, not of a numbering), local sensitivity is restored. Results: prediction of centrality metrics (betweenness...) where invariant GNNs struggle. The "generation" reading: an encoding that distinguishes neighborhoods that 1-WL confounds gives the latent more to say — the flip side of the matching problem of ch. 3: better encoding the order means better recovering it at decoding.
Work 9.3 — NGG: latent diffusion conditioned by graph features (Evdaimon, Nikolentzos, Xypolopoulos, Kammoun, …, Vazirgiannis, 2024) The Neural Graph Generator applies the latent diffusion recipe to graphs (recap: Multimodal GenAI module ch. 3-4):
  1. Encoder: a GNN compresses \(G \mapsto h_G\) (latent);
  2. Conditioned latent diffusion: a denoiser learns to denoise \(h_G\) given a vector of target features \(c\) — spectrum of the Laplacian (the first eigenvalues), degree statistics, clustering coefficient... descriptors that are global, interpretable, measurable;
  3. Decoder: from the denoised latent to the adjacency. Sampling: choose \(c\) — the statistical "specification sheet" — then generate.
Position with respect to the chapters: DiGress (ch. 5) denoises the complete object (types, edges); NGG denoises a representation — the exchangeability is that of the encoder, the per-step cost much lower, and the conditioning is "macro" (global features) where the molecular CFG of ch. 7 is "micro" (a property of one molecule). The evaluation is that of ch. 6: MMD on degrees/clustering/orbits between generated graphs and target families (SBM, citation networks...). It is also the "synthetic networks" building block of ch. 8: specify a spectrum and a degree distribution, obtain whole graphs.
Work 9.4 — Pseudo-code prompting: reasoning about graphs with programs (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024) A series of graph problems (connectivity, shortest path, existence of cycles, maximum flow, spanning tree...) is submitted to LLMs: the same instruction formulated in pseudo-code (algorithmic specification: loops, conditions, invariants) systematically beats its natural-language formulation — on both axes: understanding (executing the described algorithm) and producing (writing the algorithm for the posed problem). The reading for this module: (i) it is the experimental confirmation of route 2 of ch. 7 ("the LLM writes the generator"): the program is the most reliable graph representation for an LLM — unambiguous, verifiable, executable; (ii) the natural bridge with tool-using agents (ch. 7, ChemCrow): describe the task in pseudo-code then have it verified by a solver (NetworkX) — the proposal/verification loop of the Control module (ch. 10), instantiated on graphs.
