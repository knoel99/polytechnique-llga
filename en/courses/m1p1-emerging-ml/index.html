Today's models are no longer trained on a single machine, but on clusters, distributed data, and participants who do not all trust one another. This course covers the "systems" viewpoint of modern machine learning: distributed and federated learning, Byzantine robustness, differential privacy, scaling models and compute. These are precisely the constraints that govern LLM training in industry and in the lab: who sees the data, who does the computation, what happens when a participant is faulty or malicious.

What you will be able to do

  • Analyze distributed stochastic gradient descent: convergence speed, the effect of mini-batch size, step size, and number of workers.
  • Distinguish distributed learning architectures (centralized, local, federated) and their communication/computation trade-offs.
  • Explain the federated learning model (FedAvg) and its assumptions, in particular data heterogeneity across clients.
  • Identify Byzantine failures in a learning cluster and apply the robust aggregation rules that neutralize them.
  • Define differential privacy (ε-differential privacy), apply it to SGD, and interpret the utility/privacy trade-off.
  • Reason about collaboration challenges: per-client personalization, fairness across participants, incentives.
  • Estimate the cost of a training run (scaling models and compute) and read the scaling laws that structure the field.

0. Review: SGD and its analysis

L3/M1 review — the stochastic foundation
  • Gradient descent: \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla f(\theta_t)\); convex and \(L\)-smooth ⇒ \(\mathcal{O}(1/t)\) decrease.
  • SGD: replace \(\nabla f\) with a batch gradient \(g_t = \frac1m \sum_{i \in B_t} \nabla \ell(\theta_t; x_i)\), unbiased, \(\mathrm{Var}(g_t) = \mathcal{O}(1/m)\) (CLT, see the Refresher Statistics module).
  • Strong convexity: \(f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \leq \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y) - \tfrac{\mu}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\|x - y\|^2\) ⇒ linear convergence in the deterministic case, \(\mathcal{O}(1/t)\) in the stochastic case.
  • Descent inequality: for \(f\) \(L\)-smooth, \(f(\theta_{t+1}) \leq f(\theta_t) - \eta(1 - \tfrac{\eta L}{2})\|\nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2\) — the basic lemma for this entire chapter.
Working assumptions (A1)-(A3) (A1) \(f\) convex, \(L\)-smooth, with minimizer \(\theta^\star\) (write \(D = \|\theta_0 - \theta^\star\|\), \(f^\star = f(\theta^\star)\)); (A2) unbiased stochastic gradient \(\mathbb{E}[g_t \mid \theta_t] = \nabla f(\theta_t)\); (A3) bounded variance \(\mathbb{E}\|g_t - \nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2 \leq \sigma^2\). We write \(g_t = \nabla f(\theta_t) + \xi_t\) with \(\mathbb{E}\xi_t = 0\).
Theorem 0.1 — Constant-step SGD: full proof Under (A1)-(A3), with step size \(\eta \leq \frac{1}{2L}\): $$\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}\big[f(\theta_t) - f^\star\big] \;\leq\; \frac{D^2}{2\eta T} \;+\; \frac{\eta \sigma^2}{2} \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{L D^2}{T}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt T}\Big),$$ the last term being obtained for \(\eta = \min\big(\tfrac{1}{2L}, \tfrac{D}{\sigma\sqrt T}\big)\).
Step 1 (recursion on the distances). With \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta g_t\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\xi_t \mid \theta_t] = 0\) (the cross term vanishes): $$\mathbb{E}\|\theta_{t+1} - \theta^\star\|^2 = \|\theta_t - \theta^\star\|^2 - 2\eta \big\langle \nabla f(\theta_t),\, \theta_t - \theta^\star \big\rangle + \eta^2\, \mathbb{E}\|g_t\|^2.$$ Step 2 (the three ingredients). (i) convexity: \(\langle \nabla f(\theta_t), \theta_t - \theta^\star\rangle \geq f(\theta_t) - f^\star\); (ii) bias-variance decomposition + smoothness: \(\mathbb{E}\|g_t\|^2 = \|\nabla f(\theta_t)\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\xi_t\|^2 \leq 2L\big(f(\theta_t) - f^\star\big) + \sigma^2\) (since \(\|\nabla f(\theta)\|^2 \leq 2L(f(\theta) - f^\star)\) for \(f\) convex and \(L\)-smooth); combining: $$\mathbb{E}\|\theta_{t+1} - \theta^\star\|^2 \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}\|\theta_t - \theta^\star\|^2 \;-\; 2\eta(1 - \eta L)\,\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \;+\; \eta^2 \sigma^2.$$ Step 3 (telescoping). Sum over \(t = 0, \dots, T-1\): the sum of the distances telescopes to \(D^2\), so with \(\eta \leq \frac{1}{2L}\) (hence \(1 - \eta L \geq \tfrac12\)): $$\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \;\leq\; \frac{D^2}{2\eta T} + \frac{\eta \sigma^2}{2}. \qquad \blacksquare$$
Reading. Two terms: optimization (\(D^2/\eta T\), vanishes with \(T\)) and statistics (\(\eta\sigma^2\), the "floor" of the constant step size); the choice of \(\eta\) arbitrates. In practice, networks are trained with constant step sizes (or step sizes decreasing in stages); with a decreasing step size \(\eta_t \propto 1/t\), the average of the iterates reaches \(\mathcal{O}(\log T / T)\), and \(\mathcal{O}(\sigma^2 / \mu T)\) if \(f\) is \(\mu\)-strongly convex.
Theorem 0.2 — Lower bounds (statements) (i) Stochastic oracle (Nemirovski-Yudin): for any algorithm accessing \(f\) only through noisy gradients of variance \(\sigma^2\), there exists a convex, \(L\)-smooth function such that \(\mathbb{E}[f(\hat\theta_T) - f^\star] \geq c\, \sigma D/\sqrt T\) — the rate of Theorem 0.1 is therefore optimal; the noise is not a flaw of the algorithm but a law of the problem. (ii) Strongly convex: the bound becomes \(\Omega(\sigma^2/\mu T)\): the statistical floor cannot be beaten, even with all the available structure. Consequence for this module: parallelizing (ch. 2) can do no better than reducing \(\sigma\) — which is exactly what the aggregation of \(K\) workers does.
Proposition 0.3 — Variance reduction: SAGA and SVRG (sketch) The \(\sigma/\sqrt T\) floor comes from the constant variance of the gradient; on a finite sum \(f = \frac1n \sum_i f_i\), it can be made to decrease:
  • SVRG (Johnson & Zhang): periodically compute the exact gradient \(\nabla f(\tilde\theta)\) at a reference point \(\tilde\theta\), then use the control variate \(v_t = \nabla f_{i_t}(\theta_t) - \nabla f_{i_t}(\tilde\theta) + \nabla f(\tilde\theta)\): unbiased, and with variance proportional to \(\|\theta_t - \tilde\theta\|^2\) — which decreases over the course of the optimization; linear rate \(\mathcal{O}((n + \kappa)\log 1/\varepsilon)\) (versus \(\mathcal{O}(n\kappa \log 1/\varepsilon)\) for GD);
  • SAGA: keep in a table the latest \(\nabla f_i\) seen for each \(i\) and correct by their average — same effect, without periodic recomputation.
These control variate ideas will reappear in federated learning (SCAFFOLD, ch. 3) and in the Byzantine setting (Bulyan, ch. 8): this is THE algorithmic motif of the module.
Proposition 0.4 — Momentum, Nesterov, sampling Momentum (heavy ball): \(v_{t+1} = \beta v_t + g_t\), \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta v_{t+1}\) — damps oscillations along steep directions (poorly conditioned gradient covariance matrix); typical choice \(\beta = 0.9\). Nesterov: evaluate the gradient at the anticipated point \(\theta_t - \gamma v_t\); in the deterministic case, the rate goes from \(\mathcal{O}(LD^2/T)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(LD^2/T^2)\) — optimal for a first-order method. Weighted sampling: draw \(i\) according to a distribution \(q\) and correct with \(g = \nabla f_i/q(i)\); the variance is minimized when \(q_i \propto \|\nabla f_i\|\) (importance sampling, Zhao & Zhang, 2014); the stratified variant partitions the data into homogeneous strata and allocates the sampling budget where the internal variance is large — stratification reduces \(\sigma^2\) even before parallelizing.

2. Distributed learning

Definition 2.1 — Distributed SGD (synchronous workers) \(K\) workers hold data shards. At each step: each worker \(k\) computes \(g^{(k)}\) on its mini-batch, the server aggregates and broadcasts: $$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^K g_t^{(k)}.$$
Theorem 2.2 — Linear speedup (proof via the variance) If the \(g_t^{(k)}\) are independent, unbiased, with variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\bar g_t = \frac1K \sum_k g_t^{(k)}\) is unbiased with $$\mathbb{E}\|\bar g_t\|^2 = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{K},$$ and Theorem 0.1 applies with \(\sigma \to \sigma/\sqrt K\): $$\frac1T \sum_t \mathbb{E}[f(\theta_t) - f^\star] \leq \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{LD^2}{T} + \frac{\sigma D}{\sqrt{KT}}\Big).$$
Independence + unbiasedness: \(\mathbb{E}\|\bar g\|^2 = \|\mathbb{E}\bar g\|^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\bar g) = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{1}{K^2}\sum_k \mathrm{Var}(g^{(k)}) = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{K}\). To reach a target accuracy \(\varepsilon = \sigma D/\sqrt{T}\), one needs \(T(\varepsilon) \propto \frac{\sigma^2 D^2}{K\,\varepsilon^2}\) distributed iterations — versus \(\frac{\sigma^2 D^2}{\varepsilon^2}\) sequentially: \(K\) times fewer iterations, each \(K\) times more expensive in computation (parallelized): this is the ideal linear speedup, valid if and only if communication is negligible — which it never is (rest of this chapter). ∎
The communication wall Sending a gradient of \(10^9\) parameters (4 GB in fp32) costs more than computing it. Ring all-reduce is bandwidth-optimal: each worker only exchanges \(\frac{2(K-1)}{K}\) model sizes per step. The rest of this chapter aims to reduce the bytes exchanged.
Theorem 2.3 — Quantization: QSGD (Alistarh et al., 2017) Quantize each coordinate \(v_i\) by random rounding to the grid \(\{0, \pm s^{-1}\|v\|_2, \dots, \pm\|v\|_2\}\): the quantizer \(Q_s\) is unbiased and $$\mathbb{E}\|Q_s(v)\|^2 \;\leq\; \Big(1 + \min\Big\{\frac{d}{s^2}, \frac{\sqrt d}{s}\Big\}\Big) \|v\|^2.$$ Reading: with \(s = \sqrt d\) levels, the variance barely doubles, and the (entropy) coding of the quantized tensor costs only \(\sim d/2\) bits per coordinate instead of 32 — up to an order of magnitude of bandwidth saved at equal training quality. Extreme case: signSGD (1 bit per coordinate: the sign); combined with majority vote it converges under adapted assumptions (Bernstein et al., 2019). The general trade-off: bits ↔ variance (Theorem 2.2: more variance = less speedup).
Proposition 2.4 — Sparsification and error feedback Sending only the \(k\) largest coordinates (top-k) drastically reduces communication — but the truncated gradient is biased, and top-k alone can make training diverge. Error feedback: keep the residual: $$e_{t+1} = \big(g_t + e_t\big) - \mathrm{Top}_k\big(g_t + e_t\big), \qquad \text{one sends } \mathrm{Top}_k(g_t + e_t),$$ each "forgotten" coordinate is eventually sent once its accumulated residual enters the top-k: convergence is restored at the rate of uncompressed SGD (Seide et al. 2014 for 1-bit; analyses by Stich et al. 2018). In practice: top-k at 1% + error feedback is nearly free in quality.
Proposition 2.5 — Asynchrony and Hogwild! Synchronizing \(K\) workers means waiting for the slowest one (the "stragglers"). In asynchronous mode, each worker pushes its gradient computed on a model potentially stale by \(\tau\) steps (staleness): the bound of Theorem 0.1 degrades by a factor \((1 + \tau)\). Hogwild! (Recht et al., 2011): lock-free SGD on shared memory, valid for sparse problems (concurrent updates rarely collide); Lian et al. (2015): asynchronous SGD retains the \(1/\sqrt T\) and the linear speedup up to constant staleness — asynchrony pays off when clock variance dominates.
Theorem 2.6 — Decentralized: gossip and the graph spectrum Without a server: the workers form a communication graph \(\mathcal{G}\) (adjacency matrix \(A\), Laplacian \(\mathcal{L}\)) and exchange with their neighbors. Gossip aggregation (matrix \(W\) doubly stochastic, e.g. \(W = I - \gamma \mathcal{L}\)): $$\theta^{(k)} \leftarrow \sum_j W_{kj}\, \theta^{(j)}$$ makes all nodes converge to the average at geometric speed $$\big\|\theta_t - \bar\theta \,\mathbf{1}\big\| \;\leq\; \rho(W)^t\, \|\theta_0 - \bar\theta\,\mathbf{1}\|, \qquad \rho(W) = \max_{i \neq 1} |\lambda_i(W)| = 1 - \lambda_2,$$ where \(\lambda_2\) is the spectral gap (algebraic connectivity of the graph — see the Graph ML module, spectrum chapter; chain = slow, complete graph = fast, expander = nearly optimal). Decentralized SGD (D-PSGD, Lian et al., ICML 2017) interleaves local SGD steps and gossip: it retains the \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt T)\) rate of the centralized version and its linear speedup, with local exchanges instead of the global all-reduce — it would beat the centralized version in wall-clock time as soon as the graph is well connected and bandwidth is limited.

3. Federated learning

Algorithm 3.1 — FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) Clients \(\{1..K\}\) with local data \(\mathcal{D}_k\) that is non-iid (each has its own data, never shared). At each round:
  1. the server broadcasts \(\theta_r\) to a subset of clients;
  2. each client \(k\) performs \(E\) epochs of local SGD: \(\theta_r^{(k)} \approx \arg\min F_k\);
  3. weighted aggregation: \(\theta_{r+1} = \sum_k \tfrac{n_k}{n}\, \theta_r^{(k)}\).
Theorem 3.2 — Client drift: proving it on a quadratic example Heterogeneity makes clients drift: local SGD solves their own problems before aggregation. Exact computation on \(F_k(\theta) = \tfrac{a_k}{2}(\theta - z_k)^2\) (local SGD = exact gradient, step size \(\eta\)):
One local step: \(\theta \leftarrow (1 - \eta a_k)\theta + \eta a_k z_k\), so after \(E\) steps starting from \(\theta_r\) (geometric sum): $$\theta_r^{(k)} = z_k + c_k\,(\theta_r - z_k), \qquad c_k = (1 - \eta a_k)^E.$$ The aggregation \(\theta_{r+1} = \frac1K\sum_k \theta_r^{(k)}\) is an affine iteration whose fixed point satisfies \(\theta_\infty = \frac1K\sum_k (1 - c_k)\, z_k \,\Big/\, \frac1K \sum_k (1 - c_k)\) — a weighted average by the effective curvatures \(1 - c_k\). Now the global optimum of \(F = \frac1K\sum F_k\) is \(\theta^\star = \sum a_k z_k / \sum a_k\). The two coincide only if the \(a_k\) are equal: FedAvg converges to a point that is NOT the global optimum as soon as the curvatures differ. Numbers. \(K=2\), \(a_1 = 1, z_1 = 0\), \(a_2 = 10, z_2 = 1\), \(\eta = 0.05\), \(E = 10\): \(c_1 = 0.95^{10} \approx 0.60\), \(c_2 = 0.5^{10} \approx 0.001\); \(\theta^\star = 10/11 \approx 0.909\), but \(\theta_\infty = \frac{1 \cdot 1}{0.40 + 1} \approx 0.713\): a structural bias of ~20% that does not vanish as the number of rounds grows. ∎
Theorem 3.3 — Convergence of FedAvg (convex setting, statement) Under (A1)-(A3) per client and bounded heterogeneity, the Theorem-0.1-style analysis applied to FedAvg (Li et al., 2020) yields, with \(E\) local steps per round of \(K\) clients: $$\mathbb{E}\big[F(\theta_R)\big] - F^\star \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{D^2}{\eta E R}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu^2 K E R}\Big) + \mathcal{O}\big(\Gamma\big), \qquad \Gamma = F^\star - \sum_k \frac{n_k}{n} F_k^\star \ \text{(or equivalent)},$$ where the heterogeneity term \(\Gamma > 0\) measures the gap between the global optimum and the local optima: it is the analytic manifestation of Theorem 3.2. The first two terms decrease with the rounds (communication gain: \(E\) steps for 1 exchange); the third is a bias that only a correction (SCAFFOLD) or a penalty (FedProx) can mitigate.
Proposition 3.4 — FedProx: the role of the proximal term FedProx (Li et al., 2020): each client minimizes a regularized objective $$\theta^{(k)} \approx \arg\min_\theta\ F_k(\theta) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|\theta - \theta_r\|^2,$$ then aggregates. The proximal penalty (i) curbs the drift of Theorem 3.2 — the client stays in a neighborhood of \(\theta_r\) of radius \(\propto 1/\mu\); (ii) makes \(E\) arbitrary (tolerance to slow or partial clients — work with what has been received); (iii) can be read as a proximal step of the global problem: in the limit \(\mu \to \infty\), FedAvg with 1 step. Trade-off: too large a \(\mu\) crushes local work (back to communication-slow FedSGD), too small a \(\mu\) lets clients drift.
Algorithm 3.5 — SCAFFOLD: variance correction via control variates (Karimireddy et al., ICML 2020.) The server and the clients maintain control variates \(c\) and \(c_k\) (initially zero). Local step: $$\theta_{j+1}^{(k)} = \theta_j^{(k)} - \eta\big(g_k(\theta_j^{(k)}) - c_k + c\big),$$ update of the client control at the end of the round: $$c_k^{+} = c_k - c + \frac{\theta_r - \theta_E^{(k)}}{\eta E}, \qquad c^{+} = \frac1K \sum_k c_k^{+}.$$ Reading. The term \(-c_k + c\) replaces the local gradient with a "recentered" version: \(c_k - c\) estimates the mean difference between the local gradient and the global gradient — exactly the drift of Theorem 3.2. On the quadratic example above, the correction is exact: the drift disappears and the fixed point becomes \(\theta^\star\) again (exercise 4). Result: the number of rounds to reach \(\varepsilon\) goes from \(\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^2)\) (FedAvg, with large \(E\)) to \(\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)\) — the same motif as SVRG (Proposition 0.3): a control variate buys a factor of \(\varepsilon\).
Proposition 3.6 — Personalization: Ditto and local fine-tuning When clients want different models (hospitals with distinct practices), heterogeneity becomes a resource: learn a good global model that serves as an initialization for everyone, then personalize. Ditto (Li et al., 2021) solves, per client, $$\min_{\theta_k}\ F_k(\theta_k) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta_k - \theta^\star_{\mathrm{glob}}\|^2$$ jointly with the global problem: \(\lambda\) arbitrates specialization/collaboration — and the solution improves fairness across clients (the worst client is not sacrificed for the benefit of the average client). Pragmatic version: local fine-tuning of the federated model (standard in production, e.g. keyboards); probabilistic version: mixtures of posteriors (see ch. 8, FedEM).

def fedavg(clients, rounds=50, E=5, eta=0.1):
    """clients: list of local functions (theta -> theta after E SGD steps)."""
    theta = np.zeros(d)
    for r in range(rounds):
        local = [c(theta, E, eta) for c in clients]   # local phase
        theta = np.mean(local, axis=0)                # aggregation
    return theta

# SCAFFOLD: add the control variates
def scaffold(clients, rounds=50, E=5, eta=0.1):
    theta = np.zeros(d); c = np.zeros(d)
    ck = [np.zeros(d) for _ in clients]
    for r in range(rounds):
        new_ck, loc = [], []
        for k, client in enumerate(clients):
            theta_k, drift = client(theta, E, eta, correction=c - ck[k])
            loc.append(theta_k)
            new_ck.append(ck[k] - c + (theta - theta_k) / (eta * E))
        theta = np.mean(loc, axis=0)
        ck, c = new_ck, np.mean(ck, axis=0)
    return theta

Definition 4.1 — Byzantine client and threat model A client is Byzantine if it returns an arbitrary gradient (crash or attack: data poisoning, model poisoning, backdoor). One distinguishes:

The battle is fought on the aggregation rule (GAR): replace the mean with a robust operator — the rest of the pipeline (ch. 2-3) is unchanged.
Attacks, by hand
  • Label flipping: the client trains on flipped labels — its gradient points opposite to the consensus;
  • Large-scale model poisoning: in dimension 1, honest \(\mu = 0.5\), \(K = 10\), \(f = 1\) attacker sending \(C = \tfrac{K-f}{f}\mu + 1 = 5.5\): \(\mathrm{mean} = \frac{9 \times 0.5 - 5.5}{10} = -0.1\) — the sign is reversed by a single client: the mean is uncontrolled as soon as \(f \geq 1\) (norm of the bias arbitrarily large);
  • Backdoor: data with a trigger ("add word X to every sentence containing the secret sequence"); the poisoned model keeps normal accuracy on clean data and fires on the trigger — invisible in the global metric, dangerous in deployment.
Theorem 4.2 — Median and trimmed mean: guarantees in dimension 1 (proved) Let \(K\) values of which \(K - f\) honest ( \(f < K/2\) ) lying in an interval \(I = [m, M]\) with honest mean \(\bar g\). Then: (i) the coordinate-wise median lies in \(I\), hence \(|\mathrm{med} - \bar g| \leq M - m\); (ii) the \(\beta\)-trimmed mean (remove the \(\beta K\) largest and smallest values, \(\beta K \geq f\)) is a mean of honest values of \(I\), hence lies in \(I\).
(i) There are more than \(K/2\) honest values: the order statistic of rank \(\lceil K/2 \rceil\) is honest or bracketed by two honest values, hence in \([m, M]\); since \(\bar g \in [m, M]\), the gap is at most the diameter \(M - m\). (ii) With \(\beta K \geq f\), the \(f\) Byzantine values are numerous enough to be all removed or some remain — in both cases, what remains after trimming contains at least \(K - 2\beta K\) values, all honest if the removed extremes cover the Byzantine ones; the mean of a subset of \([m, M]\) stays in \([m, M]\). ∎
Scope and limits. The guarantee is bounded where the mean is arbitrary — but it holds coordinate by coordinate: an attacker can stay "within the interval" on each coordinate while correlating its deviations to shift the global direction (dimension-by-dimension attack — the flaw exploited by El Mhamdi et al., see ch. 8); the price is also statistical: the median and trimming use information less well than the mean (efficiency \(\sim 2/\pi\) in the Gaussian case for the median).
Algorithm 4.3 — Krum (Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Stainer, NeurIPS 2017), in detail Parameter: an upper bound \(f\) on the number of Byzantine clients, with \(K \geq 2f + 3\).
  1. compute all the distances \(D(i, j) = \|g_i - g_j\|^2\);
  2. for each \(i\), score \(s_i\) = sum of the \(m = K - f - 2\) smallest distances \(D(i, j)\) (its \(m\) nearest neighbors);
  3. return \(g_{i^\star}\), \(i^\star = \arg\min_i s_i\) — the point most "surrounded" by close points.
Intuition for the guarantee. The \(K - f\) honest gradients are concentrated around the true gradient \(\nabla F\) (i.i.d., variance \(\sigma^2\) per coordinate): each honest point has at least \(K - f - 1 \geq m + 1\) close honest neighbors, hence a score \(\leq\) \((m)\) times a quantile of the inter-honest distances, of the same order as \(\sigma^2 d\). A Byzantine point, on the other hand, has at most \(f\) close accomplices: its score includes distances to honest points, large as soon as it moves away from the cloud — unless it hides inside the honest cloud, in which case it is harmless. The theorem of the paper formalizes this: under the assumption of (2f+2, f+1)-resilience (any mean of \(f+1\) honest points is close to \(\nabla F\), and \(2f + 2\) honest points cannot all be simultaneously "close" to the same outlying point), the selected point is at distance \(\mathcal{O}(\sigma\sqrt d)\) from the true gradient — the order of magnitude of the stochastic noise itself: robustness has not destroyed convergence. ∎
Cost and limits. \(O(K^2 d)\) on the server (all pairwise distances); requires i.i.d. gradients — in heterogeneous federated learning (Th. 3.2), the honest points form several clusters and Krum may pick a cluster; attacks varying over time: alternating between two attack directions beats any one-shot selection — direct motivations for Bulyan (ch. 8).
Proposition 4.4 — Geometric median and Weiszfeld iterations The geometric median \(\hat g = \arg\min_x \sum_k \|x - g_k\|_2\) is the center minimizing the sum of distances (robust: breakdown point 1/2). Weiszfeld's algorithm (fixed point of weights inverse to the distances): $$x^{(j+1)} \;=\; \frac{\sum_k g_k \,/\, \|x^{(j)} - g_k\|}{\sum_k 1 \,/\, \|x^{(j)} - g_k\|}.$$ Each iteration is a weighted barycenter where far-away points carry little weight; convergence is guaranteed (strict convexity as soon as the \(g_k\) are not aligned). Guarantee (RGM, Chen et al., 2017): with \(f < K/2\) and i.i.d. honest gradients of variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\|\hat g - \nabla F\| = \mathcal{O}\big(\sigma\sqrt{d/K} + \tfrac{f}{K}\big)\) up to constant factors — the price of robustness is a factor \(\sqrt K\) on the stochastic part, plus a term in the fraction of corrupted clients.
The Byzantine curse of dimensionality The typical distance between two honest gradients grows like \(\sigma\sqrt d\): in high dimension, the honest "cloud" is immense, and telling a malicious deviation from noise becomes impossible without an additional assumption. Impossibility results: no rule can be simultaneously unbiased (efficient at \(1/\sqrt K\)) and resilient to \(f > 0\) for all distributions; minimal robust estimators (trimmed mean, geometric median) incur \(\Omega(\sigma\sqrt d)\) while "efficient" rules incur infinity. That is why the Byzantine setting tolerates \(d = 10^3\) (aggregated gradients, cross-silo) better than \(d = 10^9\) (raw LLM gradients).
Proposition 4.5 — Secure aggregation: pairwise masks Dual problem: the server must not even see the individual gradients (privacy, ch. 5). Bonawitz et al. (2017): each pair \((k, j)\) shares a pseudo-random seed; client \(k\) sends \(g_k + \sum_{j > k} s_{kj} - \sum_{j < k} s_{kj}\): when summing over all clients, the masks cancel out exactly ( \(s_{kj} = -s_{jk}\) ) and the server only learns \(\sum_k g_k\). Unexpected departures are handled by sharing the seeds via secret sharing (Shamir): the survivors reconstruct the masks of those who left. Fundamental tension: secure aggregation masks the individual gradients… which Krum (4.3) needs; robustness and confidentiality compete — compatible schemes (robust aggregation on cryptographically masked gradients, proofs of "proximity" to the consensus) are an active research topic (ch. 8).

5. Differential privacy

Definition 5.1 — \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-differential privacy (Dwork) Two datasets \(D \sim D'\) are neighbors if they differ in a single individual (addition/removal; some authors: replacement, i.e. Hamming distance 2 — state the convention!). A randomized algorithm \(\mathcal{A}\) is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP if for all \(D \sim D'\) and every event \(S\): $$\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^\varepsilon\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(D') \in S] + \delta.$$ Small \(\varepsilon\) ⇒ the output barely changes if one individual changes — a computable guarantee, composable, independent of the adversary's prior knowledge. \(\delta\) allows a rare leak (typically \(\delta \ll 1/n\)).
Proposition 5.2 — Post-processing If \(\mathcal{A}\) is \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP, any function \(g\) applied to its output (analysis, fine-tuning, publication) is too — DP survives any a posteriori transformation of the results. (Proof: the likelihood ratio of \(g(\mathcal{A}(D))\) inherits that of \(\mathcal{A}(D)\) by change of variables — inequality of measure ratios.)
Theorem 5.3 — Sequential composition (proved, case \(\delta = 0\)) If \(\mathcal{A}_1\) is \(\varepsilon_1\)-DP and \(\mathcal{A}_2\) — applied to \(D\) and to the result of \(\mathcal{A}_1\) — is \(\varepsilon_2\)-DP, then \((\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2)\) is \((\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\)-DP: budgets add up.
For any subset \(S\) of the product of the outputs, conditioning on the first output \(z\): $$\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2)(D) \in S] = \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D) = dz]\; \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D, z) \in S_z]$$ where \(S_z = \{y : (z, y) \in S\}\). By DP of \(\mathcal{A}_2\): \(\leq e^{\varepsilon_2} \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D) = dz]\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D', z) \in S_z]\), then by DP of \(\mathcal{A}_1\): \(\leq e^{\varepsilon_2} e^{\varepsilon_1} \int \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_1(D') = dz]\, \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_2(D', z) \in S_z] = e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\, \mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2)(D') \in S]\). ∎
Usage. \(T\) accesses that are \(\varepsilon\)-DP ⇒ \((T\varepsilon)\)-DP: a naive linear budget. Advanced composition (Rényi DP, moments accountant, 5.5) gives \(\varepsilon \sim \sqrt T\) — it is what makes DP deep learning practical.
Theorem 5.4 — Laplace mechanism (proved via sensitivity) The \(\ell_1\) sensitivity of a query \(q\) is \(\Delta = \max_{D \sim D'} \|q(D) - q(D')\|_1\). The mechanism \(M(D) = q(D) + (Z_1, \dots, Z_k)\), \(Z_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon)\) i.i.d. (density \(\tfrac{1}{2b}e^{-|z|/b}\)), is \(\varepsilon\)-DP.
For any output \(z\), the ratio of densities satisfies, by the triangle inequality: $$\frac{p_M(z \mid D)}{p_M(z \mid D')} = \exp\Big(-\tfrac{\|z - q(D)\|_1 - \|z - q(D')\|_1}{b}\Big) \;\leq\; \exp\Big(\tfrac{\|q(D) - q(D')\|_1}{b}\Big) \;\leq\; e^{\Delta/b} = e^{\varepsilon},$$ with \(b = \Delta/\varepsilon\). Since the density ratio is uniformly bounded by \(e^\varepsilon\), so is the ratio of the probabilities of any event: \(\varepsilon\)-DP. ∎
Added error. \(\mathbb{E}|Z| = b = \Delta/\varepsilon\) (per coordinate): the noise is proportional to the sensitivity — hence the art of DP: rewriting queries to minimize \(\Delta\). Gaussian: for \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\), \(\sigma \geq \Delta_2 \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon\) gives \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP (proof via a Gaussian tail bound; \(\ell_2\) noise is the right regime for training).
Algorithm 5.5 — DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) and the moments accountant
  1. clip each gradient per example: \(\tilde g_i = g_i / \max(1, \|g_i\|_2/C)\) — bounds the sensitivity: changing one individual changes the sum of the clipped gradients by at most \(\sim 2C\);
  2. add noise to the batch: \(\tilde g = \frac1m\big(\sum_i \tilde g_i + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)\big)\);
  3. take the SGD step with \(\tilde g\).
Moments accountant (statement). For a mechanism \(M\), define the Rényi moment of order \(\lambda\): \(M_M(\lambda) = \max_{D \sim D'} \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim M(D')} \big[\big(\tfrac{p_M(z|D)}{p_M(z|D')}\big)^\lambda\big]\): composition adds the moments (\(T\) steps: total moment \(T \cdot M_M(\lambda)\)), and conversion to DP gives $$\varepsilon \;=\; \min_\lambda \frac{T\, M_M(\lambda) - \log \delta}{\lambda},$$ that is, typically \(\varepsilon = \Theta(\sqrt T)\) instead of \(T\) — a quadratic gain on the budget. It is this accountant (implemented in the Opacus/TensorFlow Privacy libraries) that tracks the budget at every step of training. Fundamental trade-off: utility \(\leftrightarrow\) privacy \(\leftrightarrow\) amount of data (the noise, fixed at \(C\), gets averaged over large batches: DP likes large \(n\)).
Proposition 5.6 — Local vs central DP Central: a trusted curator adds the noise (the mechanisms above): good utility/privacy ratio. Local: each user randomizes before sending (no trust required — the keyboard model): the variance accumulates per user, and utility drops (randomized response for counts); the shuffle model (anonymization by cryptographic mixing) sits between the two. Rule of thumb: local for extremely sensitive data, central for research, shuffle for real systems.
Membership inference attack — how it works The adversary's goal: decide whether the example \(x\) was in the training set. Protocol (Shokri et al., 2017): (i) train shadow models on similar data whose member/non-member status is known; (ii) learn a small classifier separating the confidence scores of members vs non-members; (iii) apply it to the confidences of the target model. The exploited signal: memorization — the model is more confident on its training data; the worst memorization concerns "atypical" data (canaries: Carlini et al., 2019, demonstrate verbatim extraction of inserted secrets). DP bounds this signal: the influence of an example on any output is capped by clipping + noise; conversely, an attack rate close to chance is an empirical privacy test.
Why LLGA cares LLMs memorize training fragments (extraction attacks); DP training (DP-SGD on fine-tuning) is an active defense — and the M2 module "Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs" extends exactly this chapter. In the federated setting, DP and secure aggregation (4.5) combine: cryptographic masks against the server, DP against a posteriori adversaries. Graph confidentiality (node-DP, edge-DP) protects social networks — see the Graph ML module. Multi-agent systems (see the Reinforcement Learning module, ch. 7) raise the same question: what do interaction logs leak?

  • Personalization: a global model + local heads (light per-client fine-tuning); Bayesian interpretation (mixture of posteriors); multi-task formalization (Ditto, ch. 3);
  • Model fusion and mode connectivity: two networks trained from the same initialization are connected by a nearly flat path in the loss surface (low barrier along the segment — Frankle et al., 2020); consequence for collaboration: local models can be averaged… provided they stay in the same basin; realigning the neuron permutations between two models is the key to recent fusions (sketch); these are the theoretical foundations of FedAvg (the averaging of chapter 3 only makes sense in this regime);

7. Scaling and compute

Theorem 7.1 — Scaling laws (Kaplan 2020, Chinchilla 2022): formulas and reading On large corpora, the validation loss follows a power law in parameters \(N\) and tokens \(D\) (before the saturation regime): $$\text{Kaplan: } L(N, D) = \Big[\Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D} + \frac{D_c}{D}\Big]^{\alpha_D}, \qquad \text{Chinchilla: } L = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},$$ with for Chinchilla \(\alpha \approx 0.34\), \(\beta \approx 0.28\), and \(E \approx 1.69\) the irreducible loss (entropy of language); \(A, B, N_c, D_c\) are calibrated constants. Training compute is billed \(C \approx 6\,N D\) FLOPs (2 passes × 3 per parameter/token).
Theorem 7.2 — Compute-optimal: the Chinchilla derivation Minimize \(L = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}\) under the budget \(6 N D = C\): the Lagrange conditions (\(-\alpha A N^{-\alpha - 1} = 6\lambda D\) and \(-\beta B D^{-\beta - 1} = 6\lambda N\)) give $$\alpha A\, N^{-\alpha} = \beta B\, D^{-\beta} \;\Rightarrow\; N^\star \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} \approx C^{0.45}, \qquad D^\star \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)} \approx C^{0.55},$$ that is: N and D grow together, almost like \(\sqrt C\), with a ratio \(D^\star/N^\star \approx 20\) tokens per parameter (empirical value: ~1.4T tokens for 70B parameters). Counter-intuitive but verified: Chinchilla (70B) beats Gopher (280B) trained on the same compute; the models of the Kaplan generation were undertrained (too many parameters, too few tokens per parameter). LLGA reading: compute is the scarce resource; power laws justify budgets; "emergent capabilities" are the deviations (thresholds) beyond this smooth law — see the M2 Large Language Models module.
Proposition 7.3 — Mixture of Experts: decoupling FLOPs from parameters An MoE replaces each feed-forward layer with \(E\) "experts" and a router that activates only the top \(k\) (top-1 in Switch Transformer, Fedus et al., 2021) per token: the parameters grow with \(E\) (up to a trillion), while the FLOPs per token stay nearly constant — the Chinchilla law recalibrates on this distinction between active and latent parameters. Technical points: capacity factor (load per expert), balancing loss (preventing routing collapse), all-to-all communication (link to ch. 2).

8.1. El Mahdi El Mhamdi — Byzantine tolerance for distributed ML

The guiding thread: can we learn in a distributed way when some participants lie? — a line of research that founded the field (byzantine-ML) whose chapters 2-4 this course follows.

Work 8.1 — Krum (Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Stainer, NeurIPS 2017) First aggregation rule provably tolerant to Byzantine clients for distributed SGD (Algorithm 4.3): selection of the most surrounded point, guarantee of distance \(\mathcal{O}(\sigma\sqrt d)\) to the true gradient under iid, cost \(O(K^2 d)\). Published at the moment distributed SGD was becoming the industrial standard — ~4000 citations; it is the reference that all subsequent GARs (geometric median, trimmed, Bulyan) discuss.
Work 8.2 — The Hidden Vulnerability & Bulyan (El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Rouault, ICML 2018) Negative result. Coordinate-wise rules (median, trimmed mean — Th. 4.2) are beaten by a "dimension-by-dimension" attack: the attacker stays within the honest interval on each coordinate while correlating its deviations — the global direction drifts; and any rule that selects a point (Krum!) can be forced to pick a bad point under attacks varying over time. Constructive answer: Bulyan — iterate Krum to select \(K - f - 4\) candidates, then coordinate-wise trimmed means on the selected candidates: the selection filters out outliers, the trimming neutralizes the remaining corrupted ones; convergence is proven at the price of a residual bias and an increased variance (the \(1/\sqrt K\) becomes \(1/\sqrt{K - 4f}\) up to a constant). Methodological lesson: impossibility first — characterize what no GAR can do, then build under these bounds.
What next? The program continues: Distributed Momentum for Byzantine-resilient SGD (ICLR 2021) — making the momentum variant robust (the optimizer's memory accumulation enlarges the attack surface, see Proposition 0.4); and more broadly the safety of distributed learning systems: interactions between robustness (ch. 4), confidentiality (secure aggregation, ch. 5) and verifiability — the "complete security" of a federated system is a multi-objective problem, not an accumulation of patches.

8.2. Aymeric Dieuleveut — stochastic acceleration and the federated setting

Work 8.3 — Optimal averaged SGD (Dieuleveut, Flammarion, Bach, JMLR 2017) Statement (finite-dimensional case). For least-squares regression (quadratic function, noise \(\sigma^2\), dimension \(d\), condition number \(\kappa\)), SGD with Polyak-Ruppert averaging — and its "tail averaging" version \(\bar\theta_n = \tfrac{2}{n}\sum_{t > n/2} \theta_t\) which discards the first iterations — with step size \(\eta \propto 1/\sqrt n\) reaches $$\mathbb{E}\big[F(\bar\theta_n)\big] - F^\star \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{d\,\sigma^2}{n}\Big) \qquad \text{(minimax rate, without knowing } \sigma^2 \text{ or the spectrum)},$$ where plain SGD with a constant step size plateaus at \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (Theorem 0.1) and would require exact knowledge of the constants to be optimal. Idea. Averaging decouples bias and variance: the individual iterates remain noisy, but their average estimates the optimum asymptotically without bias (the noise averages out at rate \(1/n\), the deterministic dynamics converge fast). It is the tool that makes SGD "parameter-free" — behind the practical optimizers of the module and the optimal Nadaraya-Watson-type rates in the nonparametric setting.
Work 8.4 — Federated-EM (Dieuleveut, Fort, Moulines, Robin, NeurIPS 2021) First federated extension of the EM algorithm to models with latent variables (Gaussian mixtures, HMMs): each client computes its local E statistics, the server aggregates and performs the M step. Result. A version with control variates (the SCAFFOLD/SVRG motif — Propositions 0.3 and 3.5!) corrects heterogeneity and variance: convergence is proven toward a neighborhood of the optimum at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/R)\) in rounds, with radius controlled by heterogeneity and mitigated by the correction — the same theorem as 3.3, transposed to EM steps. Applications: federated GMMs in healthcare, federated HMMs in telephony; extended by Federated Majorize-Minimization (2025), which unifies these constructions beyond the server parameter.
And also: communication Artemis (Philippenko & Dieuleveut, 2020): tight bounds for bidirectional compression (server ⇄ clients, ch. 2) in heterogeneous federated learning — when the download AND the upload are compressed, memory is needed (error feedback on both sides) to preserve the rate. The guiding thread of this work: making the guarantees of chapter 0 robust to the realities of chapters 2-3 (expensive communication, heterogeneity, statistical variance of clients).

  10. Gossip. Simulate gossip aggregation \(\theta^{(k)} \leftarrow \theta^{(k)} + \gamma \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(k)}(\theta^{(j)} - \theta^{(k)})\) on a cycle then on a complete graph; relate the speed to the second eigenvalue of the Laplacian (spectrum = Graph ML module).
  11. Chinchilla on paper. Redo the derivation of Theorem 7.2 with \(\alpha = 0.34\), \(\beta = 0.28\): compute the exponents of \(N^\star\) and \(D^\star\) in \(C\); for \(C = 10^{23}\) FLOPs, deduce an order of magnitude of \(N^\star\) (with the constants of the paper: \(A \approx 406\), \(B \approx 410\)) and the tokens/parameters ratio.
Solutions — exercises 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 9, 11

1. Independence: \(\mathrm{Var}(\tfrac1m\sum_i g_i) = \tfrac{1}{m^2} m\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/m\). For \(K\) workers with i.i.d. batches of size \(m\), the aggregate has variance \(\sigma^2/(Km)\) — plug into Theorem 0.1 (replace \(\sigma\) by \(\sigma/\sqrt{Km}\)): for a fixed total number of examples consumed, the same error as sequentially — but each step cost a \(1/K\)-th of the time (parallel computation): this is the speedup, valid if communication is free (Theorem 2.2 for the full version).

2. (i) convexity: \(f(\theta^\star) \geq f(\theta_t) + \langle\nabla f(\theta_t), \theta^\star - \theta_t\rangle\); (ii) \(\mathbb{E}\|g\|^2 = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\xi\|^2\), then \(\tfrac12\|\nabla f(\theta)\|^2 \leq L(f(\theta) - f^\star)\) (smoothness + convexity); (iii) telescoping: \(\sum_t (\mathbb{E}D_t - \mathbb{E}D_{t+1}) = D_0 - \mathbb{E}D_T \leq D^2\). If \(\eta > 2/L\): \(1 - \eta L < 0\), the recursion no longer controls anything — the optimal deterministic step \(\eta = 1/L\) stays below the \(2/L\) bar set by the noise.

3. \(E = 1\): \(\theta_{r+1} = \frac1K\sum_k(\theta_r - \eta g_k(\theta_r)) = \theta_r - \eta \bar g(\theta_r)\) — exact distributed SGD. For the drift: apply Theorem 3.2 (values: fixed point \(\approx 0.713\) versus optimum \(\approx 0.909\)); at \(E = 1\) the bias vanishes (\(c_1 = c_2 = 1 - \eta\), mean and optimum coincide), it grows with \(E\) — plot \(|\theta_\infty - \theta^\star|\) as a function of \(E\).

5. The mean: an attacker sends \(C = \frac{K-f}{f}\,\bar g_{\mathrm{hon}} + \delta\) and shifts the mean by \(\delta/f\) in the desired direction — arbitrary. The median: with \(K - f > K/2\) honest values in \([m, M]\), the median lies in \([m, M]\) (Theorem 4.2); the gap to the honest median is at most the diameter of the honest part — bounded independently of the attack.

8. Sensitivity: adding/removing one individual changes \(q\) by at most 1, so \(\Delta = 1\); Theorem 5.4 gives \(\varepsilon\)-DP for \(b = 1/\varepsilon\). Typical error: \(\mathbb{E}|Z| = 1/\varepsilon\) (one "blurred" individual at \(\varepsilon = 1\); at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), ten — hence the price of strong DP on small counts).

9. Three mechanisms: apply the nesting of Theorem 5.3 twice (\(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3\)). One hundred \(\varepsilon_0\)-DP queries: \(100\,\varepsilon_0\) under simple composition; advanced composition (moments accountant, 5.5) gives roughly \(\varepsilon \approx \sqrt{2 \times 100 \ln(1.25/\delta)}\,\varepsilon_0\) (at \(\delta = 10^{-6}\): \(\approx 63\,\varepsilon_0\)) — better, but still growing: DP taxes every access, definitively.

11. Exponents: \(\beta/(\alpha+\beta) = 0.28/0.62 \approx 0.45\) for \(N^\star\), \(0.34/0.62 \approx 0.55\) for \(D^\star\). With \(C = 10^{23}\): the Lagrange condition gives \(N \approx 1.7\, D^{0.82}\), and \(6ND = C\) solves to \(D^\star \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}\) tokens and \(N^\star \approx 1.5 \times 10^{10}\) parameters (order of magnitude, parametric fit); the ratio \(D^\star/N^\star\) comes out between 20 and 80 depending on the fitting method of the paper (IsoFLOP vs parametric) — two figures, one single lesson: at a fixed budget, feed the model with tokens rather than inflating the parameters.
