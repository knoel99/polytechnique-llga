0. Reminders: spectrum, surfaces, vector calculus

L3/M1 prerequisites mobilized
  • Differential calculus: gradient, divergence, Laplacian in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (\(\Delta f = \partial_{xx} f + \partial_{yy} f\)); Green's formula;
  • Symmetric diagonalization: \(L = U\Lambda U^\top\), orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions — already seen for the graph Laplacian (Graph ML module, ch. 0);
  • Convolution & Fourier (Signal Processing module): the Laplacian is the frequency operator; heat diffusion \(\partial_t u = \Delta u\);
  • GNN (Graph ML module): a mesh is a graph — but a geometric one (3D positions, metric).
Theorem 0.1 — Spectral (reminder with proof) If \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real and there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors: \(A = U \Lambda U^\top\). Moreover the Rayleigh quotient $$R(x) = \frac{x^\top A\, x}{x^\top x}, \qquad \lambda_{\min} = \min_{x \ne 0} R(x), \quad \lambda_{\max} = \max_{x \ne 0} R(x).$$
Standard sketch. \(R\) is continuous on the (compact) unit sphere, attains its maximum at some \(u_1\), \(\|u_1\| = 1\). On the sphere, the critical points of \(R\) satisfy \(Au = R(u)\,u\) (set the gradient of \(x^\top A x\) to zero under the constraint \(x^\top x = 1\), Lagrange multipliers): \(u_1\) is an eigenvector. Iterate on the orthogonal complement of \(u_1\) (stable under \(A\) by symmetry: \(\langle Au_1, v\rangle = \lambda_1\langle u_1, v\rangle = 0\)), yielding the basis by recursion. The characterization of \(\lambda_{\min}\)/\(\lambda_{\max}\) follows from \(R(x) = \sum_k \lambda_k c_k^2\) with \(\sum_k c_k^2 = 1\) (barycenter of the \(\lambda_k\)). ∎
Generalized problem Geometric operators give pairs \((L, M)\) with \(M\) (mass) positive definite, and one solves \(L\phi = \lambda M\phi\). Setting \(\tilde L = M^{-1/2}LM^{-1/2}\) (symmetric!), thm. 0.1 applies and \(\phi = M^{-1/2}\tilde\phi\). This is exactly the igl.eigs(-L, M, k) call of the lab sessions.
Definition 0.2 — Surface, charts, atlas A surface (2-dimensional manifold) is a set covered by charts: bijections \(x : U \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \to V \subset M\) whose chart transitions \(x_2^{-1} \circ x_1\) are smooth. The collection of charts is an atlas. Example: the sphere. Two stereographic charts (from the North pole and the South pole) suffice; on their intersection, the chart transition is the inversion \(u \mapsto u/\|u\|^2\) — smooth. A function \(f : M \to \mathbb{R}\) is smooth if \(f \circ x\) is in every chart; intrinsic quantities (lengths, areas, Laplacian) do not depend on the chosen chart.
Vector calculus — Green and self-adjointness Gradient (direction of steepest slope), divergence (outgoing flux per unit area), \(\Delta f = \mathrm{div}\,\nabla f\). Green's formula $$\int_{\Omega} \langle \nabla f, \nabla g\rangle\, dA = -\int_\Omega f\,\Delta g\, dA + \oint_{\partial\Omega} f\,\partial_n g\, ds$$ (geometric integration by parts) is the reason why the Laplacian is symmetric: on a closed surface (without boundary), \(\int f \Delta g = \int g \Delta f\) — and this symmetry carries over to the discrete setting (ch. 2).

Convolution and Fourier (reminder). Convolution \((f * g)(x) = \int f(x-y)g(y)dy\) is the unique commutative, bilinear, continuous, translation-invariant product; the Fourier transform diagonalizes it: \(\widehat{f*g} = \hat f \cdot \hat g\), and \(\widehat{\Delta f} = -|\omega|^2\hat f\). This whole machinery disappears on a surface (no translation group); the Laplacian — and it alone — plays its role: this is the program of chapters 2–4.

1. Discrete differential geometry

Definition 1.1 — Triangulated surface A mesh \(M = (V, E, F)\): vertices \(v_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\), edges, triangular faces. It is both a graph and an approximation of a smooth surface; the mixed Voronoi area \(A_i\) associates a "zone" with each vertex.
Construction 1.2 — Barycentric Voronoi areas (computation) Each vertex \(i\) receives, within each incident triangle, the portion of the Voronoi cell contained in that triangle. Summed up, the dual area is written per edge: $$A_i = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j \sim i}\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big)\,\|v_i - v_j\|^2,$$ where \(\alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij}\) are the angles opposite to edge \((i,j)\). Check on a regular triangular lattice of side \(a\): each vertex has 6 neighbors, each edge has two opposite angles of \(60°\), hence \(\cot\alpha + \cot\beta = 2/\sqrt3\) per edge and $$A_i = \frac{1}{8}\cdot 6\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\,a^2 = \frac{\sqrt3\,a^2}{2} = 6 \times \frac{A_T}{3}, \qquad A_T = \frac{\sqrt3\,a^2}{4},$$ that is, exactly one third of the area of each of the six incident triangles. For obtuse triangles, the per-edge formula can become negative: one switches locally to the barycenter ("mixed", Meyer et al. 2003).
Discrete curvature: the Gauss angle deficit $$K_i = \frac{2\pi - \sum_{f \ni i}\theta_{i,f}}{A_i}$$ (flat ⇒ \(K = 0\); cone ⇒ positive; saddle ⇒ negative). This is a total measure of Gaussian curvature over the zone \(A_i\).
Why \(2\pi\) minus the sum of the angles? Rotate a vector by parallel transport around vertex \(i\), staying on the faces (which are flat): along each edge the vector does not rotate (the faces are Euclidean), but when crossing from face to face it "jumps" by the dihedral angle — and when closing the loop, the final vector makes with the initial one an angle equal to \(2\pi - \sum_f \theta_{i,f}\): the deficit. Now on a smooth surface, parallel transport around a loop encircling a total Gaussian curvature \(\int K\,dA\) rotates by exactly this angle (local Gauss–Bonnet theorem, "transport" equivalent of the theorema egregium: curvature is intrinsic). The deficit is therefore the discrete analogue of \(\int_{A_i} K\,dA\), hence the density \(K_i\). ∎
Theorem 1.3 — Discrete Gauss–Bonnet (statement + numerical example) $$\sum_{i \in V} K_i\, A_i \;=\; \sum_i \Big(2\pi - \sum_{f\ni i}\theta_{i,f}\Big) \;=\; 2\pi\,\chi(M), \qquad \chi = |V| - |E| + |F|.$$ The total curvature depends only on the topology. Example: the cube. Eight vertices, three squares (faces split into triangles: angles of \(\pi/2\)) meet at each vertex: deficit \(2\pi - 3\times\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\); total \(8 \times \frac{\pi}{2} = 4\pi = 2\pi \times 2\) — the characteristic of the sphere. A useful counterexample: the torus (donut) has \(\chi = 0\): however it is meshed, the positive and negative deficits cancel out exactly.
Principal curvatures, mean curvature: via the Laplacian of positions At each point, the normal sections of the surface have extreme curvatures \(k_1 \le k_2\) (principal); Gaussian curvature \(K = k_1k_2\) (the deficit approximates it); mean curvature \(H = \frac{k_1+k_2}{2}\). Vector version, computable directly on the mesh: $$\Delta_M\, \mathbf{x} = 2H\,\mathbf{n} \qquad\text{(positions }\mathbf{x}(p) \text{ viewed as 3 functions on } M\text{)}.$$ Justification (sketch). The vector \(\Delta\mathbf{x}\) is the gradient of area: varying the surface by \(\varepsilon\,f\,\mathbf{n}\) changes the area by \(\varepsilon \int 2Hf\,dA\) (first variation; minimal surfaces — soap bubbles — are those with \(H = 0\)). Now computing the gradient of area by integration by parts (prop. 0.2) brings out \(\Delta\mathbf{x}\); normalizing by the normal yields the formula. In the discrete setting: \(\mathbf{H}_i = \frac12 (L\mathbf{x})_i\) with the cotangent Laplacian of ch. 2 — one line of libigl.
  • Normal of a face: \(n_f \propto (v_j - v_i) \times (v_k - v_i)\); vertex normal: area-weighted (or angle-weighted) average;
  • Barycentric coordinates: in a triangle \((i,j,k)\), every point can be written \(\alpha v_i + \beta v_j + \gamma v_k\), \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1\), with \(\alpha\) = normalized area of the opposite sub-triangle; they serve to interpolate any function from the vertices to the interior of the faces (a mesh carries values only at vertices);
  • Finite differences vs finite elements: the "FD" route approximates \(\Delta f\) by combinations of neighbors (like the graph Laplacian); the "FEM" route projects onto the basis of hat functions \(\psi_i\) (1 at \(v_i\), 0 at the other vertices, affine on the faces) and assembles the stiffness matrix $$K_{ij} = \int_M \langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle\, dA, \qquad M_{ij} = \int_M \psi_i\psi_j\, dA\ \ (\text{mass}),$$ giving the operator \(L = M^{-1}K\). The explicit computation of \(K_{ij}\) on a triangle — an object of ch. 2 — recovers exactly the cotangents: FEM and discrete geometry converge.
Useful jargon The pair \((K, M)\) of ch. 0 is called "stiffness/mass" here; the convergence of discrete operators toward the smooth Laplace-Beltrami (as the mesh is refined) is subtle — the cotangent one converges in the weak sense (Wardetzky 2007); this is the reason why DiffusionNet (ch. 6, ch. 8) prefers to require only heat operators from the discrete setting.

2. The Laplace-Beltrami operator

Definition 2.1 — Laplace-Beltrami The Laplacian on the surface: \(\Delta_M f = \mathrm{div}_M(\nabla_M f)\). In a local chart with metric \(g = (g_{ab})\) (the \(g_{ab}\) measure how the chart distorts lengths): $$\Delta_M f = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{a,b}\partial_a\!\Big(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{ab}\,\partial_b f\Big),$$ which recovers \(\partial_{xx} + \partial_{yy}\) when \(g = I\). In the discrete setting, the cotangent matrix: $$L_{ij} = \begin{cases} -\frac{1}{2A_i}\sum_{j \sim i}(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}) & i = j\\[2pt] \frac{1}{2A_i}(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}) & j \sim i\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$ where \(\alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij}\) are the angles opposite to edge \((i,j)\). Like the graph Laplacian: symmetric (in the \(M\)-self-adjoint sense), positive semi-definite, Fourier basis \(\{\phi_k\}\) with \(\Delta \phi_k = -\lambda_k \phi_k\), increasing \(\lambda_k\) (= the "frequencies" of the surface).
Derivation 2.2 — The cotangent operator via the Dirichlet energy The starting point is the Dirichlet energy (the "stiffness" of \(f\)): $$E_D(f) = \tfrac12 \int_M \|\nabla f\|^2\, dA = \tfrac12\sum_{T}\int_T \|\nabla f\|^2\, dA.$$ By approximating \(f\) with its barycentric interpolant (affine on each triangle, values \(f_i\) at vertices), one gets an exact quadratic form: $$E_D(f) = \tfrac14 \sum_{(i,j) \in E} w_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2, \qquad w_{ij} = \tfrac12\big(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}\big),$$ and the cotangent matrix is by definition the matrix of this quadratic form: \(L = \arg\min\) of gradient descent, such that \(\frac{\partial E_D}{\partial f_i} = -2A_i (Lf)_i\).
Step 1 — gradient of an affine function on a triangle. On triangle \(T = (i,j,k)\), the interpolant is \(f = f_i\psi_i + f_j\psi_j + f_k\psi_k\); each \(\nabla\psi\) is constant on \(T\), orthogonal to the opposite side, with norm \(1/h\) (associated height); in particular \(\nabla\psi_i + \nabla\psi_j + \nabla\psi_k = 0\). Step 2 — inner product of two gradients. Let \(h_i, h_j\) denote the heights from \(i\) and \(j\), and \(\theta_k\) the angle at vertex \(k\) (opposite to edge \((i,j)\)). The gradient \(\nabla\psi_i\) is orthogonal to side \((j,k)\), points toward \(i\), and \(\|\nabla\psi_i\| = 1/h_i\); likewise \(\|\nabla\psi_j\| = 1/h_j\), orthogonal to side \((i,k)\). The two sides \((j,k)\) and \((i,k)\) making an angle \(\theta_k\) with each other, their normals make an angle \(\pi - \theta_k\), and the pointing directions flip the sign: \(\langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle = -\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_i h_j}\) (constant on \(T\)). Then, with \(|T|\) the area and \(\|e\|\) the side lengths: $$\int_T \langle\nabla\psi_i, \nabla\psi_j\rangle\, dA = |T|\cdot\Big(-\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_i h_j}\Big) = -\frac{|T|\,\|e_{jk}\|\,\|e_{ik}\|}{(2|T|)(2|T|)}\cos\theta_k = -\frac{\cos\theta_k}{2\sin\theta_k} = -\frac12\cot\theta_k,$$ where we used \(h_i = 2|T|/\|e_{jk}\|\), \(h_j = 2|T|/\|e_{ik}\|\) and then \(|T| = \tfrac12\|e_{jk}\|\,\|e_{ik}\|\sin\theta_k\). Step 3 — assembly. Each edge \((i,j)\) belongs to one or two triangles; summing \(\tfrac12 f_i f_j \int_T\langle\nabla\psi_i,\nabla\psi_j\rangle\) over these triangles and reorganizing the quadratic form as \(\tfrac14\sum_{ij} w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2\) (the diagonal terms absorb the off-diagonal ones — exercise 2), the cotangent weights appear, with the contribution \(-\tfrac12\cot\theta_k\) of each opposite triangle. ∎
Properties (and why they hold)
  • Symmetry: \(W = (w_{ij})\) is symmetric (each edge carries its weight, constructed symmetrically); this is the discrete version of the self-adjointness of ch. 0;
  • Constants annihilated: \(L\mathbf{1} = 0\) because the quadratic form only sees the differences \(f_i - f_j\);
  • Positivity: \(E_D(f) \ge 0\), zero iff \(f\) is constant (on each connected component); hence \(\lambda_1 = 0\) has multiplicity 1 and \(\lambda_2 > 0\) measures the "connectivity" (the Fiedler value of surfaces);
  • Convergence: as the mesh is refined, \(L\) converges to \(\Delta_M\) in the weak sense — subject to triangle quality (no too-obtuse angles; Wardetzky 2007);
  • Nodal sets: the zeros of \(\phi_k\) cut the surface into increasingly fine regions as \(k\) grows — the "wavefronts" of the surface; \(\lambda_k \sim c\,k\) (Weyl's law in dimension 2).
Theorem 2.3 — Heat kernel: spectral construction The solution of the heat equation \(\partial_t u = -\Delta u\), \(u(0) = u_0\), reads $$u(t) = e^{-t\Delta}u_0 = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\,\langle \phi_k, u_0\rangle\,\phi_k, \qquad h_t(x, y) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\,\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y).$$ Each mode \(\phi_k\) is damped by \(e^{-\lambda_k t}\): diffusion = spectral low-pass filtering.
Direct substitution: \(\partial_t\big[e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k\big] = -\lambda_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k = -\Delta\big[e^{-\lambda_k t}\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k\big]\) (term by term, \(\Delta\phi_k = -\lambda_k\phi_k\)); at \(t = 0\), the sum telescopes to \(\sum_k\langle\phi_k,u_0\rangle\phi_k = u_0\) (orthonormal basis — for the \(M\)-weighted inner product of the lab sessions). The kernel \(h_t\) is just the same writing in "integral" form: \(u(x,t) = \int h_t(x,y)u_0(y)dy\) (exercise 6). ∎
Diffusion distances: geometry of the heat kernel $$D_t^2(x, y) = \|h_t(x, \cdot) - h_t(y, \cdot)\|^2_{L^2} = \sum_k e^{-2\lambda_k t}\,\big(\phi_k(x) - \phi_k(y)\big)^2.$$ Two points are close if their "thermal fingerprints" are close; the exponential weighting short-circuits high frequencies — robust to noise, unlike the geodesic distance. This is the building block of the diffusion geometry of ch. 3.
Isometric invariance (proved) If \(T : M \to N\) is an isometry (preserves the lengths of curves), then for every \(f\) on \(N\) and every \(x \in M\), as encountered in ch. 3: $$\Delta_M (f \circ T)(x) = (\Delta_N f)(T(x)).$$ Consequence: identical spectra, transported eigenfunctions — the foundation of all non-rigid shape comparison.
An isometry preserves lengths and areas, hence inner products of gradients and integration: for all \(u, v\) on \(N\), $$\int_N \langle\nabla u, \nabla v\rangle\, dA_N = \int_M \langle\nabla(u\circ T), \nabla(v\circ T)\rangle\, dA_M$$ (change of variable \(T\), Jacobian = 1). By Green's formula (prop. 0.2 — self-adjointness), \(\int v\,\Delta_N u = \int (v\circ T)\,\Delta_M(u\circ T)\) for every \(v\); since this holds for every test function \(v\circ T\) (surjective), \(\Delta_M(u\circ T) = (\Delta_N u)\circ T\) pointwise. ∎

Why this is the central tool
  • Isometric invariance: the spectrum does not change if the surface is "bent" without stretching — non-rigid shape comparison (a moving human);
  • Diffusion: heat \(u(t) = e^{-t\Delta}u_0\) — "Can one hear the shape of a drum?": the spectrum encodes the geometry (with honest limits, ch. 3);
  • Reduced dimension: projecting onto the first \(k\) eigenfunctions ≈ PCA of the surface; all of ch. 4 follows from it.

3. Shape descriptors and distances

Definition 3.1 — Heat Kernel Signature (HKS) The amount of residual heat at \(x\) after time \(t\): $$\mathrm{HKS}(x, t) = h_t(x, x) = \sum_k e^{-t\lambda_k}\, \phi_k(x)^2$$ — an intrinsic descriptor: stable, isometrically invariant; multi-scale (small \(t\) = local detail, large = global). These signatures play the role of "features" for correspondence.
Stability (proved sketch). Perturb the metric by \(\|\delta g\| \le \varepsilon\) (scan noise, different mesh): the perturbations of the eigenvalues are bounded by Weyl's theorem (\(|\delta\lambda_k| \le C\varepsilon\lambda_k\)), and those of the eigenfunctions by \(\|\delta\phi_k\| \le C'\varepsilon\lambda_k/\mathrm{gap}_k\) (where \(\mathrm{gap}_k\) is the local spectral gap — this is why one avoids multiple eigenvalues). Then $$|\delta\mathrm{HKS}(x,t)| \le \sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\big(|\delta\lambda_k|t\,\phi_k(x)^2 + 2|\phi_k||\delta\phi_k|\big) = O(\varepsilon)\,\sum_k e^{-\lambda_k t}\lambda_k\phi_k(x)^2,$$ a convergent series for every \(t > 0\) (Weyl-type growth against exponential decay): the HKS is Lipschitz in the metric — hence its empirical robustness. Note that this computation also justifies the limitation: at large \(t\), only the small \(\lambda_k\) contribute (a global, not very discriminative descriptor); at small \(t\), the series is sensitive to high frequencies (noise); hence the choice of a logarithmic grid of \(t\). ∎
  • Scale-normalized HKS: dividing \(\mathrm{HKS}(x, t)\) by its max at \(x\) (or by \(\max_x\mathrm{HKS}(x,t)\)) makes the descriptor scale-invariant — the \(\lambda_k\) rescale by \(s^{-2}\) when the shape is dilated by \(s\), and the normalization absorbs the factor;
  • Wave Kernel Signature (Aubry et al., 2011): a quantum particle of energy \(e\) distributed log-uniformly: \(\mathrm{WKS}(x, e) = \sum_k \phi_k(x)^2 e^{-(\log e - \log\lambda_k)^2/2\sigma^2}\) — a Gaussian window on a logarithmic scale: each energy probes one precise frequency band (better localization than the HKS, whose every \(t\) mixes low frequencies);
  • ShapeDNA (Reuter et al.): the vector \((\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)\) — normalized by area — as a global "fingerprint"; \(L^2\) distance between fingerprints;
  • Intrinsic distances: geodesic (shortest path on the surface): Dijkstra's algorithm on the graph of the mesh (edge lengths) = lower bound; continuous refinement (Fast Marching, Kimmel–Sethian) solves the eikonal equation \(|\nabla d| = 1\) by a growing wavefront; the modern "heat method" variant (Crane et al.) first solves heat and then takes its renormalized gradient — O(linear solver); diffusion distance (prop. 2.4): truncated spectral approximations of both;
  • Can one hear the shape of a drum? No — Gordon, Webb, and Wolpert (1992) constructed pairs of surfaces that are isospectral but not isometric. The spectrum does not determine the shape: this is a fundamental (and mathematically sound) limit of the whole spectral pipeline — ShapeDNA, spectral constraints of functional maps, HKS. In practice: the spectrum discriminates almost everything, and one always complements it with operator and descriptor constraints (ch. 4);
  • Flattening (embedding): the first \(k\) eigenfunctions give an embedding \(x \mapsto (\phi_1(x), \dots, \phi_k(x))\) that "unfolds" the shape — the exact analogue of spectral clustering/LapEig for graphs (Advanced GNN, ch. 3): same algebra, different object.

4. Functional maps

Theorem/Concept 4.1 — Functional Maps (Ovsjanikov et al., 2012) Instead of looking for the point-to-point correspondence (a combinatorial discrete map, costly), transport functions defined on the surfaces: $$T_F : \mathcal{F}(M) \to \mathcal{F}(N), \qquad T_F(g) = g \circ T^{-1}$$ Restricted to the spectral bases \(\Phi_M, \Phi_N\) (the first \(k\) eigenfunctions), the map becomes a small matrix \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}\): $$b_N \approx C\, b_M \quad (b = \Phi^\top f).$$ A correspondence of \(10^5\) points is replaced by \(k^2\) numbers (\(k \sim 30\)): this is the paradigm shift of the field.
Constraints 4.2 — what C must satisfy
  • Descriptor preservation: if \(D_M \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times q}\) are the coefficients of \(q\) descriptors (HKS, WKS...) on \(M\) and \(D_N\) on \(N\) (in the same order): $$C\,D_M \approx D_N \qquad (k \times q \text{ equations});$$
  • Commutation with operators: $$C\,\Lambda_M \approx \Lambda_N\,C$$ (spectral regularization — the subject of the proof below);
  • Orthogonality: if one believes in an isometry, \(C^\top C \approx I\) (preserves inner products of functions; follows from \(\Phi_N^\top C = \Phi_M^\top\) for orthonormal bases);
  • Correspondence coupling: if a few pairs of points are known, \(\Phi_N^\top \Pi^\top \Phi_M \approx C\) with \(\Pi\) the permutation matrix (hard constraint vs least squares).
Commutation: proved. Assume \(T : M \to N\) is an isometry and let \(T_F\) denote the transport of functions. The invariance theorem of ch. 2 (full proof in 2.5) says: \(\Delta_M(f \circ T) = (\Delta_N f)\circ T\), in other words $$\Delta_M \, T_F = T_F\, \Delta_N \quad\text{(after identifying the directions)}.$$ Apply to \(f = \phi_k^N\) (eigenfunction of \(N\), \(\Delta_N\phi_k^N = -\lambda_k^N\phi_k^N\)): \(\Delta_M\,T_F\phi_k^N = T_F(-\lambda_k^N\phi_k^N) = -\lambda_k^N\,T_F\phi_k^N\): the transport of an eigenfunction is an eigenfunction for the same eigenvalue. If the spectrum is simple, \(T_F\phi_k^N = \pm\phi_k^M\); in the eigenbasis, the matrix of \(T_F\) is therefore almost diagonal: \(C_{ij} \ne 0\) only if \(\lambda_i^M\) and \(\lambda_j^N\) coincide — hence the constraint \(C\Lambda_M \approx \Lambda_N C\) (weak: in the least-squares sense, since real surfaces are only approximately isometric and multiple eigenvalues create blocks). ∎
Resolution 4.3 — least squares and normal equations $$C^* = \arg\min_C\ \big\|C D_M - D_N\big\|_F^2 + \alpha\big\|C\Lambda_M - \Lambda_N C\big\|_F^2 + \beta\big\|C^\top C - I\big\|_F^2 + \mu\|C\|_F^2.$$ Without the orthogonality term, each row \(c_i\) of \(C\) is solved independently by normal equations: $$c_i = \big(D_M D_M^\top + \alpha\,\Omega + \mu I\big)^{-1} D_M d_i^{\;N\top},$$ with \(\Omega\) the regularization matrix derived from commutation — a \(k \times k\) linear system solved in a few milliseconds. With orthogonality, one projects onto rotations (SVD: \(C \leftarrow UV^\top\) — Procrustes projection).
Conversion to point-to-point correspondence + refinement Soft correspondence: $$P = \Phi_N\, C\, \Phi_M^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{|V_N| \times |V_M|}, \qquad \Pi(x) = \arg\max_y P_{yx}$$ — the matrix \(P\) (barely dense, a product of three long matrices) encodes the "probability" that \(y\) corresponds to \(x\). Refinement (the ZoomOut spirit, ch. 8): iteratively, (1) increase \(k\) by one notch; (2) recompute \(C\) using the current hard correspondence as a coupling constraint; (3) re-derive \(P\). The growing spectral resolution acts as annealing: large structures settle at small \(k\) (strong regularization), details adjust afterwards — algorithm: 
C = least_squares(k=k0, descriptors)          # coarse start
for k in range(k0, kmax, step):
    Pi = argmax_per_column(Phi_N[k] @ C @ Phi_M[k].T)   # hard
    C = solve(constraints + coupling(Pi), dim=k)        # soft
return Pi                                          # final correspondence
  • Partial maps: when only a part of \(M\) corresponds to \(N\), \(T_F\) becomes a restriction; the matrix \(C\) is then rectangular (or one embeds \(M\) into \(N\) via a prolongation operator); the constraints adapt (descriptors only on the visible part);
  • Learned extensions: the descriptors \(D\) can be produced by a network (SpectralNet, then GeomFMaps — deep geometric functional maps): the network learns descriptors that are optimal for the matrix C, the resolution remaining a differentiable least squares; the complete computation graph (network → C → correspondence loss) is trained end to end;
  • Supervised vs unsupervised: with annotated pairs (FAUST), one directly supervises the geodesic error of \(\Pi\); otherwise, one maximizes orthogonality and preservation (HKS on both sides): unsupervised works well for quasi-isometries (humans);
  • Applications: texture transfer: the texture (a color function!) on \(M\) is transported by \(b_N = C\,b_M\) — a matrix multiplication; pose transfer (the correspondence coordinates), morphing;
  • LLGA connection: a functional map ≈ an optimal transport between reduced bases; shape alignment ≈ graph alignment by Gromov-Wasserstein (OT module, ch. 5); the three languages — GNN, OT, functional maps — describe the same operation of structural correspondence.

5. Parameterization and processing

Harmonic maps — equations proved Flatten a surface with boundary onto the disk \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2\): look for \(u : M \to \mathbb{R}^2\) minimizing the Dirichlet energy \(E_D(u) = \tfrac12\int\|\nabla u\|^2\) with the boundary fixed on \(\partial\Omega\) (unit circle, uniformly — Tutte).
First variation: for every perturbation \(\delta u\) that vanishes on the boundary, $$0 = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E_D(u + \varepsilon\,\delta u)\Big|_0 = \int_M \langle\nabla u, \nabla\delta u\rangle\, dA = -\int_M \delta u\,\Delta u\, dA + \oint_{\partial M}\delta u\,\partial_n u\, ds.$$ The boundary term vanishes (\(\delta u = 0\) on \(\partial M\)); since \(\delta u\) is arbitrary in the interior, \(\Delta u \equiv 0\) (component by component): the minimizer is harmonic. Tutte's theorem further guarantees that if the boundary is sent to the convex contour of the circle, the map is injective (no folding). In the discrete setting: the discrete Dirichlet energy (derivation 2.2!) leads to the linear system \(L u = 0\) with boundary rows replaced by the constraints — a Poisson solver, a few tens of milliseconds. ∎
  • Conformal: a map is conformal if it preserves angles — the local Jacobian is a similarity \(du = s\,R\) (Cauchy–Riemann intuition: real and imaginary parts are conjugate harmonic); it preserves the shape of small details (textures without shear), but distorts areas; the algorithms (LSCM and descendants) enforce conformity through quadratic constraints;
  • ARAP — as-rigid-as-possible: minimize $$E_{\mathrm{ARAP}}(u) = \sum_{T}\min_{R_T \in SO(2)} \big\|J_T(u) - R_T\big\|_F^2,$$ where \(J_T(u)\) is the Jacobian of the map on face \(T\) and \(R_T\) the "closest" rotation; local/global solver: (a) local: each face fixes \(R_T = UV^\top\) (SVD of \(J_T\)); (b) global: with the rotations fixed, \(E\) becomes quadratic in \(u\) → linear system; alternate until convergence — a geometric EM; an angle/area compromise without excessive distortion;
  • Spectral smoothing/filtering: attenuate high frequencies (denoise), sharpening — signal processing (M1) transported to surfaces;
  • Deformation: laplacian editing: move handles while preserving \(\Delta\mathbf{x}\) of the positions (the details): minimize \(\sum\|L\mathbf{x}' - L\mathbf{x}\|^2\) under constraints — the same machinery as the harmonic map; skinning, morphing — graphics/AR applications of the sister ViCAI module;
  • Poisson reconstruction: from a normal field \(\mathbf{v}\) (oriented, scan): look for the surface \(\chi\) (indicator function) such that $$\nabla\chi = \mathbf{v} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Delta\chi = \mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}$$ (projection of the field onto gradients — in the least-squares sense, then a Poisson solve); this is the bridge scan → closed mesh;
  • Decimation — quadric error metric: associate to each vertex the quadric \(Q_i = \sum_{p \in \text{incident planes}} A_p^\top A_p\) (sum of the products of the unit plane equations); the cost of contracting edge \((i,j)\) to the point \(\bar v\) is \(Q_{ij}(\bar v) = Q_i(\bar v) + Q_j(\bar v)\) — a quadratic form, minimized in closed form; one contracts the lowest-cost edges with a binary heap;
  • Curvature-based remeshing: vertex density \(\propto\) local curvature (deficit of ch. 1) — allocate the triangle budget where the geometry demands it.

6. Deep learning on surfaces

Architecture 6.1 — PointNet (Qi et al., 2017) Point clouds (without a mesh): per point: shared MLP; global max aggregation (permutation-invariant — the same requirement as GNNs, Graph ML module ch. 2); cloud classification/segmentation: $$f(X) = \rho\Big(\max_{x_i \in X}\ \gamma(x_i)\Big), \qquad |X| = n,\ x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3.$$
Invariance. A permutation \(\pi\) reorders the rows of \(X\); the entrywise max of a set does not depend on the order (the shared MLP is applied column by column): \(f(\pi X) = f(X)\). Why max rather than another invariant aggregator? The product \(\prod_i \gamma(x_i)\) is likewise permutation-invariant, but numerically disastrous (explodes toward 0 or \(\infty\), unstable multiplicative gradients); the mean is stable but dilutes local signals — a discriminative sub-region representing 1% of the cloud is drowned; the max is selective: it captures "at least one point exhibits the characteristic", which is exactly the building block of the universal approximation that follows. Universality (sketch). On a compact set \(K\), a continuous permutation-invariant function can be uniformly approximated by sums of "soft indicator functions" of balls \(g(x) \approx \sum_j c_j\,\mathbb{1}_{B(c_j, r_j)}(x)\); now \(\mathbb{1}_{B(c,r)}(x) \approx h_\varepsilon(\|x - c\|)\) with \(h_\varepsilon\) a steep sigmoid, and \(\max_{x_i}\big[1 - h_\varepsilon(\|x_i - c\|)\big]\) equals ~1 as soon as a single point falls in the ball — the union of balls is expressed via the max: PointNet approximates the functions of "membership of at least one point in a ball", the universal building block for compact sets (thm. 1 of the paper). ∎
PointNet++ and DGCNN — injecting the local PointNet only sees unions of regions (the global max ignores co-localization). Two answers:
  • PointNet++: (a) farthest point sampling: iteratively choose the point farthest from the already chosen centers (uniform coverage, greedy algorithm in \(O(n\,k)\)); (b) ball query: group the neighbors in a ball of fixed radius; (c) a local PointNet per group, then a hierarchy (progressive subsampling — exactly a spatial pooling); multi-scale through nested radii;
  • DGCNN: dynamic k-NN graph in feature space (EdgeConv): $$x_i' = \square_{j : (i,j) \in \mathrm{kNN}}\ h_\theta(x_i, x_j), \qquad h_\theta(x_i, x_j) = \mathrm{MLP}\big(x_i,\ x_j - x_i\big),$$ the max (or mean) over the edges; the graph is rebuilt at every layer — the neighbors drift apart in latent space: a growing receptive field (from local to global, like stacked convolutions).
Geodesic convolutions and the gauge problem A convolution filters a patch (geodesic neighborhood, anisotropic). But on a surface, there is no canonical frame in the tangent plane: where should "north" go? Every choice of gauge (local rotation of the frame) rotates the filter: an anisotropic filter is not well-defined. Three strategies:
  • measure the alignment with an extrinsic direction (the gradient, the normal) — fragile;
  • principal coordinates (eigenframes of the curvature tensor): stable but sign-ambiguous;
  • isotropic filtering (angularly invariant): one gives up anisotropy — this is the spectral answer (filters that are functions of \(\Delta\), cf. ChebNet of the Graph ML module) and... the DiffusionNet answer below.
DiffusionNet (Sharp, Attaiki, Crane, Ovsjanikov — TOG 2022): complete equations Replace convolution with learned diffusion. Each layer transforms features \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\):
  1. Learned diffusion: learn \(m\) scales \(t_1, \dots, t_m > 0\) (free parameters): $$H \leftarrow \mathrm{concat}\Big(H,\ \ \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\, e^{-t_j\Delta}H\Big) \ \ \text{(learned mixture of heats at all scales)};$$
  2. Per-point MLP: \(H \leftarrow \mathrm{MLP}(H)\) — a pointwise operator (no gauge!);
  3. Gradient of features: the gradient \(\nabla_M H\) (tangent vector per vertex, cotangent-Derivation 2.2 generalized) contains the direction of the variations — anisotropy reintroduced without a frame: concatenated to \(H\);
  4. back to (1) — 8–12 layers.
Properties: discretization-agnostic (only \(e^{-t\Delta}\) and \(\nabla\) are required — available on meshes and point clouds via heat estimation); isotropic (no gauge) + directional gradient; learned scales (no more fixed grid of \(t\)); SOTA on FAUST/SCAPE at the time, robust to re-meshings — the "discrete element" of the field.
  • Transformers on surfaces: global attention between points/vertices with intrinsic positional encodings (HKS, random geodesic distances) as the positional add-on: the GPS recipe of the Advanced GNN module (global + local) transplanted; quadratic cost → hierarchical subsampling;
  • Self-supervised learning: geometric pretexts — predict descriptors (HKS: "know thyself"), local orientation, reconstruct masked regions; contrastive pre-training between views/poses of the same shape (the spirit of methods like SurfContrast): LLM pre-training applied to surfaces;

7. Euclidean equivariance

Definition 7.1 — Equivariance and invariance For a group \(G\) acting on inputs and outputs: \(f\) is equivariant if $$f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x) \qquad \forall g \in G,$$ and invariant if \(f(g\cdot x) = f(x)\). For clouds/meshes, the natural group is \(E(n)\) (rotations \(R\), translations \(t\), reflections): a rotated molecule remains the same molecule — the network should neither relearn every orientation, nor confuse them.
EGNN (Satorras, Hoogeboom, Welling — ICML 2021): equations State: positions \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^n\), features \(h_i\). Layer: $$m_{ij} = \phi_e\big(\|x_i - x_j\|^2,\ \|h_i - h_j\|^2,\ h_i, h_j\big) \qquad \text{(message, functions of distances only)}$$ $$x_i' = x_i + \sum_{j \ne i} (x_i - x_j)\,\phi_x(m_{ij}) \qquad \text{(equivariant update)}$$ $$h_i' = \phi_h\big(h_i, \textstyle\sum_j m_{ij}\big) \qquad \text{(invariant update)}.$$
Invariance of the messages (proved). Let \(g = (R, t) \in E(n)\), \(g\cdot x = Rx + t\) (take \(R\) orthogonal): $$\|g\cdot x_i - g\cdot x_j\|^2 = \|R(x_i - x_j)\|^2 = (x_i - x_j)^\top R^\top R (x_i - x_j) = \|x_i - x_j\|^2,$$ since \(R^\top R = I\); the messages depend only on the mutual distances — invariant. Equivariance of the positions. For the layer: $$f(g\cdot x_i) = R x_i + t + \sum_j (Rx_i - Rx_j)\,\phi_x(m_{ij}) = R\Big[x_i + \sum_j (x_i - x_j)\phi_x(m_{ij})\Big] + t = g\cdot f(x_i),$$ the \(\phi_x(m_{ij})\) being unchanged (invariance of the messages): the layer intertwines the action of \(E(n)\). Recursion over the layers: the whole network is equivariant; aggregating it with an invariant sum yields an invariant classifier. ∎
  • SE(3)-transformers (sketch): for the continuous version, attention is built on equivariant fields (irreducible representations; invariant keys/queries, vector-valued values) — the machinery exceeds this course, but the principle stands: invariant similarities, transformed values;
  • Connection with the Advanced GNN module, ch. 8: EGNN is a GNN whose messages are constrained by geometry — the perfect example of "geometry first, network second"; molecule/protein applications of ch. 9; the generation of conformers by EGNN (positions = equivariant output) meets the diffusion of the Multimodal GenAI module (ch. 7);
  • Equivariance vs invariance: for classification, invariance suffices (max pooling) — but to generate or localize, one needs equivariance (to output positions consistent with the inputs); the same debate as "final aggregation vs messages" in GNN theory.

8. Research of the teaching team

Maks Ovsjanikov heads the Geometric Data Analysis group at LIX (École Polytechnique). The four publications below are not merely "references" for this course: they are the course — every chapter from 2 to 6 stems directly from them.

Functional Maps — SIGGRAPH 2012 (the founding paper) M. Ovsjanikov, M. Ben-Chen, J. Solomon, A. Butscher, L. Guibas, arXiv:1204.3967 — Best Paper Award. The story. Before 2012, shape correspondence was handled point to point: combinatorial matching of tens of thousands of vertices, fragile heuristics. The idea: represent the map as a linear operator on functions (ch. 4), which becomes a small matrix in spectral bases. Beyond chapter 4. The paper also introduces: the effective computation of the constraints, the inverse conversion (matrix → correspondence), and above all the demonstration that a few well-chosen constraints suffice (descriptors + commutation). Extensions (by the hundreds): dense coupling, partial maps, the shape Differences formulation (comparing how two shapes transport functions), and the ZoomOut refinement below. Positioning. It is one of the most influential papers of modern shape analysis; the framework is today the standard and the basis of learned methods (GeomFMaps).
HKS — Sun, Ovsjanikov, Guibas, SGP 2009 A Concise and Provably Informative Multi-Scale Signature. The genesis. The heat kernel \(h_t(x, y)\) (ch. 2) transports information between points; its diagonal \(h_t(x,x)\) — the heat that returns to its starting point — is a descriptor of x at every scale t. Contributions. (1) the multi-scale characterization (small t = local, large = global); (2) the proof of isometric invariance and stability (the proved sketch of ch. 3); (3) the analysis of the space of scales. Impact. The HKS and its WKS variant are the input descriptors of almost all correspondence pipelines (including functional maps); the scale-invariant version extends it to heterogeneous scans.
ZoomOut — SIGGRAPH Asia 2019 S. Melzi, J. Ren, E. Rodolà, A. Sharma, P. Wonka, M. Ovsjanikov, arXiv:1904.07865. Problem. The spectrum truncated to \(k\) eigenfunctions is a trade-off: small \(k\) = robust but coarse; large \(k\) = precise but noisy. Idea. The iterative spectral refinement of ch. 4: reconstruct a hard correspondence at resolution \(k\), reinject it as a coupling constraint at resolution \(k + \Delta\), and repeat. The regularization emerges from the upsampling rhythm; no additional data is required. Results. Substantial improvements on mediocre initial correspondences, near-zero cost (matrix products); it became the standard post-processor. Positioning. The "soft ↔ hard" loop is the geometric ancestor of iterative refinements; its spirit resurfaces in the local/global solvers of ch. 5.
DiffusionNet — TOG 2022 N. Sharp, S. Attaiki, K. Crane, M. Ovsjanikov, arXiv:2012.00888. Problem. All "CNN-like" architectures on surfaces depend on the discretization (mesh vs cloud, resolution) and run into the gauge problem (ch. 6). Idea. A minimal and geometrically honest layer: diffusion at learned scales + pointwise MLP + feature gradient — complete equations in ch. 6. Results. SOTA correspondence (FAUST, SCAPE, SHREC'07 non-rigid), demonstrated robustness to discretization changes, works on clouds without a mesh (diffusion is replayed via Monte-Carlo estimation of heat). Positioning. The prototype of "sober geometric deep learning": instead of adapting convolution to the surface, inject the physical operator (heat) as a learning building block — the convergence of chapters 2 (operators) and 6 (networks).
The common thread HKS (2009: eigenfunctions as descriptors) → Functional Maps (2012: the map as an operator) → ZoomOut (2019: spectral refinement) → DiffusionNet (2022: the operator inside the network): fifteen years of research that follow exactly the progression of this course — from the spectral to deep learning.

Solution 1 — Symmetry and constants Each edge weight \(w_{ij} = \frac12(\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij})\) is constructed symmetrically in \(i, j\) (the two opposite angles favor neither endpoint): \(W = W^\top\). And \(L\mathbf{1} = 0\) because the diagonal term of each row \(i\) is the opposite of the sum of the off-diagonal weights: \(-\sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij} + \sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij} = 0\). On a regular flat grid, the contributions of the neighbors cancel out and \(Lf = 0\) at interior points; at the boundary, the angle deficit gives a nonzero Laplacian — that is the boundary!
Solution 2 — Stiffness of a triangle Case \(i \ne j\): redo step 2 of derivation 2.2: \(\nabla\psi_i\) constant, orthogonal to the opposite side, with norm \(1/h_i\); the angle between \(\nabla\psi_i\) and \(\nabla\psi_j\) equals \(\pi - \theta_k\), hence \(\int_T\langle\nabla\psi_i,\nabla\psi_j\rangle dA = |T|\cdot\big(-\frac{\cos\theta_k}{h_ih_j}\big) = -\frac12\cot\theta_k\) (via \(h_i = 2|T|/\|e_{jk}\|\), \(h_j = 2|T|/\|e_{ik}\|\), \(|T| = \frac12\|e_{jk}\|\|e_{ik}\|\sin\theta_k\)). Case \(i = j\): \(\int_T\|\nabla\psi_i\|^2 dA = |T|/h_i^2 = \frac{\|e_{jk}\|}{2h_i} = \frac12(\cot\theta_j + \cot\theta_k)\), because the height from \(i\) splits the opposite side into segments \(h_i\cot\theta_j\) and \(h_i\cot\theta_k\) (an algebraic identity, also valid for obtuse angles via the signs of the cotangents).
Solution 3 — Gauss–Bonnet on the cube Each corner receives three angles of \(\pi/2\): deficit \(2\pi - 3\times\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\), total \(8\times\frac{\pi}{2} = 4\pi = 2\pi\chi\) with \(\chi = 2\). The vertices added by subdivision (face centers, edge midpoints) are flat — zero deficit — and the 8 corners keep their \(\frac{\pi}{2}\): the sum is unchanged. This illustrates that the right-hand side of Gauss–Bonnet is topological; it is a practical integrity test for a closed mesh: \(\sum_iK_iA_i = 2\pi\chi\) whatever the resolution.
Solution 5 — HKS invariance By theorem 2.5 (proved invariance), \(\phi_k^N \circ T\) is an eigenfunction of \(M\) for \(\lambda_k^N\); the spectra coincide after sorting and, if the eigenvalues are simple, \(\phi_k^N(Tx) = \pm\phi_k^M(x)\). Then $$\mathrm{HKS}_N(Tx,t) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k^N(Tx)^2 = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k^M(x)^2 = \mathrm{HKS}_M(x,t)$$ (the square kills the sign; for multiple eigenvalues: equality after a basis rotation within each eigenspace). On FAUST: at small \(t\), extremities (hands, head) = self-descriptive points; at large \(t\), convergence toward the constant component — hence the logarithmic grid in \(t\).
Solution 6 — The heat kernel solves the PDE Term-by-term derivative: \(\partial_t h_t(x,y) = -\sum_k\lambda_ke^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y)\) and \(-\Delta_yh_t(x,y)\) gives the same sum after applying \(\Delta\phi_k = -\lambda_k\phi_k\) to the second factor — equal. As \(t \to 0\), \(h_t(x,\cdot) \to \delta_x\) (an approximate identity family, orthonormal basis); by superposition, \(u(t) = e^{-t\Delta}u_0\) with each mode damped by \(e^{-\lambda_kt}\).
Solution 8 — Commutation and the structure of C The proof of 4.2 establishes \(T_F\phi_k^N = \pm\phi_{\sigma(k)}^M\) with \(\lambda_k^N = \lambda_{\sigma(k)}^M\): the matrix of \(T_F\) in eigenbases is therefore block-diagonal — \(C_{ij} \ne 0\) only if \(\lambda_i^M\) and \(\lambda_j^N\) coincide. For quasi-isometric surfaces, the equalities become proximities: the soft constraint \(\|C\Lambda_M - \Lambda_NC\|_F^2\) encodes this structure, locks the frequency correspondences, and blocks spectrally absurd matchings. Limitation: close or multiple eigenvalues leave undecided blocks — resolved by the descriptor constraints.
Solution 9 — PointNet aggregators Max and product are permutation-invariant (reordering a set changes neither its max nor its product); experiment confirms this on a permuted cloud. But: (a) the product of \(n\) factors below 1 collapses (underflow) and multiplies the gradients — unstable; (b) the mean is stable but dilutes: if 1% of the cloud carries the discriminative signal, it drowns it where the max captures it; (c) the max learns local "detectors" — the building block of universality (proof of 6.1).
