8. Conversational NPCs (LLMs) and anti-cheat

8.1. Conversational agents in games

LLMs open an unprecedented slot: NPCs with unscripted dialogue, with memory and personality. The founding experiment: Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) — a village of 25 LLM agents, each maintaining:

  • a memory stream (all observed events, timestamped, with recency/importance/relevance scores for retrieval);
  • a periodic reflection process (synthesizing memories into higher-level beliefs: "I am dedicating myself to my research");
  • a planner (a day of activities, revised on events).

Result: credible social behaviors (a birthday party organizes itself "on its own"), measured by human evaluation. Voyager (2023) demonstrates the "agent in an open world" variant (Minecraft): the LLM writes and stores reusable skills (macro code) — an ever-growing skill library. These architectures are detailed in the Reinforcement Learning module, chapter 7 (autonomous agents in the age of LLMs) and in the M2 Large Language Models module — we focus here on the game constraints.

8.2. Cheating: detect, limit, compensate

Three families, in increasing order of difficulty for the studio:

  1. information cheating (wallhack: seeing through walls): never send the client what it must not see — interest management (sending only "audible/visible" entities) is a networking and anti-cheat feature (and a bandwidth gain — see chapter 9);
  2. action cheating (aimbot, speedhack): server authority (the client proposes, the server decides) + verified physical bounds (max speed, fire rate, humanly plausible reaction time);
  3. statistical cheating (organized boosting, bot farming): detection by learning on game traces — reaction-time distributions, inhuman regularities, social graphs of collusion (see the Graph ML module for player graph analysis).
Example 8.1 — An aimbot test Human reaction times follow a log-normal law (median ~250 ms, and rarely < 150 ms); an aimbot produces near-zero or constant "reaction times". Over 1000 eliminations by one player: median 90 ms, standard deviation 8 ms, zero values above 150 ms — two orders of magnitude too "perfect". A simple test: compare the observed median to the human reference (rank test, see the Refresher in Statistics module); in production, combine several indicators and weight by social behavior to avoid false positives (a very good player is rare, not impossible — see the statistical lessons of the Machine Learning module on multiple testing).
Note — The assumed "cheating" AI: rubber-banding The reverse "cheating" — by the game itself — is a legitimate design tool: rubber-banding (opponents slow down/speed up according to their distance to the player), tension Director (chapter 4), adaptive difficulty via a player model. The rule of the art: the assistance must be invisible and perceived as symmetric — players accept being pushed, not being robbed; exercise 10 formalizes an assistance controller with hysteresis.
