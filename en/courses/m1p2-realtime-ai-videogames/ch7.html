7. Cooperative and multi-agent games

7.1. The structure of the problem

Moving from one to \(n\) agents changes the nature of the problem: each agent's environment contains the other agents — non-stationary as soon as they learn. Standard decomposition (formalized in the Reinforcement Learning module, chapter 7):

settinggame examplekey difficulty
fully cooperativeOvercooked, Hanabi, PvE co-opcredit assignment: who contributed?
purely competitivezero-sum games, duelsauto-curriculum (chapter 6)
mixedMOBA, battle royale, MMOtemporary alliances, non-stationarity
Definition 7.1 — CTDE: centralized training, decentralized execution The dominant pattern of cooperative multi-agent RL: during training, the value function (the critic) may see the states and actions of all agents; during execution, each policy (the actor) sees only its own observations. Since the game is deployed on the players' machines, decentralizing execution is not an option; centralizing learning, on the other hand, is free (the simulator knows everything).

7.2. Emergent cooperative behaviors

The spectacular reference: Emergent Tool Use in Multi-Agent Interaction (OpenAI, 2019) — hide-and-seek in a physical sandbox: six teams of PPO agents in self-play successively discover running, hiding, box barricades, then counters (ramps, block jumps), then counter-counters — an auto-curriculum spiral where each strategy triggers a new one. Lesson for game design: simple rules + competitive pressure suffice to generate an escalation of strategies — exactly what a designer seeks to elicit from players.

Other results to know: Hanabi (imperfect-information cooperative — the theory of mind challenge: reasoning about what partners know); asymmetric coordination games (a "operator" player and a "runner"); and, on the theory side: communication learning (discrete channels between agents — emergent protocols) and credit assignment (team value decomposition; QMIX and the value decomposition network family).

7.3. Designing collaborative actions

Example 7.2 — A "throw-and-catch" with three contracts A collaborative action = (1) synchronization: a common execution window (the thrower and the receiver must both be ready within ±0.2 s); (2) complementarity: distinct roles evaluated together (a successful pass requires a thrower and a call for the ball); (3) shared outcome: a single team reward (scoring), not two. In implementation terms: a common stochastic action proposed by one agent and ratified by the others (two-phase protocol: proposal — commitment), with a timed abort if a partner does not ratify — the determinism of chapter 1 guarantees that both machines see the same execution. It is the same contract, in symbolic form, as the coordination by Hungarian matching of chapters 2 and 4.
Note — Asynchronous games and human players An NPC cooperating with a human must handle: the skill asymmetry (adapting to the player's level — see the AI Director, chapter 4); legibility (signaling intention: animations, signals, combat "chatter"); and perceived reliability (a human forgives a weak ally, not an unpredictable one). These three requirements explain why industrial co-op games often remain BT-based (chapter 3) with RL layered on top — predictability first.
