5. Monte-Carlo Tree Search: deciding in a few ms

5.1. The bandit reminder: UCB1

Reminder (Reinforcement Learning module, chapter 6) — UCB1
A bandit with \(K\) arms, rewards in \([0,1]\); the UCB1 policy draws $$a_t = \arg\max_{a} \left[ \bar r_a + c\,\sqrt{\frac{2 \ln t}{n_a}} \right],$$ where \(\bar r_a\) is the average reward of arm \(a\), \(n_a\) its number of draws, \(t\) the total time. The first term exploits, the second explores the little-tried arms; UCB1 achieves logarithmic regret (Auer et al., 2002) — the theoretical basis of MCTS.

5.2. The MCTS loop

Definition 5.1 — Monte-Carlo Tree Search MCTS incrementally builds a game tree where each node stores \((N_n, W_n)\): visits and cumulative reward. Each iteration:
  1. Selection: from the root, descend by choosing at each node the child maximizing UCT;
  2. Expansion: at the first node not fully developed, add one child;
  3. Simulation (rollout): play at random (default policy) until the end of the game;
  4. Backpropagation: propagate the result (1 win / 0 loss, from the point of view of the player who just moved) up through all the traversed nodes.
The UCT formula (Upper Confidence bound applied to Trees): $$\mathrm{UCT}(n, a) = \frac{W_{n'}}{N_{n'}} + c\,\sqrt{\frac{\ln N_n}{N_{n'}}},$$ where \(n'\) is the child of \(n\) via action \(a\). After the iteration budget, play the most visited action at the root.
Example 5.2 — Tic-tac-toe: one iteration in numbers Root (X to move, 21 visits, 12 wins). Three candidate children:

center1290.750.7·√(3.04/12) = 0.351.10
corner740.570.7·√(3.04/7) = 0.461.03
edge210.500.7·√(3.04/2) = 0.861.36 → selected
The edge, little explored, is chosen (dominant exploration term); random rollout: X wins. Update: root → edge path: \(N\!: 2 \to 3\), \(W\!: 1 \to 2\) (for X's nodes); the opponent's nodes in the tree receive the complementary reward. One observes the key property: each node is a bandit whose arms are the moves, and the sample of each arm is obtained by playing out the game — hence "Monte-Carlo". On tic-tac-toe, ~2000 iterations suffice for optimal play; exercise 6 works it out by hand.
Proposition 5.3 — The exploration bonus decreases as \(\sqrt{\ln N / n}\) For a child node visited \(n\) times when the parent totals \(N\) visits, the exploration bonus decreases as \(n^{-1/2}\): a consistently disappointing child sees its candidacy melt away, but it remains re-examined logarithmically (the \(\ln N\) in the numerator keeps growing — never a definitive elimination, hence no irreversible regret if the initial estimate was misleading).
The bonus equals \(c\sqrt{\ln N / n}\). At fixed \(N\), it is proportional to \(n^{-1/2}\), strictly decreasing in \(n\) (derivative \(-\tfrac12 c \sqrt{\ln N}\, n^{-3/2} < 0\)). Between two children of means \(\mu_1 > \mu_2\), child 2 stops being chosen when \(\mu_2 + c\sqrt{\ln N/n_2} \leq \mu_1\) — but since \(\ln N \to \infty\), any frozen \(n_2\) eventually violates the inequality: the child is tried again. This is exactly the mechanism of UCB1's logarithmic regret (see RL module, chapter 6). ∎

5.3. MCTS in real time: engineering tricks

  • budget: one limits by iterations or by time (e.g. 5 ms per decision); the tree is reused at the next move (the sub-tree of the played move becomes the root);
  • random rollouts too weak: replace the default policy with a learned policy or a light heuristic (dominations, patterns); or truncate and evaluate with a value function — this is the bridge toward AlphaZero (chapter 6);
  • huge branching factor (continuous games, real time): progressive widening: allow only \(\lceil k\,N^\alpha \rceil\) children per node (α ≈ 0.5); in real time: "determinized" MCTS over sampled futures (imperfect-information games);
  • interruptibility: MCTS is "anytime" — quality grows with the budget, and an interrupted decision remains playable (take the most visited action): perfect for the tick of chapter 1.
Note — The legacy Coulom (2006) introduced MCTS (CIG); Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006) grafted UCT onto it; the application to Go and then the successes of AlphaGo (2016) — MCTS guided by policy and value networks — are covered in the Reinforcement Learning module, chapters 5–6; the reference synthesis is the survey by Browne et al. (2012). In industry: Total War (Creative Assembly) uses MCTS for strategic AI; many digital board games (Through the Ages, Armello…) make it their decision engine.
