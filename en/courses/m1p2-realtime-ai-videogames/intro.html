Introduction and overview

ModuleReal-time AI in Video Games: decisive & collaborative actions
CodeMSc&T Brochure
Team and volumeInstructors: master's team (brochure instructor to be confirmed) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · real-time AI for video games, from the game-loop tick to learned agents: navigation, behavior trees, MCTS, emergent collective behaviors and conversational NPCs — an ideal laboratory for decisions under a strict compute budget.
Status in the programP2 OptionOutside recommended track

This module studies the decisional AI of video games: how an agent perceives, decides and acts when the game loop grants it only a few milliseconds per frame. Navigation, behaviors, tree search, learned agents and conversational NPCs meet here under a strict compute budget — which makes video games a demanding playground for the master's decisional AI. It is also a domain where algorithmic decision-making and learning mix at every moment, in a system where everything shows on screen.

What you will be able to do

  • Break down a game loop and allocate a compute budget among perception, decision and action.
  • Choose a representation of space (grid, navmesh) and implement A* on it with a suitable heuristic.
  • Design agent behaviors with state machines, behavior trees and utility systems.
  • Program emergent collective decisions: steering, boids, formations.
  • Implement Monte-Carlo Tree Search to decide in a few milliseconds.
  • Articulate scripted agents and learned agents (RL, self-play) and balance their mix according to the budget.
  • Integrate an LLM into a conversational NPC, together with the associated anti-cheat challenges.
  • Profile a game AI and meet quantified performance budgets.

Where this course fits in the program

An option of period 2 of M1 (coming from the MSc&T brochure, outside the recommended track), for 36 h and 4.5 ECTS. The course builds on the algorithmics refresher of the Refresher in Computer Science module — graphs, priority queues, probabilities — reactivated in chapter 0; the Reinforcement Learning and Autonomous Agents module, taken in parallel in P2, sheds light on the chapter devoted to learned agents. Downstream, conversational NPCs prepare the question of agents in the Large Language Models module (M2), and the discipline of real-time budgets reappears in ML Ops and LLM Engineering Principles, where LLM inference faces the same latency constraints.

Course outline

  • Refresher: graphs, priority queues, probability — the algorithmics and probability groundwork put back in hand before any real-time use.
  • The game loop and the real-time budget — the architecture that governs everything else: a finite time per frame, to be split among the AI building blocks.
  • Navigation: grids, navmesh, A* — representing traversable space and planning correct, inexpensive paths in it.
  • Behaviors: state machines, behavior trees, utility — the major families of decision architectures for an individual NPC.
  • Collective decisions: steering, boids, formations — producing convincing group behaviors through simple local rules.
  • Monte-Carlo Tree Search: deciding in a few ms — stochastic tree search for perfect-information games, constrained by the budget.
  • Learned agents: RL and self-play in games — what reinforcement learning brings to game agents, and at what cost.
  • Cooperative and multi-agent games — coordinating several agents whose objectives intertwine, from cooperation to negotiation.
  • Conversational NPCs (LLMs) and anti-cheat — hooking a language model to a character, without opening the door to cheating.
  • Performance, profiling and budgets — measuring, locating bottlenecks and reporting back in milliseconds.
  • Exercises — targeted implementations for each building block, from A* to MCTS.
  • References — the sources underlying each chapter.

How to study this module

  • Start with the refresher chapter: A* and priority queues come back in almost all subsequent chapters.
  • Implement A* and then MCTS yourself at least once, without a library, before trying to optimize.
  • Measure everything in milliseconds: every building block must be visible in a profile, not by feel.
  • Set yourself a constant tick budget and fit every improvement within it — that is the spirit of the course.
  • Cross-reference with the reinforcement learning module of the same period: learned agents are its direct application.
  • Analyze the games you know as decisional systems: spotting behaviors and their compute limits in them is the best training.
The module in the LLGA master's program Video games condense the positioning of the master's program: algorithmic decision-making on graphs, reinforcement learning and conversational LLMs coexist within one constrained system. The skills practiced here — compute budgets, multi-agent coordination, LLM integration — transfer to gameplay programming, simulation and interactive systems careers, while the questions of agents and self-play open directly onto research.
