6.2. Self-play: building your own training curriculum

Definition 6.1 — Self-play and auto-curriculum The agent (or teams of agents) plays against itself; since the opponent improves along with it, the difficulty of the problem grows automatically — an auto-curriculum. Variants: symmetric self-play (two instances of the same policy), league (AlphaStar: "exploiter", "main" agents, frozen past agents as adversaries), population (OpenAI Five plays against past snapshots).

Three famous pitfalls: the exploit race (the agent discovers a pathological strategy that the opponent then counters — a cycle without progress); the naive self-play collapse (the agent adapts to itself and becomes vulnerable to novel styles); reward hacking (a poorly specified reward is optimized "to the letter" — the boat sailing in circles to harvest speed points: see chapter 7). Industrial recipes: sample opponents from a pool of past snapshots, mix optimization and exploitation (PFSP — prioritized fictitious self-play), and above all test against external bots.

6.3. Using RL in the games industry

  • playing against humans: competitive game bots (Dota, StarCraft — see the table); calibrated-difficulty challenges;
  • testing the game: RL agents exploring the content look for dead ends, infinite loops, off-map paths — far faster than human testers; several major studios run "quality assurance bot" pipelines;
  • balancing: simulating meta-games (strategy matchups) to detect dominations before release;
  • animation / motion: motor control (physics + RL) for procedural characters — a gateway toward the M2 module "Analysis and Deep Learning on Geometric Data" for animation;
  • imitation: learning human behaviors from player traces (behavioral cloning; learned rewards — inverse RL) for credible "human-level" NPCs without crushing domination.
Note — What games bring to RL (and vice versa) The game engine is the perfect simulator: fast, exact, resettable, risk-free — everything robotics lacks. This is why RL breakthroughs were almost all born in games; in return, industry imposes constraints that academic RL ignores: tiny inference times (small network; see the Advanced Deep Learning (P2) module, chapter 6), readable and "fair-play" behavior, absence of exploits (otherwise players find them within 48 h), and validation over thousands of games against diverse policies.
