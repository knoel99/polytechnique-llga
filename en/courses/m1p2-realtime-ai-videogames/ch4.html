4. Collective decisions: steering, boids, formations

4.1. Steering behaviors

Definition 4.1 — Steering (steering behavior) A steering behavior computes a steering force \(s = \mathrm{seek}(p, v, p_{\mathrm{target}}, v_{\mathrm{target}})\) — a desired acceleration — added to the velocity, typically bounded \(\|s\| \leq a_{\max}\) and smoothed (weighted blend of behaviors). Classic components (Reynolds, 1999): seek, flee, arrive (soft deceleration), wander, pursue (anticipate the target), avoid (obstacles).
Example 4.2 — Arrive: the right deceleration Pure seek toward a point gives a jittery orbit around the target (the velocity always points at it); arrive defines a desired velocity decreasing with distance: $$v_{\mathrm{des}} = \hat d \cdot v_{\max} \cdot \min\!\Big(1, \frac{\|d\|}{r_{\mathrm{arr}}}\Big), \qquad s = \frac{v_{\mathrm{des}} - v}{\tau},$$ with \(d = p_{\mathrm{target}} - p\), arrival radius \(r_{\mathrm{arr}}\) and response time \(\tau\) (e.g. 0.2 s): braking is anticipated instead of oscillating — the famous "overshooting" of amateur NPCs comes from the absence of this term.

4.2. Boids: three local rules, one global behavior

Reynolds' boids model (1987) simulates bird flocks with, per individual, three forces computed on its neighbors (perception radius \(r\), possibly anisotropic):

  1. Separation: \(s_{\mathrm{sep}} = -\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \frac{p_i - p_j}{\|p_i - p_j\|}\) — move away from neighbors that are too close (weighted by proximity);
  2. Alignment: \(s_{\mathrm{ali}} = \bar v_{\mathcal{N}(i)} - v_i\) — adopt the average velocity of the neighbors;
  3. Cohesion: \(s_{\mathrm{coh}} = \bar p_{\mathcal{N}(i)} - p_i\) — move toward the local center of mass.

The acceleration is a weighted blend of the three (plus seek toward a possible leader); no global variable exists: the flock is an emergent behavior. This result — "three local rules produce a coherent group" — is the gateway to collective AI: we program the individuals, we get the crowd. The same engine drives schools of fish, zombie hordes (to be mixed with the flow navigation of chapter 2), swarms of projectiles… The number of neighbors to consider is bounded (computational parsimony: neighborhood query by spatial partitioning — see chapter 9 and Refresher CS, chapter 8, for quadtrees).

4.3. Formations: slots and the virtual leader

Definition 4.3 — Slot-based formation A formation is a set of relative positions \(\{o_1, \dots, o_k\}\) in the formation's frame; the world position of slot \(j\) is $$p_j^{\mathrm{slot}} = p_{\mathrm{anchor}} + R(\theta)\, o_j,$$ where the anchor (a real leader or a virtual leader — an invisible point following the global path) provides position \(p_{\mathrm{anchor}}\) and orientation \(\theta\). With each member steering toward its slot, the formation moves credibly; slot assignment is a minimum-cost matching \(\sum_{i} \|p_i - p_{\sigma(i)}^{\mathrm{slot}}\|\) — the Hungarian algorithm, again (the same assignment problem as in the Computational Optimal Transport module, chapters 1–2, and in the Computer Vision (P2) module, chapter 4, for tracking).
Note — Coordinating without talking: the blackboard and beyond Three industrial coordination patterns: (1) blackboard — shared memory where agents publish/consume observations (an NPC "announces" the player's position); (2) assigned roles — a leader distributes (left flank, suppression, medic), often via the Hungarian matching; (3) emergence — boids/RVO: no negotiation. The AI Director of Left 4 Dead (Valve, 2008) illustrates a higher level: a global system regulates the intensity (enemy spawns, items, music) according to the players' measured tension — game design AI, not NPC AI.
