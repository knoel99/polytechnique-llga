Introduction and overview

No model, no LLM, no knowledge graph exists without data that is stored, queried, and made trustworthy: that is the role of database management systems. This course covers the complete chain — relational model, SQL, design, storage, indexes, transactions, crash recovery, optimization — then opens onto graph databases, vector databases, and the integration of heterogeneous web data. It is the "data" side of the LLGA master's program: the RAG of LLMs and the GraphRAG of GNNs literally rest on these systems, and this is a skill immediately monetizable in industry.

What you will be able to do

  • Express a query in both relational algebra and SQL, including aggregates and window functions.
  • Design a clean schema: functional dependencies, normal forms, and know when to denormalize wisely.
  • Explain how a query actually executes: pages, files, external sorting, B+ trees, hashing.
  • Reason about a transaction: ACID, isolation levels, concurrency, deadlocks.
  • Describe the crash recovery guarantee provided by the log (WAL) — what makes a database trustworthy.
  • Read an execution plan and optimize a slow query (indexes, rewriting, cost).
  • Choose between relational, NoSQL, graph database, and vector database depending on the workload and the data model.
  • Integrate heterogeneous web data and assess its trustworthiness, up to data fact-checking.

Official objectives and syllabus (2026-2027 catalog)

In the official catalog, the course unit Système de Gestion de Base de Données (X3A VE3) sets itself the objective of a "broad and in-depth presentation of the fundamental concepts in very large-scale data management", drawing on PostgreSQL to introduce declarative queries, query optimization, data modeling, concurrency control and crash recovery, with labs where students load and query a real database, including through a web application. This is exactly the arc followed by these materials, from the relational model to optimization — and our opening chapters (NoSQL, web data) extend this foundation toward the RAG and GraphRAG systems of the master's program. The sheet lists no prerequisites (not disclosed); the assessment combines a final written exam and three graded tests in small classes.

Where this course fits in the program

Elective of Year 1 · Period 1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS), outside the recommended track: the "data" choice of the period. Official prerequisites: not stated on the sheet; these materials lay down the Refresher in Computer Science module as their foundation, whose sets, relations and complexity analysis they constantly mobilize — external sorting and indexes are reasoned about in algorithmic cost.

Downstream, this module feeds three parts of the master's program. The Graph Machine and Deep Learning for Generative AI module builds on the graph databases seen in the "Beyond relational" chapter, up to GraphRAG architectures that combine GNNs and graph querying; the M2 module Large Language Models assumes the culture of vector databases and RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) laid down here; ML Ops and LLM Engineering Principles reuses transactions, reliability, and data pipeline integration for industrialization. The research of the CEDAR team (Inria) on knowledge graphs and fact-checking also announces concrete internship topics.

Course outline

  • Review: sets, relations, complexity: the mathematical and algorithmic foundation taken up from the Refresher in Computer Science.
  • The relational model and algebra: the theory behind SQL — operators, semantics, equivalences.
  • SQL: queries, aggregates, windowing: the most directly reusable skill of the master's program.
  • Design: dependencies and normal forms: designing schemas without redundancy or anomalies.
  • Storage: pages, files, external sorting: what happens under the query, when data overflows memory.
  • Indexes: B+ trees and hashing: fetching information without reading everything — the real performance gain.
  • Transactions and concurrency: ACID, isolation, locks — many users, one consistent database.
  • The log and crash recovery: why a well-designed database loses nothing, even on a crash.
  • Query optimization: execution plans, rewrites, costs — diagnosing and speeding up.
  • Beyond relational: NoSQL, graphs, vectors: the modern database families, and when each one wins — the pivotal chapter for GraphRAG and RAG.
  • Web data and heterogeneous integration: confronting multiple, inconsistent, sometimes false sources.
  • Research of the teaching team: the research of the CEDAR team (Inria) — knowledge graphs and data fact-checking.

How to study this module

  • Practice SQL from the first week on a real database (SQLite or PostgreSQL): the SQL chapter is a gesture, kept up by typing queries.
  • For each internal mechanism (index, log, external sorting), ask yourself "what happens if the data is a hundred times the memory?" — that is the question that structures the course.
  • Go back if needed to the complexity chapter of the Refresher in Computer Science module: B+ trees and optimization are reasoned about in big-O.
  • Build a small schema yourself, populate it, then deliberately degrade it (concurrency, wild deletions) to observe transactions and the log at work.
  • Pay particular attention to the "Beyond relational" chapter: it is the one that directly connects to Graph ML and to the M2 LLMs.
  • Do the optimization exercises on real execution plans (EXPLAIN): knowing how to read a plan is highly valued in internships and jobs alike.
A word from the teaching team Crossing LLMs and graphs — the identity of the LLGA master's program — requires knowing where and how data lives: relational databases, graph databases, vector databases. This module provides this command of the data foundation, shared by the engineers who deploy RAG systems and the researchers who make knowledge graphs speak.
