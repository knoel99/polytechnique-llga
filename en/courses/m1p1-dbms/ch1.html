1. The relational model and algebra

The relational model, proposed by E. F. Codd in 1970, rests on a radically simple idea: representing data as (mathematical) relations, that is, sets of tuples, and querying them with operations that are closed on these sets. The physical structure is invisible: this separation between the declarative and the physical is what made the model successful, and it is what will make the automatic optimization of chapter 8 possible.

Definition 1.1 — Schema, relation, instance A relation schema is a name and a list of attributes \(R(A_1\!: D_1, \dots, A_n\!: D_n)\), where \(D_i\) is a domain of values. An instance of \(R\) is a finite set \(r \subseteq D_1 \times \cdots \times D_n\) (no duplicates: a relation is a set). A database schema is a set of relation schemas. A key constraint \(K \subseteq \{A_1,\dots,A_n\}\) requires that two distinct tuples do not coincide on \(K\).
Example 1.2 — Running example database Three relations suffice for the whole chapter:
Student (id, name, major)Course (code, title, credits)
(1043, Anna, DS) · (1088, Bruno, AI) · (2101, Chloé, DS) (DB, Databases, 4) · (DL, Deep Learning, 5) · (OT, Optimal Transport, 3)
and the association relation Enrolled(id, code, year) with the tuples (1043, DB, 2025), (1043, DL, 2026), (1088, DB, 2025), (1088, OT, 2025), (2101, DL, 2025). The pair (id, code) is the key of Enrolled.
Definition 1.3 — Relational algebra Relational algebra is the smallest family of operations, all closed (an operation on relations returns a relation):
  • selection \(\sigma_p(R)\): tuples of \(R\) satisfying the predicate \(p\) (comparisons and logical connectives);
  • projection \(\pi_L(R)\): restriction to the attributes of \(L\), with duplicate elimination;
  • Cartesian product \(R \times S\), union \(R \cup S\) (compatible schemas), difference \(R - S\), renaming \(\rho_{A \to B}(R)\);
  • join \(R \Join_p S := \sigma_p(R \times S)\) and natural join \(R \Join S\) (equality of identically named attributes, then projection of duplicated columns);
  • aggregation \({}_G\gamma_{f(A)}(R)\): partition of \(R\) according to the attributes \(G\), computation of the group function \(f\) (\(\texttt{count}, \texttt{sum}, \texttt{avg}, \texttt{min}, \texttt{max}\)) — an extension of Codd's initial algebra, indispensable in practice.
Proposition 1.4 — Fundamental equivalence laws The following equivalences hold for all instances (verified by expanding the set-theoretic definitions):
  • \(\sigma_{p_1 \wedge p_2}(R) = \sigma_{p_1}(\sigma_{p_2}(R))\) and \(\sigma_{p_1}(\sigma_{p_2}(R)) = \sigma_{p_2}(\sigma_{p_1}(R))\) (commutativity);
  • if \(p\) only involves attributes of \(R\): \(\sigma_p(R \Join S) = \sigma_p(R) \Join S\) — this is the selection push-down law, the heart of the heuristics of ch. 8;
  • \(\pi_{L_1}(\pi_{L_2}(R)) = \pi_{L_1}(R)\) whenever \(L_1 \subseteq L_2\);
  • \(R \Join_p S = S \Join_p S'\) with symmetrized predicate (commutativity) and \((R \Join S) \Join T = R \Join (S \Join T)\) (associativity) — hence the freedom of choice of the join order, exponential but fruitful.
Proof of the push-down law (the others are in the same style). Let \(t\) be a tuple of \(R \Join S\) that satisfies \(p\). By definition \(t = (u, v)\) with \(u \in R\), \(v \in S\), and the join predicate satisfied; since \(p\) mentions only attributes of \(R\), \(p(t)\) depends only on \(u\), hence \(u \in \sigma_p(R)\) and \(t \in \sigma_p(R) \Join S\). Conversely the inclusion is immediate since \(\sigma_p(R) \subseteq R\). ∎
Example 1.5 — Step-by-step evaluation "Majors of the students enrolled in 2025 in a course of at least 4 credits": $$\pi_{\texttt{major}}\Big(\sigma_{\texttt{year}=2025}\big(\texttt{Enrolled}\big) \Join \pi_{\texttt{code}}\big(\sigma_{\texttt{credits}\geq 4}(\texttt{Course})\big) \Join \texttt{Student}\Big).$$ On Example 1.2: the courses kept are {DB, DL}; \(\sigma_{2025}\) keeps (1043, DB), (1088, DB), (1088, OT), (2101, DL); the join eliminates (1088, OT); after the join with Student and projection: {DS, AI, DS} → {DS, AI} by eliminating duplicates. The order of the operations above (the most restrictive first) is not the only possible one: starting with \(\texttt{Enrolled} \Join \texttt{Student}\) would be correct but more costly — the whole subject of ch. 8.
Definition 1.6 — Relational division The division \(R \div S\) (with the schema of \(S\) included in that of \(R\)) returns the tuples \(u\) over the remaining attributes such that \(\{u\} \times S \subseteq R\). It expresses the universal quantifier: "the \(x\) associated with all the \(y\) of \(S\)". It is programmed without a dedicated operator by the scheme \(\pi_X(R) - \pi_X\big((\pi_X(R) \times S) - R\big)\), where \(X\) are the attributes of \(R\) absent from \(S\).
Theorem 1.7 — Codd: algebra and relational calculus Relational algebra (with renaming) is equivalent to the safe tuple relational calculus: every query expressible in one is expressible in the other. This result (1972) establishes the relational completeness of SQL: everything that is computable in a finite, set-oriented way on relations is expressible in SQL. On the other hand, neither the basic algebra nor SQL can express the transitive closure of a graph (queries of the "ancestors" type), except by using the recursion of ch. 2 or specific extensions.
Sketch. For the calculus → algebra direction, one translates a safe formula \( \{x \mid \varphi(x)\} \) by structural induction: the positive use of \(\exists\) becomes a projection of a product (with renaming for the free variables), conjunction a join, negation a difference — safeness (domains remain finite) guarantees that these constructions terminate. The algebra → calculus direction is an immediate induction over the operators. The inexpressiveness of transitive closure is proved by bounding arguments (any non-recursive query "sees" a number of joins fixed in advance, hence a bounded diameter in the graph). ∎
Definition 1.8 — Entity-association (E-A) model and the move to relational Design starts at the conceptual level, before any relation schema: an entity-association model describes entities (students, courses), their attributes, and associations between them, each association carrying a cardinality (1-1, 1-N, N-M) — for example Student—Enrolled(year)—Course is an N-M association carrying the attribute year. The translation to the relational model is mechanical: each entity becomes a relation (its identifier becomes the key); a 1-N association is absorbed by a foreign key on the N side; an N-M association becomes a relation in its own right whose key is the union of the keys of the linked entities (exactly the Enrolled relation of Example 1.2). This E-A → relational passage is the first step of the normalized design of ch. 3.
Note — Set semantics vs. multiset semantics The algebra described here is set-based (no duplicates). Real engines manipulate multisets: without DISTINCT, duplicates survive, and the cardinalities of ch. 8 account for them. Duplicate elimination costs a sort or a hash — never free; that is why SQL does not do it by default.
