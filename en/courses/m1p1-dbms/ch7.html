7. The log and crash recovery

Durability requires writing to persistent storage before answering "commit", and atomicity requires being able to undo — even after a crash right in the middle. Both requirements are solved by one and the same structure: the log (write-ahead log, WAL), an ordered sequence of records describing every modification, written sequentially (hence fast, see ch. 4).

Definition 7.1 — WAL, LSN, STEAL/FORCE policy Each log record receives a sequence number (LSN). For a page write by a transaction: <LSN, T, page, row, before-image, after-image, previous-LSN-of-T>. The WAL rule: the log fragment of a modification must reach the disk before the modified page. Possible policies: STEAL (one may evict an uncommitted modified page from the buffer → one must know how to undo) vs NO-STEAL; FORCE (all pages are written at commit → no need to redo) vs NO-FORCE. Every serious DBMS chooses STEAL/NO-FORCE — the most performant, the one that requires both undo and redo.
Proposition 7.2 — Idempotence and ARIES recovery The recovery passes must be idempotent (replayed twice, same effect): this holds if redo/undo are driven by the LSNs stored in the pages. The ARIES algorithm (Mohan et al., 1992), deployed in all major DBMSs, recovers in three phases:
  • Analysis: reread the log since the last checkpoint to rebuild the table of in-progress transactions and the dirty page table;
  • Redo: replay, in LSN order, the after-image of every modification whose LSN exceeds the one recorded in the page (repeating history, including uncommitted transactions);
  • Undo: cancel, in reverse LSN order, the modifications of uncommitted transactions, writing compensation records (CLR) — if the machine crashes again during recovery, one resumes where one had stopped.
Example 7.3 — Rolling out a recovery by hand Log (LSN: 1-7) then crash:
LSNRecordEffect
1checkpointT1 active
2T1: update X (before 10 → after 15)page P1 dirty
3T2: update Y (before 5 → after 8)page P2 dirty
4T1: commitT1 completed
5T2: update X (before 15 → after 20)P1 dirty
6T3: update Z (before 3 → after 7)P3 dirty
7T2: … (no commit)crash here
ARIES recovery: analysis → T2 and T3 in progress, T1 committed, pages P1-P3 potentially dirty. Redo: LSN 2, 3, 5, 6 replayed (X=20, Y=8, Z=7). Undo (reverse order): LSN 6 canceled (Z=3, CLR written), then LSN 5 (X=15) and LSN 3 (Y=5); T2 and T3 are rolled back, T1 survives. Note that redo reapplies LSN 5 (a write of T2, uncommitted) before undoing it: this is the mark of ARIES — "repeat history" — and the reason for the CLR, which makes undo itself restartable.
Note — Checkpoints and the cost of durability A fuzzy checkpoint forces the buffer to disk and bars a record in the log, bounding the length of the redo; without it, recovery would reread the whole log. In exchange, every commit waits for at least one sequential log write (group commit: concurrent commits share a single flush): typically ~1 ms — the measurable price of ACID.
