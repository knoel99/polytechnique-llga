3. Design: dependencies and normal forms

A poorly designed database leads to the three classic anomalies: insertion (impossible to record one fact without another), deletion (losing one fact while deleting another), update (repeating the same fact in several places). Normalization theory, also due to Codd and developed by Bernstein, Rissanen and others, diagnoses and corrects these defects through functional dependencies.

Definition 3.1 — Functional dependency (FD) Let \(R\) be a schema and \(r\) an instance. A functional dependency \(X \to Y\) (where \(X, Y\) are sets of attributes) is satisfied by \(r\) if $$\forall\, t_1, t_2 \in r,\quad t_1[X] = t_2[X] \;\Rightarrow\; t_1[Y] = t_2[Y].$$ \(X\) "functionally determines" \(Y\): knowing \(X\) suffices (in \(r\)) to know \(Y\). A set \(F\) of FDs is a logical object: it is required to hold for all instances allowed by the schema. \(X \to Y\) with \(Y \subseteq X\) is called trivial.
Theorem 3.2 — Armstrong's axioms (validity and completeness) The following three rules are valid (every derivable FD is satisfied by every instance satisfying \(F\)):
  • reflexivity: if \(Y \subseteq X\) then \(X \to Y\);
  • augmentation: if \(X \to Y\) then \(XZ \to YZ\);
  • transitivity: if \(X \to Y\) and \(Y \to Z\) then \(X \to Z\).
They are moreover complete: every FD logically implied by \(F\) is derivable by these rules.
Validity (direct proofs). Reflexivity: if \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) and \(Y\subseteq X\), the restrictions to \(Y\) coincide. Augmentation: assume \(X\to Y\) and \(t_1[XZ]=t_2[XZ]\); then \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) hence \(t_1[Y]=t_2[Y]\), and \(t_1[Z]=t_2[Z]\) by hypothesis; therefore \(t_1[YZ]=t_2[YZ]\). Transitivity: \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) gives \(t_1[Y]=t_2[Y]\), which gives \(t_1[Z]=t_2[Z]\). Completeness: one shows that if \(X \to Y\) is not derivable, then the two-tuple instance built as follows violates it while satisfying \(F\): \(t_1\) carries distinct values per equivalence class of \(X^{+}\) (the closure of Def. 3.3), \(t_2\) equals \(t_1\) on \(X^{+}\) and differs elsewhere; every FD \(V \to W \in F\) is then preserved (either \(V \subseteq X^{+}\) and \(W\) stays there, or \(V \not\subseteq X^{+}\) and the tuples already differ on \(V\)), while \(X\to Y\) is violated. ∎
Definition 3.3 — Closure \(X^+\) and key The closure of \(X\) under \(F\) is \(X^{+} = \{A \mid X \to A \text{ derivable from } F \text{ by Armstrong}\}\); it is computed in \(O(|F|)\) passes (greedy algorithm: add the attributes determined by what we already have, up to a fixed point). \(K\) is a key if \(K^{+}\) contains all the attributes and no proper subset of \(K\) does; attributes belonging to some key are called prime.
Definition 3.4 — Normal forms Let \(R\) be equipped with \(F\), and \(K\) a key:
  • 1NF: all values are atomic (no lists or structured values in a cell);
  • 2NF: 1NF and no FD \(X \to Y\) with \(X\) a strict subset of a key and \(Y\) non-prime (no partial dependency on the key);
  • 3NF: 2NF and for every nontrivial FD \(X \to Y\): \(X\) is a superkey, or \(Y\) is prime;
  • BCNF (Boyce-Codd): for every nontrivial FD \(X \to Y\), \(X\) is a superkey — the purest condition, without the "prime attribute" escape hatch.
Theorem 3.5 — Lossless binary decomposition Let \(R\) be decomposed into \(\rho = (R_1, R_2)\), with \(R_1 \cup R_2 = R\). The decomposition is lossless (for every instance \(r\) satisfying \(F\): \(r = \pi_{R_1}(r) \Join \pi_{R_2}(r)\)) if and only if $$(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1 \quad\text{or}\quad (R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2 \;\in\; F^{+}.$$
Forward direction (the only one proved in full). Assume \(X = R_1 \cap R_2\) and \(X \to R_1\). The inclusion \(r \subseteq \pi_{R_1}(r) \Join \pi_{R_2}(r)\) always holds. Conversely, take \(t_1 \in \pi_{R_1}(r)\) and \(t_2 \in \pi_{R_2}(r)\) agreeing on \(X\), and let us show that their join \(s = t_1 \circ t_2\) is in \(r\). Let \(u_1, u_2 \in r\) be witnesses (\(u_1[R_1] = t_1\), \(u_2[R_2] = t_2\)). Since \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) agree on \(X\), we have \(u_1[X] = u_2[X]\); the FD \(X \to R_1\) forces \(u_1[R_1] = u_2[R_1]\), so \(u_2\) equals \(t_1\) on \(R_1\) and \(t_2\) on \(R_2\): \(u_2 = s\) after all, and \(s \in r\). (The converse is proved by exhibiting, when neither FD holds, a two-tuple instance whose join produces a spurious tuple.) ∎
Example 3.6 — Normalizing an orders table \(R(\text{order}, \text{item}, \text{qty}, \text{customer}, \text{city}, \text{unit\_price})\) with \(F = \{\)order·item \(\to\) qty,  order \(\to\) customer,  customer \(\to\) city,  item \(\to\) unit_price\(\}\). Key: \(\{\)order, item\(\}\) (its closure generates everything). Visible anomalies: a customer's city is repeated on each of their lines (update anomaly); an item with no order cannot exist (insertion). Normalization:
  • 2NF: order \(\to\) customer depends on a part of the key → extract Order(order, customer) and Line(order, item, qty); likewise item \(\to\) unit_price → Item(item, unit_price);
  • 3NF: in Order(order, customer, city), the FD customer \(\to\) city has a determinant that is not a superkey and city is not prime → extract Customer(customer, city);
  • result: Line(order, item, qty), Order(order, customer), Customer(customer, city), Item(item, unit_price) — each binary decomposition is validated by Theorem 3.5 (remember to check the intersection at each step).
Note — 3NF vs BCNF, and real life Every relation admits a 3NF decomposition that is lossless and dependency-preserving (Bernstein synthesis: minimal cover, one schema per determinant, plus a key); some relations, on the other hand, have no BCNF decomposition that is both lossless and dependency-preserving — classic case: \(R(\text{city}, \text{street}, \text{zip\_code})\) with \{city, street\} \(\to\) zip_code and zip_code \(\to\) city. 3NF is thus the minimal guarantee, BCNF the ideal when it preserves. In practice, warehouses deliberately denormalize (joins paid at write time rather than at read time): theory says what to pay, not whether to pay.
