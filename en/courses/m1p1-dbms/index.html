No model, no LLM, no knowledge graph exists without data that is stored, queried, and made trustworthy: that is the role of database management systems. This course covers the complete chain — relational model, SQL, design, storage, indexes, transactions, crash recovery, optimization — then opens onto graph databases, vector databases, and the integration of heterogeneous web data. It is the "data" side of the LLGA master's program: the RAG of LLMs and the GraphRAG of GNNs literally rest on these systems, and this is a skill immediately monetizable in industry.

  • Reason about a transaction: ACID, isolation levels, concurrency, deadlocks.

0. Review: sets, relations, complexity

L3 review material used
  • Sets and Cartesian products: \(A_1 \times \cdots \times A_k\), cardinality of the product \(|A_1 \times A_2| = |A_1|\cdot|A_2|\), subsets, differences; a relation in the database sense is a subset of a Cartesian product;
  • Binary relations: reflexivity, transitivity, transitive closure — the hidden tool behind the theory of functional dependencies (ch. 3) and recursive queries (ch. 2);
  • First-order logic: quantifiers \(\forall, \exists\), negation of quantifiers (\(\neg\forall x\, P \equiv \exists x\, \neg P\)) — SQL is nothing but an evaluable fragment of this logic;
  • Complexity: notations \(O, \Theta, \Omega\), logarithms (\(\log_b x = \ln x / \ln b\)), average complexity of hash tables (L3 algorithms review), sorting and merging;
  • A bit of probability: uniform draws, expectation, the "\(n\) balls into \(m\) bins" paradigm used in ch. 5 (hashing).
Proposition 0.1 — Sizes of products and projections Let \(R \subseteq A \times B\) be a finite binary relation. Then $$|\pi_A(R)| \leq |R| \leq |\pi_A(R)| \cdot |\pi_B(R)|,$$ where \(\pi_A\) denotes the projection (the set of values of \(A\) actually present). The left bound is attained as soon as one value of \(A\) appears in several pairs; the right one is attained for the complete product \(R = \pi_A(R) \times \pi_B(R)\). These bounds, trivial in appearance, are the foundation of all the cardinality estimates of chapter 8: an optimizer never knows the exact content of the tables, only statistics (number of distinct values, histograms), and must bracket the sizes of intermediate results in order to choose an execution plan.

1. The relational model and algebra

The relational model, proposed by E. F. Codd in 1970, rests on a radically simple idea: representing data as (mathematical) relations, that is, sets of tuples, and querying them with operations that are closed on these sets. The physical structure is invisible: this separation between the declarative and the physical is what made the model successful, and it is what will make the automatic optimization of chapter 8 possible.

Definition 1.1 — Schema, relation, instance A relation schema is a name and a list of attributes \(R(A_1\!: D_1, \dots, A_n\!: D_n)\), where \(D_i\) is a domain of values. An instance of \(R\) is a finite set \(r \subseteq D_1 \times \cdots \times D_n\) (no duplicates: a relation is a set). A database schema is a set of relation schemas. A key constraint \(K \subseteq \{A_1,\dots,A_n\}\) requires that two distinct tuples do not coincide on \(K\).
Example 1.2 — Running example database Three relations suffice for the whole chapter:
Student (id, name, major)Course (code, title, credits)
(1043, Anna, DS) · (1088, Bruno, AI) · (2101, Chloé, DS) (DB, Databases, 4) · (DL, Deep Learning, 5) · (OT, Optimal Transport, 3)
and the association relation Enrolled(id, code, year) with the tuples (1043, DB, 2025), (1043, DL, 2026), (1088, DB, 2025), (1088, OT, 2025), (2101, DL, 2025). The pair (id, code) is the key of Enrolled.
Definition 1.3 — Relational algebra Relational algebra is the smallest family of operations, all closed (an operation on relations returns a relation):
  • selection \(\sigma_p(R)\): tuples of \(R\) satisfying the predicate \(p\) (comparisons and logical connectives);
  • projection \(\pi_L(R)\): restriction to the attributes of \(L\), with duplicate elimination;
  • Cartesian product \(R \times S\), union \(R \cup S\) (compatible schemas), difference \(R - S\), renaming \(\rho_{A \to B}(R)\);
  • join \(R \Join_p S := \sigma_p(R \times S)\) and natural join \(R \Join S\) (equality of identically named attributes, then projection of duplicated columns);
  • aggregation \({}_G\gamma_{f(A)}(R)\): partition of \(R\) according to the attributes \(G\), computation of the group function \(f\) (\(\texttt{count}, \texttt{sum}, \texttt{avg}, \texttt{min}, \texttt{max}\)) — an extension of Codd's initial algebra, indispensable in practice.
Proposition 1.4 — Fundamental equivalence laws The following equivalences hold for all instances (verified by expanding the set-theoretic definitions):
  • \(\sigma_{p_1 \wedge p_2}(R) = \sigma_{p_1}(\sigma_{p_2}(R))\) and \(\sigma_{p_1}(\sigma_{p_2}(R)) = \sigma_{p_2}(\sigma_{p_1}(R))\) (commutativity);
  • if \(p\) only involves attributes of \(R\): \(\sigma_p(R \Join S) = \sigma_p(R) \Join S\) — this is the selection push-down law, the heart of the heuristics of ch. 8;
  • \(\pi_{L_1}(\pi_{L_2}(R)) = \pi_{L_1}(R)\) whenever \(L_1 \subseteq L_2\);
  • \(R \Join_p S = S \Join_p S'\) with symmetrized predicate (commutativity) and \((R \Join S) \Join T = R \Join (S \Join T)\) (associativity) — hence the freedom of choice of the join order, exponential but fruitful.
Proof of the push-down law (the others are in the same style). Let \(t\) be a tuple of \(R \Join S\) that satisfies \(p\). By definition \(t = (u, v)\) with \(u \in R\), \(v \in S\), and the join predicate satisfied; since \(p\) mentions only attributes of \(R\), \(p(t)\) depends only on \(u\), hence \(u \in \sigma_p(R)\) and \(t \in \sigma_p(R) \Join S\). Conversely the inclusion is immediate since \(\sigma_p(R) \subseteq R\). ∎
Example 1.5 — Step-by-step evaluation "Majors of the students enrolled in 2025 in a course of at least 4 credits": $$\pi_{\texttt{major}}\Big(\sigma_{\texttt{year}=2025}\big(\texttt{Enrolled}\big) \Join \pi_{\texttt{code}}\big(\sigma_{\texttt{credits}\geq 4}(\texttt{Course})\big) \Join \texttt{Student}\Big).$$ On Example 1.2: the courses kept are {DB, DL}; \(\sigma_{2025}\) keeps (1043, DB), (1088, DB), (1088, OT), (2101, DL); the join eliminates (1088, OT); after the join with Student and projection: {DS, AI, DS} → {DS, AI} by eliminating duplicates. The order of the operations above (the most restrictive first) is not the only possible one: starting with \(\texttt{Enrolled} \Join \texttt{Student}\) would be correct but more costly — the whole subject of ch. 8.
Definition 1.6 — Relational division The division \(R \div S\) (with the schema of \(S\) included in that of \(R\)) returns the tuples \(u\) over the remaining attributes such that \(\{u\} \times S \subseteq R\). It expresses the universal quantifier: "the \(x\) associated with all the \(y\) of \(S\)". It is programmed without a dedicated operator by the scheme \(\pi_X(R) - \pi_X\big((\pi_X(R) \times S) - R\big)\), where \(X\) are the attributes of \(R\) absent from \(S\).
Theorem 1.7 — Codd: algebra and relational calculus Relational algebra (with renaming) is equivalent to the safe tuple relational calculus: every query expressible in one is expressible in the other. This result (1972) establishes the relational completeness of SQL: everything that is computable in a finite, set-oriented way on relations is expressible in SQL. On the other hand, neither the basic algebra nor SQL can express the transitive closure of a graph (queries of the "ancestors" type), except by using the recursion of ch. 2 or specific extensions.
Sketch. For the calculus → algebra direction, one translates a safe formula \( \{x \mid \varphi(x)\} \) by structural induction: the positive use of \(\exists\) becomes a projection of a product (with renaming for the free variables), conjunction a join, negation a difference — safeness (domains remain finite) guarantees that these constructions terminate. The algebra → calculus direction is an immediate induction over the operators. The inexpressiveness of transitive closure is proved by bounding arguments (any non-recursive query "sees" a number of joins fixed in advance, hence a bounded diameter in the graph). ∎
Definition 1.8 — Entity-association (E-A) model and the move to relational Design starts at the conceptual level, before any relation schema: an entity-association model describes entities (students, courses), their attributes, and associations between them, each association carrying a cardinality (1-1, 1-N, N-M) — for example Student—Enrolled(year)—Course is an N-M association carrying the attribute year. The translation to the relational model is mechanical: each entity becomes a relation (its identifier becomes the key); a 1-N association is absorbed by a foreign key on the N side; an N-M association becomes a relation in its own right whose key is the union of the keys of the linked entities (exactly the Enrolled relation of Example 1.2). This E-A → relational passage is the first step of the normalized design of ch. 3.
Note — Set semantics vs. multiset semantics The algebra described here is set-based (no duplicates). Real engines manipulate multisets: without DISTINCT, duplicates survive, and the cardinalities of ch. 8 account for them. Duplicate elimination costs a sort or a hash — never free; that is why SQL does not do it by default.

2. SQL: queries, aggregates, windowing

SQL is a declarative language: one describes the result, not the algorithm. Its logical semantics is understood in three stages, which one must know in order to predict what a query returns — and which every optimizer rewrites in its own way (ch. 8): FROM produces the Cartesian product/joins, WHERE selects row by row, then GROUP BY + SELECT group, aggregate, and project.

Definition 2.1 — Semantics of the basic block The query SELECT [DISTINCT] L FROM R1, …, Rk WHERE p returns $$\pi_L\big(\sigma_p(R_1 \times \cdots \times R_k)\big)$$ (multiset if DISTINCT is absent, set otherwise). A subquery is a nested query; IN and EXISTS correspond to semi-joins: WHERE EXISTS (SELECT …) tests that the answer is non-empty, which makes it possible in particular to express differences with NOT EXISTS.
Proposition 2.2 — Translating quantifiers "The \(x\) such that \(\forall y \in S,\; \theta(x,y)\)" is translated in SQL by anti-join: … WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM S WHERE NOT θ). "There exists" is a simple EXISTS. This double negation is the direct legacy of the fact that SQL has no universal quantifier — and of Proposition 1.7: the division of ch. 1 does not exist as an operator, it is programmed.
Definition 2.3 — Aggregates and the HAVING clause A query with GROUP BY g partitions the selected rows according to the values of \(g\), and each group produces one row: the SELECT may mention only \(g\) and aggregates. The HAVING clause filters the groups (after aggregation), whereas WHERE filters the rows (before): $$\pi_{g,f}\big({}_g\gamma_f( \sigma_p(R))\big) \;=\; \texttt{SELECT g, f(a) … WHERE p GROUP BY g HAVING q(f)}.$$

  SELECT parent, child FROM parent
  UNION                                    -- UNION (without ALL) = deduplicated
  SELECT p.parent, a.d

-- semantics: fixed point; at each iteration, only the tuples

3. Design: dependencies and normal forms

A poorly designed database leads to the three classic anomalies: insertion (impossible to record one fact without another), deletion (losing one fact while deleting another), update (repeating the same fact in several places). Normalization theory, also due to Codd and developed by Bernstein, Rissanen and others, diagnoses and corrects these defects through functional dependencies.

Definition 3.1 — Functional dependency (FD) Let \(R\) be a schema and \(r\) an instance. A functional dependency \(X \to Y\) (where \(X, Y\) are sets of attributes) is satisfied by \(r\) if $$\forall\, t_1, t_2 \in r,\quad t_1[X] = t_2[X] \;\Rightarrow\; t_1[Y] = t_2[Y].$$ \(X\) "functionally determines" \(Y\): knowing \(X\) suffices (in \(r\)) to know \(Y\). A set \(F\) of FDs is a logical object: it is required to hold for all instances allowed by the schema. \(X \to Y\) with \(Y \subseteq X\) is called trivial.
Theorem 3.2 — Armstrong's axioms (validity and completeness) The following three rules are valid (every derivable FD is satisfied by every instance satisfying \(F\)):
  • reflexivity: if \(Y \subseteq X\) then \(X \to Y\);
  • augmentation: if \(X \to Y\) then \(XZ \to YZ\);
  • transitivity: if \(X \to Y\) and \(Y \to Z\) then \(X \to Z\).
They are moreover complete: every FD logically implied by \(F\) is derivable by these rules.
Validity (direct proofs). Reflexivity: if \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) and \(Y\subseteq X\), the restrictions to \(Y\) coincide. Augmentation: assume \(X\to Y\) and \(t_1[XZ]=t_2[XZ]\); then \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) hence \(t_1[Y]=t_2[Y]\), and \(t_1[Z]=t_2[Z]\) by hypothesis; therefore \(t_1[YZ]=t_2[YZ]\). Transitivity: \(t_1[X]=t_2[X]\) gives \(t_1[Y]=t_2[Y]\), which gives \(t_1[Z]=t_2[Z]\). Completeness: one shows that if \(X \to Y\) is not derivable, then the two-tuple instance built as follows violates it while satisfying \(F\): \(t_1\) carries distinct values per equivalence class of \(X^{+}\) (the closure of Def. 3.3), \(t_2\) equals \(t_1\) on \(X^{+}\) and differs elsewhere; every FD \(V \to W \in F\) is then preserved (either \(V \subseteq X^{+}\) and \(W\) stays there, or \(V \not\subseteq X^{+}\) and the tuples already differ on \(V\)), while \(X\to Y\) is violated. ∎
Definition 3.3 — Closure \(X^+\) and key The closure of \(X\) under \(F\) is \(X^{+} = \{A \mid X \to A \text{ derivable from } F \text{ by Armstrong}\}\); it is computed in \(O(|F|)\) passes (greedy algorithm: add the attributes determined by what we already have, up to a fixed point). \(K\) is a key if \(K^{+}\) contains all the attributes and no proper subset of \(K\) does; attributes belonging to some key are called prime.
Definition 3.4 — Normal forms Let \(R\) be equipped with \(F\), and \(K\) a key:
  • 1NF: all values are atomic (no lists or structured values in a cell);
  • 2NF: 1NF and no FD \(X \to Y\) with \(X\) a strict subset of a key and \(Y\) non-prime (no partial dependency on the key);
  • 3NF: 2NF and for every nontrivial FD \(X \to Y\): \(X\) is a superkey, or \(Y\) is prime;
  • BCNF (Boyce-Codd): for every nontrivial FD \(X \to Y\), \(X\) is a superkey — the purest condition, without the "prime attribute" escape hatch.
Theorem 3.5 — Lossless binary decomposition Let \(R\) be decomposed into \(\rho = (R_1, R_2)\), with \(R_1 \cup R_2 = R\). The decomposition is lossless (for every instance \(r\) satisfying \(F\): \(r = \pi_{R_1}(r) \Join \pi_{R_2}(r)\)) if and only if $$(R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_1 \quad\text{or}\quad (R_1 \cap R_2) \to R_2 \;\in\; F^{+}.$$
Forward direction (the only one proved in full). Assume \(X = R_1 \cap R_2\) and \(X \to R_1\). The inclusion \(r \subseteq \pi_{R_1}(r) \Join \pi_{R_2}(r)\) always holds. Conversely, take \(t_1 \in \pi_{R_1}(r)\) and \(t_2 \in \pi_{R_2}(r)\) agreeing on \(X\), and let us show that their join \(s = t_1 \circ t_2\) is in \(r\). Let \(u_1, u_2 \in r\) be witnesses (\(u_1[R_1] = t_1\), \(u_2[R_2] = t_2\)). Since \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) agree on \(X\), we have \(u_1[X] = u_2[X]\); the FD \(X \to R_1\) forces \(u_1[R_1] = u_2[R_1]\), so \(u_2\) equals \(t_1\) on \(R_1\) and \(t_2\) on \(R_2\): \(u_2 = s\) after all, and \(s \in r\). (The converse is proved by exhibiting, when neither FD holds, a two-tuple instance whose join produces a spurious tuple.) ∎
Example 3.6 — Normalizing an orders table \(R(\text{order}, \text{item}, \text{qty}, \text{customer}, \text{city}, \text{unit\_price})\) with \(F = \{\)order·item \(\to\) qty,  order \(\to\) customer,  customer \(\to\) city,  item \(\to\) unit_price\(\}\). Key: \(\{\)order, item\(\}\) (its closure generates everything). Visible anomalies: a customer's city is repeated on each of their lines (update anomaly); an item with no order cannot exist (insertion). Normalization:
  • 2NF: order \(\to\) customer depends on a part of the key → extract Order(order, customer) and Line(order, item, qty); likewise item \(\to\) unit_price → Item(item, unit_price);
  • 3NF: in Order(order, customer, city), the FD customer \(\to\) city has a determinant that is not a superkey and city is not prime → extract Customer(customer, city);
  • result: Line(order, item, qty), Order(order, customer), Customer(customer, city), Item(item, unit_price) — each binary decomposition is validated by Theorem 3.5 (remember to check the intersection at each step).
Note — 3NF vs BCNF, and real life Every relation admits a 3NF decomposition that is lossless and dependency-preserving (Bernstein synthesis: minimal cover, one schema per determinant, plus a key); some relations, on the other hand, have no BCNF decomposition that is both lossless and dependency-preserving — classic case: \(R(\text{city}, \text{street}, \text{zip\_code})\) with \{city, street\} \(\to\) zip_code and zip_code \(\to\) city. 3NF is thus the minimal guarantee, BCNF the ideal when it preserves. In practice, warehouses deliberately denormalize (joins paid at write time rather than at read time): theory says what to pay, not whether to pay.

Definition 4.1 — Page, record, organization The page (block) is the unit of input/output: one reads and writes whole pages (typically 4 to 16 KiB), never isolated bytes. Records are stored in pages with slots (slotted pages: a directory at the head of the page gives the address of each record, allowing insertions, deletions, and variable-length records). A file organization defines how pages are linked: heap (append at the end, sequential read), sorted (order maintained: costly insertion, binary search), or hashed (ch. 5).
Proposition 4.2 — Cost of a sequential scan For a file of \(N\) pages and a sequential throughput \(D\) pages/s, a full scan costs \(N/D\). Worked example: a 10 GB table in 8 KiB pages occupies \(N = 10^{10}/8192 \approx 1.22\times 10^6\) pages; on HDD at 150 MB/s: \(\approx 67\) s; on NVMe SSD at 3 GB/s: \(\approx 3.3\) s; in random accesses at 10,000 IOPS: 122 s — hence the DBMS obsession with sequential access and prefetching.
Theorem 4.3 — External merge sort With \(M\) pages of buffer memory available and \(N\) pages to sort, external merge sort produces sorted initial runs of \(M\) pages, then merges them with arity \(M-1\) (one output page). The number of passes equals $$p \;=\; 1 + \Big\lceil \log_{M-1} \lceil N/M \rceil \Big\rceil,$$ and the total I/O cost is \(2Np\) (each pass rereads and rewrites everything).
Pass 0 reads \(N\) pages and writes \(\lceil N/M\rceil\) sorted runs: cost \(2N\). At each merge pass, at most \(M-1\) runs are consumed into one run (fan-in \(M-1\) because one buffer page must be kept for output): the number of runs is divided by at least \(M-1\), hence \(\lceil\log_{M-1}\lceil N/M\rceil\rceil\) merges. Each merge rereads/rewrites the pages involved, hence \(2N\) per pass. ∎
Example 4.4 — Numbers \(N = 1000\) pages, \(M = 50\): initial runs: 20; \(\log_{49}(20) \to 1\) merge pass (fan-in 49 ≥ 20); \(p = 2\), cost \(2\times1000\times2 = 4000\) pages read+written. With \(N = 10^6\), \(M = 10^3\): 1000 runs, fan-in 999, \(\lceil\log_{999}(1001)\rceil = 2\), \(p=3\), cost \(6\times10^6\) — at 10,000 pages/s, about ten minutes: sorting remains nearly linear in practice thanks to the base (not the factor) of the logarithm.
Definition 4.5 — Buffer pool Between the disk and the processor, the DBMS manages a buffer pool: a RAM cache of page bytes, organized into frames the size of a page, with a page-to-frame mapping table. Every read goes through it: hit (the page is already there, RAM access) or miss (disk read then installation in a free frame). When no frame is free, a replacement policy chooses the victim — LRU as a first approximation, but real DBMSs prefer variants (LRU-K, clock with a reference bit, least-frequently-used): a massive sequential scan must not evict hot pages (hence the usage counter of scans). Writes: a modified page becomes dirty and is rewritten to disk only upon eviction — or earlier, by checkpointing (ch. 7). The parameter \(M\) of the sort and join algorithms (ch. 8) is precisely the share of the buffer pool allocated to the operator: the whole physics of queries plays out in this cache.
Note — Storing columns, not just rows Modern warehouses (Parquet, BigQuery, Snowflake, DuckDB) store tables by columns: reading 3 columns out of 100 reads only ~3% of the data, with adapted encodings (dictionary, RLE) and min/max statistics per block to skip chunks. The logical model remains relational: a perfect illustration of logical/physical independence — and reading columns fast is producing learning matrices fast (see the Machine Learning module, ch. 6).

5. Indexes: B+ trees and hashing

An index is a secondary structure that speeds up locating records without scanning them all. Two families dominate: B+ trees for range queries and sorting, hashing for exact equality. The DBMS maintains the index at every write: each additional index slows down insertions — there is no free index.

Definition 5.1 — B+ tree A B+ tree of order \(d\) is a balanced tree in which every node contains between \(d\) and \(2d\) keys (only the root may contain fewer, at least 1), every internal node has (number of keys + 1) children, all the data resides in the leaves, and the leaves are chained together (range scan without going back up). The keys of an internal node are separators: the subtree between two keys contains the records of the corresponding open interval. When a leaf splits, the median key is copied to the parent (it remains present in the right leaf); when an internal node splits, it is moved up to the parent.
Theorem 5.2 — Height of a B+ tree A B+ tree of order \(d\) containing \(n\) entries has height $$h \;\leq\; 2 + \log_{d+1}(n/2),$$ so that search, insertion, and deletion cost \(\Theta(\log_d n)\) logical I/Os — in practice 3 to 5 pages.
Count the minimal number of leaves of a tree of height \(h\) (root at level 1, leaves at level \(h\), \(h \geq 2\)). The root has at least 2 children; every internal non-root node has at least \(d+1\) children (because at least \(d\) keys); every leaf carries at least \(d\) entries. The number of leaves is therefore at least \(2\,(d+1)^{h-2}\), and each contains at least \(d\) entries: \(n \geq 2d\,(d+1)^{h-2} \geq 2\,(d+1)^{h-2}\) (since \(d \geq 1\)), hence \((d+1)^{h-2} \leq n/2\) and the announced bound. Search: one page per level; insertion: descent plus possible split cascades upward, one page per level; both are \(\Theta(h)\). ∎
Example 5.3 — A billion entries in 4 hops With 8 KiB pages, 16 B keys+pointers, a node holds up to ~512 keys: take \(d = 63\) (fan-out 64 to 128). For \(n = 10^9\) entries: \(h \leq 2 + \log_{64}(5\times10^8) = 2 + \frac{\ln(5\times10^8)}{\ln 64} = 2 + \frac{20.03}{4.16} \approx 6.8\), hence \(h \leq 6\) (and even 5 for a fan-out of 128). The upper levels fit in RAM: an equality search costs 1-2 page reads instead of the \(1.2\times10^6\) of the full scan (Prop. 4.2).
Example 5.4 — Cascading insertions (d = 1) Insert 10, 20, 5, 15, 30, 25, 35, 40, 45 into a B+ tree of order 1 (1 to 2 keys per node). After 10, 20: leaf [10, 20]. The 5 overflows: split into [5, 10] and [20], copy of 20 to the new root. The 15 refills the first leaf: [5,10,15] → [5,10] and [15], root [15, 20], three leaves. The 30 fills the right leaf [20, 30]. The 25 makes it overflow: [20,25] / [30], copy of 30 — the root [15,20,30] overflows in turn: internal split, key 20 is moved to the new root [20]. The 35, the 40 (split of [30,35,40] → [30,35] / [40], copy of 40) and the 45 lead to the final tree (height 3): 
                    [20]
             ┌────────┴────────┐
           [15]              [30, 40]
         ┌──┴──┐        ┌──────┼──────┐
      [5,10]  [15]   [20,25] [30,35] [40,45]
      ──────── leaves chained ────────▶
Each leaf has between 1 and 2 keys, the root at least 1: the structure stays at least half full after each deletion rebalanced by borrowing or merging — this is what guarantees Theorem 5.2 over time.
Definition 5.5 — Static and extendible hashing An on-disk hash table applies \(h(k) = k \bmod B\) to store the record with key \(k\) in bucket \(h(k)\): exact equality in 1 + (overflows) I/Os. Extendible hashing grows \(B\) dynamically: a directory of \(2^g\) pointers (global depth \(g\)) pointing to buckets, each of which uses only the \(p_i \leq g\) high-order bits of the hash (local depth); when a bucket overflows, double the directory if needed (\(g{+}{=}g{+}1\)) then split the bucket according to bit \(p_i{+}1\). Example: buckets of 2 slots, hashes 4 (100), 12 (1100) and 20 (10100) — all in 00; at \(g=2\) bucket 00 overflows, moves to depth 1 and splits into 0 (4, 20) and 1 (12) without doubling the directory. The load factor stays under control without global reorganization; Litwin's linear hashing achieves the same effect without a directory.

6. Transactions and concurrency

Several programs access the same data at the same time. The transaction is the unit of consistency: a sequence of read (r) and write (w) operations that must execute as if it were alone. The ACID acronym summarizes the contract: Atomicity (all or nothing), Consistency (constraints hold), Isolation (equivalence to a serial execution), Durability (a commit survives a crash — the subject of ch. 7).

Definition 6.1 — Schedules, conflicts, serializability A schedule \(H\) is a sequence of operations from several transactions, preserving the internal order of each. Two operations are in conflict if they belong to different transactions, bear on the same object, and at least one is a write. \(H\) is conflict-serializable (CSR) if it is equivalent by conflicts to a serial schedule; the serialization graph \(SG(H)\) has one vertex per transaction and an edge \(T_i \to T_j\) as soon as a conflicting pair of operations places an operation of \(T_i\) before one of \(T_j\).
Theorem 6.2 — The serialization graph criterion \(H\) is conflict-serializable if and only if \(SG(H)\) is acyclic; in that case, every topological order of \(SG(H)\) gives an equivalent serial execution.
If \(H\) is equivalent to the serial schedule \(T_{\sigma(1)} \dots T_{\sigma(k)}\), every edge \(T_i \to T_j\) of \(SG(H)\) must be respected by \(\sigma\) (conflict equivalence preserves the order of all conflicting pairs): the graph is embedded in a total order, hence acyclic. Conversely, if \(SG(H)\) is acyclic with topological order \(\sigma\), one checks that the associated serial schedule has the same conflicting pairs in the same order: for a given pair, the directed edge in \(SG(H)\) (which exists by definition) imposes the same relative order as \(\sigma\). ∎
Example 6.3 — Lost update Let \(H = r_1(X)\, r_2(X)\, w_1(X)\, w_2(X)\, c_1\, c_2\) with X a stock counter worth 100, \(T_1\) adding 50 and \(T_2\) subtracting 30. Serialized (\(T_1;T_2\)): \(100 \to 150 \to 120\). In \(H\): both reads see 100, \(w_1\) writes 150, then \(w_2\) overwrites with 70: the update of \(T_1\) is lost. Conflicts on X: \(r_1(X) < w_2(X)\) gives \(T_1 \to T_2\), and \(r_2(X) < w_1(X)\) gives \(T_2 \to T_1\): a cycle, \(H\) is not serializable — the graph had announced it even before reading the values.
Definition 6.4 — Two-phase locking (2PL) A transaction follows 2PL if it acquires all its locks before releasing a single one: growing phase (acquisitions), lock point, shrinking phase (releases). Strict 2PL (or rigorous) releases a write's locks only after commit — it avoids cascading aborts. Locks can be shared (read) or exclusive (write), with S-S the only compatibility.
Theorem 6.5 — Every legal 2PL execution is serializable If a schedule \(H\) respects 2PL (for each transaction, legality of the locks being ensured: no lock acquired while the conflicting lock has not been released), then \(SG(H)\) is acyclic, hence \(H\) is CSR.
Reason by contradiction and assume a cycle \(T_1 \to T_2 \to \cdots \to T_k \to T_1\). For each edge \(T_i \to T_j\) there exists an object \(q\) and a conflicting pair \(o_i[q] < o_j[q]\); since at least one of the two operations is a write, the locks of \(T_i\) and \(T_j\) on \(q\) are incompatible, hence do not overlap: legality imposes \(\text{unlock}_i(q) < \text{lock}_j(q)\). Let \(u_i\) be the instant when \(T_i\) releases its first lock (its lock point); then \(u_i \leq \text{unlock}_i(q) < \text{lock}_j(q) \leq u_j\), the last inequality coming from 2PL: \(T_j\) cannot acquire a lock after \(u_j\), so \(\text{lock}_j(q) \leq u_j\). Chaining along the cycle: \(u_1 < u_2 < \cdots < u_k < u_1\) — contradiction. ∎

7. The log and crash recovery

Durability requires writing to persistent storage before answering "commit", and atomicity requires being able to undo — even after a crash right in the middle. Both requirements are solved by one and the same structure: the log (write-ahead log, WAL), an ordered sequence of records describing every modification, written sequentially (hence fast, see ch. 4).

Definition 7.1 — WAL, LSN, STEAL/FORCE policy Each log record receives a sequence number (LSN). For a page write by a transaction: <LSN, T, page, row, before-image, after-image, previous-LSN-of-T>. The WAL rule: the log fragment of a modification must reach the disk before the modified page. Possible policies: STEAL (one may evict an uncommitted modified page from the buffer → one must know how to undo) vs NO-STEAL; FORCE (all pages are written at commit → no need to redo) vs NO-FORCE. Every serious DBMS chooses STEAL/NO-FORCE — the most performant, the one that requires both undo and redo.
Proposition 7.2 — Idempotence and ARIES recovery The recovery passes must be idempotent (replayed twice, same effect): this holds if redo/undo are driven by the LSNs stored in the pages. The ARIES algorithm (Mohan et al., 1992), deployed in all major DBMSs, recovers in three phases:
  • Analysis: reread the log since the last checkpoint to rebuild the table of in-progress transactions and the dirty page table;
  • Redo: replay, in LSN order, the after-image of every modification whose LSN exceeds the one recorded in the page (repeating history, including uncommitted transactions);
  • Undo: cancel, in reverse LSN order, the modifications of uncommitted transactions, writing compensation records (CLR) — if the machine crashes again during recovery, one resumes where one had stopped.

Note — Checkpoints and the cost of durability A fuzzy checkpoint forces the buffer to disk and bars a record in the log, bounding the length of the redo; without it, recovery would reread the whole log. In exchange, every commit waits for at least one sequential log write (group commit: concurrent commits share a single flush): typically ~1 ms — the measurable price of ACID.

8. Query optimization

SQL is declarative: the execution plan — the order of operations, the join algorithms, the indexes used — is chosen by the optimizer. The principle: rewrite the query as an algebraic tree, generate the equivalent plans via the laws of Proposition 1.4, estimate their cost with a model, keep the cheapest. It is a closed loop between theory (equivalences) and physics (the cost model of ch. 4).

Definition 8.1 — Cost model and selectivity For each table, the catalog stores \(n\) (number of tuples), \(b\) (pages), and for each column \(ND(A)\) (number of distinct values) or even a histogram. The selectivity of a predicate is the expected fraction of rows: \(sel(A = c) \approx 1/ND(A)\), \(sel(A < c)\) via histogram, \(sel(p_1 \wedge p_2) \approx sel(p_1)\cdot sel(p_2)\) (independence — a false but convenient assumption, corrected by modern engines via dynamic sampling). Estimated cardinality of a node = selectivity × cardinality of the child.
Definition 8.2 — Join algorithms Let \(R\) (\(B_R\) pages) and \(S\) (\(B_S\) pages), with a buffer of \(M\) pages:
  • nested loops (NLJ): for each page of \(R\), scan \(S\) — cost \(B_R + B_R\!\cdot\!B_S\); by blocks of \(M-1\) pages: \(B_R + \lceil B_R/(M-1)\rceil \cdot B_S\);
  • sort-merge join: sort \(R\) and \(S\) (Thm 4.3) then merge — cost \(2B_R(1+\lceil\log_{M-1}\lceil B_R/M\rceil\rceil) + 2B_S(\dots) + B_R + B_S\), zero if already sorted (B+ index);
  • hash join: partition both relations on the join key with the same hash (one pass if everything fits in \(M\)), then build a hash table per partition and probe — cost \(\approx 3(B_R+B_S)\), in memory if \(B_R\) or \(B_S\) fits in RAM.
Example 8.3 — Numbers: the winning plan cannot be guessed \(B_R = 1000\) pages, \(B_S = 500\), \(M = 50\). Simple nested loops: \(1000 + 1000\times500 = 501\,000\) pages. By blocks: \(1000 + \lceil 1000/49\rceil \times 500 = 1000 + 21\times500 = 11\,500\). Sort-merge: sorting \(R\) in 2 passes → 4000, sorting \(S\) in 2 passes → 2000, merge 1500: total 7500. Hash: \(3\times(1000+500) = 4500\) — and if \(S\) fits in memory, \(1000 + 500 = 1500\).

Proposition 8.4 — Safe heuristic rewrites Before any cost-based search, one applies the "always winning" rewrites: push down selections (Prop. 1.4), push down projections (reduce tuple width), reject Cartesian products outside useful joins, eliminate redundant subqueries, transform IN into semi-joins. Each selection pushed down divides the cardinality of the subtree involved: with \(sel = 1/100\), filtering \(10^6\) rows before rather than after a join gains a factor ~100 on that stream. Engines add costly but profitable rewrites: elimination of unnecessary joins (a join by foreign key without reading the columns of \(S\) and without multiplicity is superfluous), materialized CTEs, plan reuse (plan cache).
Definition 8.5 — Evaluating selection: access paths Even before the joins, each relational operator comes in several implementations that the optimizer compares:
  • selection: sequential scan (\(B_R\) pages, Def. 4.2), or B+ tree descent when an index covers the predicate (\(\lceil \log_F B_R \rceil + 1\) pages), or an index-only scan if the index contains all requested columns (zero table access);
  • projection and aggregation: tuple stream, with sorting or hashing to eliminate duplicates and group (sort-agg vs hash-agg, depending on available memory);
  • union, difference: merge of sorted streams or hash tables, in the same spirit as the joins of Def. 8.2.
The cost of each variant combines the selectivity model (Def. 8.1), the buffer pool (Def. 4.5), and the available indexes (ch. 5): reading an EXPLAIN is seeing this choice materialized node by node.
Note — Reading an EXPLAIN EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN (SQLite) or EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) (PostgreSQL) shows the chosen tree and the real cardinalities. Typical diagnosis: an estimate off by a factor of 1000 (stale statistics, unmodeled correlation) → nested loops where hashing would be needed. Reflex: compare estimated vs actual rows node by node, and trace back to the first divergence.

9. Beyond relational: NoSQL, graphs, vectors

The relational model dominates transactional workloads, but three developments of the 2000-2020 period seeded new models: horizontal scale (NoSQL), data as graphs (knowledge graphs), and search by similarity over vectors — the fuel of learning systems and of RAG (see the Large Language Models module, chapter 7).

Theorem 9.1 — CAP (Brewer, 2000; Gilbert-Lynch, 2002) In a distributed system subject to network failures (partitions), one cannot simultaneously guarantee strong consistency (C) and availability (A): one must choose, at least during the partition.
Sketch. Partition the network into two components \(\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2\). One client writes into \(\Gamma_1\), another reads from \(\Gamma_2\). If both operations succeed (A), the read cannot see the write: inconsistency (not C). To stay consistent, \(\Gamma_2\) must refuse or block (not A). ∎
NoSQL systems therefore claim AP with eventual consistency (BASE); so-called CP systems sacrifice availability during partitions. The trade-off is managed per query (readable / writable consistency levels, quorum \(r + w > n\)).

Definition 9.4 — Vector databases and ANN search A vector store indexes embeddings \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (produced by encoders — see the NLP module, ch. 3, and Multimodal GenAI, ch. 5) with a distance (cosine, \(L^2\)) and answers "the \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(q\)". Exact search costs \(O(nd)\): for \(n = 10^7\), \(d = 768\), that is \(7.7\times10^{10}\) multiplications per query — prohibitive in service. Approximate (ANN) search trades a bit of recall for orders of magnitude of speed:
  • IVF (inverted file): partition the space into \(n_{list}\) cells by k-means (see the Machine Learning module, ch. 6); explore only the \(n_{probe}\) closest cells. \(n_{list} = 4096\), \(n_{probe} = 16\) over \(10^7\) vectors: ~ \(39\,000\) distances instead of \(10^7\) — 250× less work, typical recall > 95%;
  • HNSW (Malkov-Yashunin): hierarchical "navigable small-world" graph. Layer 0 contains all the vectors, the upper layers increasingly sparse vertices (a node appears at layer \(\ell\) with probability \(p^{-\ell}\)). The search starts from the top layer's vertex and performs greedy routing (move to the neighbor that gets closest to \(q\)) while going down layer by layer; insertion links the new node to its \(M\) nearest neighbors found this way. The long links of the upper layers (chosen by a diversity heuristic) provide the nearly logarithmic shortcut; practical cost ~\(O(\log n)\), tunable recall, at the price of costly construction and an in-memory graph;
  • quantization (PQ): split \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) into \(m\) sub-vectors each quantized over \(2^8\) centroids; the distance becomes a sum of terms read from tables — memory divided by ~\(d\), at the price of an approximate distance.
Example 9.5 — Vector search in Python (full scan + IVF) 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import MiniBatchKMeans

rng  = np.random.default_rng(0)
X    = rng.normal(size=(100_000, 64)).astype("float32")   # vector "database"
q    = rng.normal(size=(64,)).astype("float32")

def normalize(A): return A / np.linalg.norm(A, axis=1, keepdims=True)
Xn, qn = normalize(X), q / np.linalg.norm(q)

# --- 1. exact search (brute force): O(n d)
d_all = Xn @ qn
top10_exact = set(np.argsort(d_all)[-10:])

# --- 2. IVF: nlist cells by k-means, we probe nprobe cells
nlist, nprobe = 256, 8
km = MiniBatchKMeans(n_clusters=nlist, n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(Xn)
cell_of_X = km.labels_
d_centro  = np.linalg.norm(km.cluster_centers_ - qn, axis=1)
probes    = np.argsort(d_centro)[:nprobe]
cand      = np.where(np.isin(cell_of_X, probes))[0]
top10_ivf = set(cand[np.argsort(Xn[cand] @ qn)[-10:]])

print(len(top10_exact & top10_ivf) / 10, "recall@10")     # typically 0.9-1.0
print(f"exact: {len(Xn)} distances; IVF: {len(cand) + nlist} distances")
Recall @k is traded against cost: it is the same trade-off as for B+ trees (exact, logarithmic, constrained by prefixes) — but with an assumed probabilistic tolerance. Relational DBMSs today integrate these indexes as extensions (pgvector; the Large Language Models module, ch. 7, shows the complete RAG pipeline that consumes them).

10. Web data and heterogeneous integration

The web is the largest database in the world — without schema, without keys, without constraints. The data feeding learning (and data journalism) arrives as HTML pages, semi-structured files (JSON, XML), tables, documents. This chapter addresses the specific problems: extract, match, merge.

Definition 10.1 — Semi-structured data and the web of data Data is semi-structured when its schema is implicit, carried by the data itself (JSON/XML trees, RDF graphs). The web of data (linked data) publishes RDF with shared IRIs, and also nestles into ordinary pages via embedded annotations (schema.org in JSON-LD): search engines derive "rich snippets" from them. Interoperability rests on ontologies (shared vocabularies: FOAF, Dublin Core, schema.org) and alignments (owl:sameAs between IRIs denoting the same real-world entity).
Definition 10.2 — Extraction, entity linking, entity resolution Extraction turns unstructured data into structured data: DOM wrappers for pages, table extraction (header row detection, column typing), information extraction by models — the NLP module (ch. 6: NER) is its engine. Entity linking attaches a text mention ("Macron", "E. Macron") to a reference entity (the president…); entity resolution (record linkage) decides whether two records describe the same thing. Typical signature: Jaccard similarity over sets of n-grams, \(J(A,B) = |A \cap B|\,/\,|A \cup B|\), e.g.: \(\{AL, LG, LGA\}\) vs \(\{LG, GA, GAX\}\): intersection {LG, GA} (2), union 4 → \(J = 0.5\).
Proposition 10.3 — Blocking: making quadratic matching practical Comparing \(n = 10^6\) records pairwise costs \(n(n-1)/2 \approx 5\times10^{11}\) comparisons. Blocking compares only the pairs sharing a blocking key (name prefix, soundex, locality). With 1000 balanced blocks of 1000 records: \(1000 \times \binom{1000}{2} \approx 5\times10^8\) comparisons — 1000× fewer. The risk is the false negative (two duplicates in different blocks); one therefore multiplies the blocking keys and divides the comparisons by filtering (pairs whose name similarity is below a threshold are discarded before the costly comparisons).
Definition 10.4 — Integration: matching, fusion, provenance Integrating \(k\) sources requires: (i) schema matching ("Prenom" ≈ "first_name", by similarity of names, types, values), (ii) fusion of conflicting values (voting, freshness, trust in the source), (iii) provenance — which source produced which value, in order to audit and correct. Architectures: ETL toward a schematized warehouse, or ELT (load raw, transform at query time); the data lake stores raw data, the lakehouse adds transactions and a catalog. These topics (and data-assisted fact-checking) are the home ground of the CEDAR team — the subject of the next chapter.
Note — Quality and trust The GIGO rule (garbage in, garbage out) is constant: data preparation consumes the majority of ML project time. Knowing where data is born and how it is merged is not "data plumbing": it is the condition for model reproducibility — and for the credibility of published figures.

  1. Algebra and SQL (solved). On the database of Example 1.2, express in algebra then in SQL: (a) the majors of the students enrolled in the DB course; (b) the students enrolled in all courses of at least 4 credits; (c) the courses with no enrollee. Solution. (a) \(\pi_{\texttt{major}}(\texttt{Student} \Join \sigma_{\texttt{code}=DB}(\texttt{Enrolled}))\) — SQL: join + WHERE. (b) division: \(\pi_{\texttt{id}}(\texttt{Enrolled}) \div \pi_{\texttt{code}}(\sigma_{\texttt{credits}\geq4}(\texttt{Course}))\) then join with Student; in SQL, NOT EXISTS (SELECT … FROM Course c WHERE credits >= 4 AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT … FROM Enrolled i WHERE i.id = e.id AND i.code = c.code)) — the double negation of Prop. 2.2, applied to the division of ch. 1. (c) \(\pi_{\texttt{code}}(\texttt{Course}) - \pi_{\texttt{code}}(\texttt{Enrolled})\), SQL: NOT EXISTS or LEFT JOIN … WHERE i.id IS NULL — the anti-join.
  2. Closure and 3NF (solved). \(R(A,B,C,D,E)\), \(F = \{A \to B,\; BC \to D,\; D \to E\}\). (a) Compute the keys. (b) Is the relation in 3NF? (c) Decompose into 3NF without loss and verify each step with Theorem 3.5. Solution. (a) \((AC)^{+}\): \(A\) gives \(B\), \(ABC\) gives \(D\), \(D\) gives \(E\) → everything: \(AC\) is a key (\(A^{+} = AB\), \(C^{+} = C\): neither \(A\) nor \(C\) alone). Prime attributes: \(A, C\). (b) \(D \to E\): \(D\) is not a superkey (\(D^{+} = DE\)) and \(E\) is not prime → not 3NF (2NF yes: no FD from a strict subset of the key). (c) By 3NF synthesis (minimal cover \(\{A\to B, BC\to D, D\to E\}\), one schema per determinant, plus a key): \((AB, BCD, DE, AC)\) — lossless and FD-preserving. The "by hand" decomposition \((ABCD, DE)\) then \((AB, ACD)\) preserves \(\{A\to B\}\) but loses \(BC \to D\) (check with Thm 3.5: the intersection \(B\) determines nothing): synthesis avoids precisely this pitfall.
  3. B+ by hand (solved). Insert in order 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 into a B+ tree of order \(d=1\); give the final tree and its height (same conventions as Example 5.4: when a leaf \([a,b,c]\) splits, the copied separator is \(c\), the first key of the right leaf). Solution. 2 then 4: leaf [2,4]. The 6 overflows [2,4,6] → [2,4] / [6], copy of 6: root [6]. The 8: leaf [6,8]. The 10: [6,8,10] → [6,8] / [10], copy of 10: root [6,10], leaves [2,4] [6,8] [10]. The 12: leaf [10,12]. The 14: [10,12,14] → [10,12] / [14], copy of 14: the root [6,10,14] overflows (3 keys); internal split: the median key 10 is moved to the new root, internal children [6] and [14]. The 14 already placed: final tree 
                  [10]
          ┌──────┴──────┐
        [ 6 ]         [ 14 ]
      ┌──┴──┐        ┌──┴──┐
    [2,4] [6,8]   [10,12] [14]
    Height: 3. Check: all leaves at the same level, 1 ≤ keys ≤ 2 everywhere — the invariants of Definition 5.1 hold at every step.
  4. Serializability (solved). Let \(H = r_1(X)\, r_2(X)\, w_1(X)\, w_2(X)\, r_1(Y)\, w_1(Y)\, c_1\, c_2\). Build \(SG(H)\); is \(H\) serializable? recoverable? What would 2PL do? Solution. Conflicts on X: \(r_1<w_2\) (T1→T2), \(r_2<w_1\) (T2→T1), \(w_1<w_2\) (T1→T2). On Y: T2 does not write Y. Graph: T1↔T2 → cycle → not serializable (Thm 6.2). Recoverable? T2 reads X (original value) then writes X after \(w_1\): T2 reads nothing written by T1 → yes, recoverable — but with a lost update. Under 2PL, \(T_1\) keeping its X lock until its lock point (after \(w_1(Y)\)), \(w_2(X)\) waits: the forbidden interleaving is simply impossible — Theorem 6.5 in action.
  5. External sort. \(N = 2\times10^5\) pages, \(M = 500\). Compute the initial runs, passes, total I/O cost; how long on a disk at 20,000 pages/s? Hint. 400 runs, fan-in 499 → \(\lceil\log_{499} 400\rceil = 1\), \(p = 2\), cost \(2Np = 8\times10^5\) pages → 40 s.
  6. Plan choice. Join \(R \Join S\) with \(B_R = 200\), \(B_S = 10\,000\), \(M = 52\), a selection on \(R\) of selectivity \(1/100\) that can be pushed before the join. Compare: sort-merge on \((R,S)\); hashing; and the order \(S \Join \sigma(R)\) in block nested loops. Hint. \(\sigma(R)\) fits in RAM (2 pages); NLJ with \(R\) filtered on the outside: \(2 + 1\times10\,000\) … the index changes everything: think "small relation in memory = outer loop".
  7. SQLite recursion. Write in recursive SQL: (a) the degree (distance) of separation of each pair of users of a network (table follow(a,b)); (b) the connected components of an undirected graph. Hint. (a) propagate the length by accumulating and take the MIN per pair (window or GROUP BY); (b) propagate the MIN of reached identifiers up to the fixed point.
  8. Vectors: sizing. \(10^7\) embeddings in \(d = 768\) (float32): memory size? Number of multiplications for an exact k-NN?
