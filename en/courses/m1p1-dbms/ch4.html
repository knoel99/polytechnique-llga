4. Storage: pages, files, external sorting

A DBMS lives on a hierarchy of memories with performance gaps of several orders of magnitude. The whole discipline of storage follows from this brute fact:

LevelOrder of magnitudeAccess
Registers / L1-L3 cache1-100 nswords
RAM~100 ns, a few tens of GBbytes
NVMe SSD~20-100 µs per access, TBpages (4-16 KiB)
SATA SSD / HDD~100 µs / ~5-10 ms, tens of TBblocks
Network / object store~10-100 ms, unlimitedobjects
Definition 4.1 — Page, record, organization The page (block) is the unit of input/output: one reads and writes whole pages (typically 4 to 16 KiB), never isolated bytes. Records are stored in pages with slots (slotted pages: a directory at the head of the page gives the address of each record, allowing insertions, deletions, and variable-length records). A file organization defines how pages are linked: heap (append at the end, sequential read), sorted (order maintained: costly insertion, binary search), or hashed (ch. 5).
Proposition 4.2 — Cost of a sequential scan For a file of \(N\) pages and a sequential throughput \(D\) pages/s, a full scan costs \(N/D\). Worked example: a 10 GB table in 8 KiB pages occupies \(N = 10^{10}/8192 \approx 1.22\times 10^6\) pages; on HDD at 150 MB/s: \(\approx 67\) s; on NVMe SSD at 3 GB/s: \(\approx 3.3\) s; in random accesses at 10,000 IOPS: 122 s — hence the DBMS obsession with sequential access and prefetching.
Theorem 4.3 — External merge sort With \(M\) pages of buffer memory available and \(N\) pages to sort, external merge sort produces sorted initial runs of \(M\) pages, then merges them with arity \(M-1\) (one output page). The number of passes equals $$p \;=\; 1 + \Big\lceil \log_{M-1} \lceil N/M \rceil \Big\rceil,$$ and the total I/O cost is \(2Np\) (each pass rereads and rewrites everything).
Pass 0 reads \(N\) pages and writes \(\lceil N/M\rceil\) sorted runs: cost \(2N\). At each merge pass, at most \(M-1\) runs are consumed into one run (fan-in \(M-1\) because one buffer page must be kept for output): the number of runs is divided by at least \(M-1\), hence \(\lceil\log_{M-1}\lceil N/M\rceil\rceil\) merges. Each merge rereads/rewrites the pages involved, hence \(2N\) per pass. ∎
Example 4.4 — Numbers \(N = 1000\) pages, \(M = 50\): initial runs: 20; \(\log_{49}(20) \to 1\) merge pass (fan-in 49 ≥ 20); \(p = 2\), cost \(2\times1000\times2 = 4000\) pages read+written. With \(N = 10^6\), \(M = 10^3\): 1000 runs, fan-in 999, \(\lceil\log_{999}(1001)\rceil = 2\), \(p=3\), cost \(6\times10^6\) — at 10,000 pages/s, about ten minutes: sorting remains nearly linear in practice thanks to the base (not the factor) of the logarithm.
Definition 4.5 — Buffer pool Between the disk and the processor, the DBMS manages a buffer pool: a RAM cache of page bytes, organized into frames the size of a page, with a page-to-frame mapping table. Every read goes through it: hit (the page is already there, RAM access) or miss (disk read then installation in a free frame). When no frame is free, a replacement policy chooses the victim — LRU as a first approximation, but real DBMSs prefer variants (LRU-K, clock with a reference bit, least-frequently-used): a massive sequential scan must not evict hot pages (hence the usage counter of scans). Writes: a modified page becomes dirty and is rewritten to disk only upon eviction — or earlier, by checkpointing (ch. 7). The parameter \(M\) of the sort and join algorithms (ch. 8) is precisely the share of the buffer pool allocated to the operator: the whole physics of queries plays out in this cache.
Note — Storing columns, not just rows Modern warehouses (Parquet, BigQuery, Snowflake, DuckDB) store tables by columns: reading 3 columns out of 100 reads only ~3% of the data, with adapted encodings (dictionary, RLE) and min/max statistics per block to skip chunks. The logical model remains relational: a perfect illustration of logical/physical independence — and reading columns fast is producing learning matrices fast (see the Machine Learning module, ch. 6).
