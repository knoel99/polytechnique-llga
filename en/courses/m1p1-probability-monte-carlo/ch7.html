7. Importance sampling

Definition 7.1 — Importance sampling estimator To estimate \(\theta = \int h(x) f(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_f[h(X)]\), draw \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) i.i.d. from a density \(g\) (the proposal) with \(g > 0\) everywhere \(hf \neq 0\), and weight by \(w(y) = f(y)/g(y)\): $$\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)\, h(Y_i), \qquad \mathbb{E}_g[\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}}] = \theta \ \text{(unbiased)}.$$ Exact variance: \(\mathrm{Var}_g(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}}) = \frac1n\big(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2h^2] - \theta^2\big) = \frac1n\big(\int \tfrac{h^2 f^2}{g} - \theta^2\big)\), minimal (zero variance) for \(g^\star \propto |h| f\) — the optimal proposal concentrates the draws where the target and the function of interest meet. An indispensable variant: the self-normalized estimator \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i h(Y_i)}{\sum_i w_i}\), usable when \(f\) is known only up to a constant (the Bayesian case), with bias of order \(\mathcal{O}(1/n)\).
Proposition 7.2 — Weight degeneracy and effective sample size (proved) Set \(\mathrm{cv}^2 = \mathrm{Var}_g(w)/(\mathbb{E}_g[w])^2\) (squared coefficient of variation of the weights; here \(\mathbb{E}_g[w] = 1\)). The effective sample size is $$\mathrm{ESS} = \frac{n}{1 + \mathrm{cv}^2}, \qquad \mathrm{cv}^2 = \chi^2(f \,\|\, g) = \int \frac{(f-g)^2}{g},$$ where \(\chi^2(f\|g)\) is the chi-squared divergence. In particular \(\mathrm{ESS} \leq n\), with equality if and only if \(f = g\), and \(\mathrm{ESS}/n \to 0\) as soon as \(f\) and \(g\) differ substantially.
\(\mathrm{Var}(\bar w) = \mathrm{cv}^2/n\) where \(\bar w\) is the empirical mean of the weights. For the \(\chi^2\) form: \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2] = \int f^2/g\) and \((\mathbb{E}_g w)^2 = 1\), hence \(\mathrm{cv}^2 = \int \frac{f^2}{g} - 1 = \int \frac{(f-g)^2}{g}\) (expand the square and use \(\int f = \int g = 1\)).∎
Gaussian case (exact computation): \(f = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(g = \mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) gives \(w(y) = e^{-\mu y + \mu^2/2}\) then \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2] = e^{\mu^2}\), hence $$\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-\mu^2} \qquad (\mu = 2:\ 1.8\%\ ;\ \mu = 3:\ 0.012\%\ ;\ \mu = 4:\ 1.1\times10^{-7}).$$ In dimension \(d\) with a per-coordinate mismatch \(\mu\), the effect multiplies: \(\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-d\mu^2}\) — the degeneracy of importance sampling is exponential in dimension, where only an adapted proposal (sequential adaptation, particle methods — SMC) survives.
Example 7.3 — Tail probability \(p = \mathbb{P}(Z > 4) = 3.17\times10^{-5}\) (numbers)
  • Crude MC: expected number of "hits" \(np\); for a 10% relative error one needs \(n \approx \frac{1-p}{p \times 0.01} \approx 3.2\times10^6\) draws;
  • IS, exponential tilt \(g = \mathcal{N}(4,1)\): exact computation \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2 \mathbf{1}_{Z>4}] = e^{16}\,\Phi(-8) \approx 5.9\times10^{-9}\), i.e. a per-draw standard deviation \(\approx 7\times10^{-5}\) versus \(p \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-5}\) for the estimator: relative error ~\(2.2/\sqrt n\) — 500 draws suffice where three million were needed;
  • Optimal IS: sample directly in the tail (\(g = f\) conditioned on \(Z>4\), by rejection): constant weight \(w = 1/p\), zero variance — a literal illustration of \(g^\star \propto |h| f\).
Moral: IS turns a rare-event problem into the sampling of typical events — a central technique in reliability, finance (VaR), and Bayesian inference (sequential methods). 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)

n = 200_000
p_exact = 1 - stats.norm.cdf(4.0)               # 3.167e-5

mc = rng.standard_normal(n) > 4.0               # crude Monte Carlo
g = rng.normal(4.0, 1.0, n)                     # exponential tilt
w = np.exp(-4.0*g + 8.0)                        # f/g, N(0,1)/N(4,1)
isw = w * (g > 4.0)

for name, est in [("crude", mc.mean()), ("IS", isw.mean())]:
    print(f"{name:5s} {est:.3e}  (exact {p_exact:.3e})")

ess = n / (1.0 + w.var(ddof=1))                 # ESS on the overall weights
print(f"ESS = {ess:.1f} / {n}")                 # weights highly degenerate outside the tail
Usage diagnostics Always report \(\mathrm{ESS}\) and the histogram of the weights: a few overwhelming weights mean the estimator "rests" on a handful of draws (unstable variance). Remedies: weight truncation, self-normalization, or moving to an adaptive method (SMC); in high dimension, prefer MCMC (ch. 8) or Langevin (ch. 9). ESS will reappear as a central diagnostic of MCMC chains in chapter 8.
