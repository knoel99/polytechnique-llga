How do we compute an intractable expectation, sample from a complex distribution, or generate plausible data? This course builds the modern probabilistic answer: from measure theory to Markov chains, then to Monte Carlo methods, MCMC, and Langevin dynamics. Behind state-of-the-art generative models — diffusion, score-based models — hide exactly these objects: samplers, Markov processes, stochastic dynamics. This is the module that gives the LLGA master's program its mathematical depth on the randomness side.

What you will be able to do

  • Handle expectation and integration in the measure-theoretic sense (Tonelli-Fubini, conditional expectation) without loss of rigor.
  • State the convergence results (LLN, CLT, delta method) and use them to build confidence intervals by simulation.
  • Analyze a Markov chain: stationarity, reversibility, ergodicity, speed of convergence to the stationary distribution.
  • Implement the basic sampling methods: inverse, rejection, Box-Muller.
  • Build a Monte Carlo estimator and make it efficient: variance reduction, importance sampling.
  • Implement MCMC algorithms — Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs — with the diagnostics that certify convergence, and use simulated annealing.
  • Connect Langevin dynamics to generative models: sample a high-dimensional density by following a stochastic process.

0. Review: measure, random variables, convergence

L3 review material used
  • Probability spaces: sigma-algebra, probability measure, random variables, pushforward distribution — the vocabulary of the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 1; if this vocabulary is shaky, now is the time to consolidate it;
  • Standard distributions: binomial, Poisson, exponential, Gaussian, gamma — densities, expectations, variances (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 3); independence and product measures;
  • Elementary inequalities: Markov, Chebyshev; "workhorse" laws of large numbers and central limit theorem (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 4);
  • Real analysis: numerical series, monotone convergence, power series (expansions of \(e^x\), \(\cos\), \(\sin\)), integration by parts, multivariate differential calculus (Jacobian).
Definition 0.1 — Sigma-algebra, measure, probability space A measurable space is a set \(\Omega\) equipped with a sigma-algebra \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega\) (it contains \(\Omega\) and is stable under complement and countable union). A probability \(\mathbb{P}\) is a measure on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) with total mass 1: \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1\), countably additive. A random variable (r.v.) is a measurable map \(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) (the preimage of a Borel set lies in \(\mathcal{F}\)); its distribution is the pushforward measure \(\mathbb{P}_X = \mathbb{P} \circ X^{-1}\). This viewpoint — the distribution as a pushforward — is the one that generalizes painlessly to the function spaces of machine learning.
Proposition 0.2 — Markov and Chebyshev (proved) Let \(X\) be a real r.v.
  • Markov: if \(X \geq 0\) and \(a > 0\), \(\mathbb{P}(X \geq a) \leq \mathbb{E}[X]/a\);
  • Chebyshev: if \(\mathrm{Var}(X) < \infty\) and \(t > 0\), $$\mathbb{P}\big(|X - \mathbb{E}X| \geq t\big) \leq \frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{t^2}.$$
Markov: \(\mathbb{E}[X] \geq \mathbb{E}[X\,\mathbf{1}_{X \geq a}] \geq a\,\mathbb{P}(X \geq a)\). Chebyshev: apply Markov to \((X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\) at level \(t^2\).∎
Why this module All of probabilistic machine learning — VAEs, diffusion, Bayesian inference, stochastic training — rests on three operations: computing expectations (integration), conditioning (conditional expectation), and sampling (simulation, Monte Carlo, MCMC). This module builds these three pillars under minimal assumptions, then connects them to modern generative algorithms (see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3, for the diffusion part). It completes the "recipe" treatment of the Machine Learning module, chapter 6 (EM), by providing the foundations.

1. Measure and integration: expectation, Tonelli-Fubini

Definition 1.1 — Construction of the Lebesgue integral Let \(X \geq 0\) be measurable on \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\). We set $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sup\Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, \mathbb{P}(A_i)\;:\; 0 \leq \textstyle\sum_i a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i} \leq X,\; n \geq 1 \Big\} \in [0,\infty],$$ where the \(a_i \geq 0\) and \(A_i \in \mathcal{F}\): we approximate \(X\) from below by simple functions. For \(X\) of arbitrary sign, \(X = X^+ - X^-\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^+] - \mathbb{E}[X^-]\) if both terms are finite (we then say \(X\) is integrable, written \(X \in L^1\)). The variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2]\).
Theorem 1.2 — Monotone convergence, linearity, monotonicity
  • Monotone convergence (Beppo-Levi): if \(0 \leq X_n \uparrow X\) (increasing, pointwise) then \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \uparrow \mathbb{E}[X]\), even if the limit is \(+\infty\) — it is the tool that makes every limit/integral swap legitimate in the nonnegative case;
  • Linearity: if \(X,Y \in L^1\), \(\mathbb{E}[aX+bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\) — with no independence assumption;
  • Monotonicity and domination: \(X \leq Y\) implies \(\mathbb{E}[X] \leq \mathbb{E}[Y]\); if \(|X| \leq Y\) with \(Y \in L^1\) then \(X \in L^1\).
Theorem 1.3 — Tonelli and Fubini Let \((X_1,\mathcal{F}_1,\mu_1)\) and \((X_2,\mathcal{F}_2,\mu_2)\) be two \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces, and \(f : X_1 \times X_2 \to \mathbb{R}\) measurable.
  • Tonelli (nonnegative functions): if \(f \geq 0\), $$\int_{X_1 \times X_2} f\,d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) = \int_{X_1}\Big(\int_{X_2} f(x_1,x_2)\,d\mu_2\Big) d\mu_1 = \int_{X_2}\Big(\int_{X_1} f(x_1,x_2)\,d\mu_1\Big) d\mu_2,$$ and the three quantities may equal \(+\infty\);
  • Fubini (integrability): if \(\iint |f|\,d(\mu_1\otimes\mu_2) < \infty\), the three integrals above coincide and are finite.
In probability: for independent \(X, Y\) and measurable \(g \geq 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[g(X,Y)] = \iint g(x,y)\,d\mathbb{P}_X(x)\,d\mathbb{P}_Y(y)\) — the variables are "decoupled". Classic counterexample: \(f(x,y) = \tfrac{xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\) on \([-1,1]^2 \setminus \{0\}\): the two iterated integrals equal \(\pm\pi/4\) — the integrability assumption is not decorative.
Proposition 1.4 — Expectation of a mixture (worked example) Let \(X\) be drawn from the mixture \(0.3\,\mathcal{N}(-1,\,0.5^2) + 0.7\,\mathcal{N}(2,\,1^2)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.3 \times (-1) + 0.7 \times 2 = 1.1\) and $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid J)] + \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X \mid J]) = \big(0.3 \times 0.25 + 0.7 \times 1\big) + 0.3 \times 0.7 \times 3^2 = 0.775 + 1.89 = 2.665,$$ where \(J\) is the component index (the law of total variance is proved in chapter 3). Gaussian mixtures serve as the running example of the whole module: they are also the target distributions of generative models (see the Machine Learning module, chapter 6, and the Deep Learning module, chapter 7).
Example 1.5 — The Gaussian integral, via Fubini and a change to polar coordinates $$I^2 = \int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy \overset{\text{polar}}{=} \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \times \frac12 = \pi,$$ hence \(I = \sqrt{\pi}\) and \(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-u^2/2}du = \sqrt{2\pi}\): the normalization constant of the Gaussian is not magic. The Jacobian \(dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta\) — the factor \(r\) that "saves" the integral — is the same one that will appear in the proof of Box-Muller (Prop. 5.4). Numerical check: \(\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 2.5066\).
What to remember from this chapter The expectation is a Lebesgue integral: to swap it with another integral (expectation, sum, limit), it suffices to have either positivity (Tonelli, Beppo-Levi) or an \(L^1\) domination (Fubini, dominated convergence). All the convergence proofs of the following chapters amount to checking one of these two assumptions.

2. Convergence: LLN, CLT, delta method

Definition 2.1 — Modes of convergence Let \((X_n)\) be a sequence of r.v. and \(X\) a real r.v.
  • In probability: \(\forall \varepsilon > 0,\; \mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \to 0\);
  • Almost surely: \(\mathbb{P}(\{\omega : X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega)\}) = 1\);
  • In \(L^p\): \(\mathbb{E}|X_n - X|^p \to 0\);
  • In distribution: \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_n)] \to \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\) for every bounded continuous \(f\) — the only mode where \(X_n\) and \(X\) may live on different spaces.
Classic implications: \(L^p\) and a.s. imply in probability; in probability implies in distribution; no other general implication (counterexample: the dyadic "sliding window" \(X_n = \mathbf{1}_{[j/2^k,\,(j+1)/2^k]}\) with \(n = 2^k + j\) on \([0,1]\) equipped with Lebesgue measure converges in probability to 0 but takes the value 1 infinitely often — no almost-sure convergence).
Theorem 2.2 — (Weak) law of large numbers (proved) Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be i.i.d. and square-integrable, with expectation \(\mu\). Then \(\bar{X}_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \to \mu\) in probability (and in \(L^2\)).
Linearity (Prop. 1.2): \(\mathbb{E}[\bar X_n] = \mu\). Independence and Fubini (Thm 1.3): \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\). Chebyshev (Prop. 0.2): \(\mathbb{P}(|\bar X_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \leq \sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) \to 0\), and \(\mathbb{E}|\bar X_n - \mu|^2 = \sigma^2/n \to 0\).∎
The strong version (almost sure, under the sole assumption \(\mathbb{E}|X_1| < \infty\)) requires the Borel-Cantelli lemma and Kolmogorov's maximal inequality: admitted here, it justifies the frequentist interpretation of probabilities and the convergence of ergodic chains (ch. 4 and 8).
Theorem 2.3 — Central limit theorem (proved via characteristic functions) Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be i.i.d., \(\mathbb{E}X_1 = \mu\), \(0 < \sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(X_1) < \infty\). Then $$\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\big(\bar X_n - \mu\big) \;\xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{\text{law}}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$
1. Reduction: assume \(\mu = 0\) (replace \(X_i\) by \(X_i - \mu\)). Set \(\varphi(u) = \mathbb{E}[e^{iuX_1}]\), continuous at 0 with \(\varphi(0)=1\). 2. Taylor of \(\varphi\): since \(\mathbb{E}|X_1|^2 < \infty\), we may differentiate twice under the expectation (domination): \(\varphi(u) = 1 - \tfrac{\sigma^2 u^2}{2} + u^2\,\varepsilon(u)\) with \(\varepsilon(u) \to 0\). Setting \(\psi = \log \varphi\) (defined near 0) and using \(\log(1+z) = z + o(z)\): $$\psi(u) = -\frac{\sigma^2 u^2}{2} + u^2\,\tilde\varepsilon(u), \qquad \tilde\varepsilon(u) \to 0.$$ 3. Product: by independence, the characteristic function of \(S_n = \sqrt{n}\,\bar X_n/\sigma\) equals \(\varphi_n(t) = \varphi(t/(\sigma\sqrt n))^n\), hence $$\log \varphi_n(t) = n\,\psi\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big) = n\Big(-\frac{t^2}{2n} + \frac{t^2}{n}\tilde\varepsilon\big(t/(\sigma\sqrt n)\big)\Big) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} -\frac{t^2}{2}.$$ 4. Conclusion: \(\varphi_n(t) \to e^{-t^2/2}\), which is the characteristic function of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); Lévy's continuity theorem (a pointwise limit of characteristic functions continuous at 0 is a characteristic function, and convergence of characteristic functions is equivalent to convergence in distribution — admitted) concludes the proof.∎
Theorem 2.4 — Delta method (proved) If \(\sqrt{n}(T_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{\text{law}} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) and \(g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is \(\mathcal{C}^1\) in a neighborhood of \(\theta\) with \(g'(\theta) \neq 0\), then $$\sqrt{n}\big(g(T_n) - g(\theta)\big) \xrightarrow{\text{law}} \mathcal{N}\big(0,\; \sigma^2\, g'(\theta)^2\big).$$
Taylor with controlled remainder: \(g(T_n) = g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(T_n - \theta) + R_n\) with \(|R_n| \leq \tfrac12 \sup_{|u - \theta| \leq \delta}|g''(u)|\,(T_n-\theta)^2 = o(|T_n - \theta|)\) on the event \(|T_n - \theta| \leq \delta\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt n\): \(\sqrt n R_n = \sqrt n\, o(|T_n - \theta|) = o(1) \cdot \sqrt n |T_n - \theta|\). Now \(\sqrt n (T_n - \theta)\) converges in distribution, hence is tight (\(\mathcal{O}_\mathbb{P}(1)\)), and \(\mathbb{1}_{|T_n-\theta| > \delta} \to 0\) in probability: the remainder is negligible and \(\sqrt n (g(T_n) - g(\theta)) = g'(\theta)\,\sqrt n (T_n-\theta) + o_\mathbb{P}(1)\). Conclude with Slutsky's lemma.∎
Example 2.5 — Intervals and variance stabilization (numbers)
  • A poll with \(n = 1000\) on a proportion \(p\) has standard error \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n} \leq \sqrt{1/(4n)} \approx 1.58\,\%\): the "margin of error ±3 points" quoted by polling institutes is a 95% interval (CLT + \(\Phi^{-1}(0.975) = 1.96\)).
  • For \(T_n = \bar X_n\) and \(g = \log\), the limiting variance becomes \(\sigma^2/\theta^2\): it no longer depends on the parameter — this is the principle of variance-stabilizing transformations, used before the computer age to make variances homoscedastic.
  • For the empirical correlation coefficient \(r\) of a Gaussian pair, Fisher's transformation \(g(r) = \tfrac12\log\frac{1+r}{1-r}\) gives a limiting Gaussian distribution with variance \(1/(n-3)\) — a classic example of the delta method in dimension 2 (with the gradient).

3. Conditional expectation

Definition 3.1 — General conditional expectation Let \(X \in L^1\) and \(\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) be a sub-sigma-algebra. The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) is the unique (up to a null set) r.v. \(Y\) such that: (i) \(Y\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable; (ii) for every \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\int_A Y\,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}\). If \(\mathcal{G} = \sigma(Z)\), we write \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z]\). Hilbertian interpretation (for \(X \in L^2\)): it is the orthogonal projection of \(X\) onto the closed subspace of square-integrable \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable r.v. — the best least-squares approximation of \(X\) by a function of the available information \(\mathcal{G}\). All of regression is right there (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 7, and the Machine Learning module, chapter 2).
Proposition 3.2 — Tower property (proved) For every sub-sigma-algebra \(\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\) and \(X \in L^1\): $$\mathbb{E}\big[\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \,\big|\, \mathcal{H}\,\big] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \quad \text{a.s.}$$
Set \(Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]\). Candidate for the identity: \(Y\) itself. (i) \(Y\) is \(\mathcal{H}\)-measurable, hence a fortiori a candidate for the conditional expectation of \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) given \(\mathcal{H}\) (the outer operator conditions on \(\mathcal{H}\), the smaller one). (ii) For every \(A \in \mathcal{H}\): since \(A \in \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\), the definition of \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) (applied to \(A\)) gives \(\int_A \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}\), and the definition of \(Y\) gives \(\int_A Y\,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}\). Hence \(\int_A \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \mid \mathcal{H}]\,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A \mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\,d\mathbb{P} = \int_A Y\,d\mathbb{P}\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{H}\): by uniqueness in Definition 3.1, the identity is proved.∎
Geometrically: projecting onto \(L^2(\mathcal{G})\) and then onto \(L^2(\mathcal{H})\) is the same as projecting directly onto \(L^2(\mathcal{H})\).
Proposition 3.3 — Formula sheet (some points admitted)
  • Linearity, monotonicity, convexity (Jensen): \(\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]) \leq \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}]\) for convex \(\varphi\) (proof by convex duality, admitted);
  • Taking out of the sigma-algebra: if \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and bounded, \(\mathbb{E}[ZX \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\); independence: if \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]\);
  • Gaussian case: if \((X,Y)\) is Gaussian, \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = \mathbb{E}X + \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\,\mathrm{Var}(Y)^{-1} (Y - \mathbb{E}Y)\): the projection is affine — this is exactly Kalman-Bucy filtering and kriging (see the Machine Learning module, chapter 4, on kernels).
Proposition 3.4 — Total variance (proved) For \(X \in L^2\) and any sub-sigma-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\): $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid \mathcal{G})\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\big).$$
\(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid \mathcal{G}]]\) (tower with \(\mathcal{H} = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\)) \(= \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid \mathcal{G}) + \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]^2]\). On the other hand \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]]\). Combining: \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2 = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X\mid\mathcal{G})] + \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2\), which is the announced sum.∎
Example 3.5 — Conditioning reduces variance (full computation) Let \(U, V\) be i.i.d. \(\mathcal{U}[0,1]\) and \(X = U + V\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid U] = U + \tfrac12\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X \mid U) = \tfrac{1}{12}\), and Prop. 3.4 recovers \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \tfrac{1}{12} + \tfrac14 = \tfrac16\). Monte Carlo application (ch. 6): the conditioned estimator \(U + \tfrac12\) has variance \(1/4 < 1/6\), the same expectation, and less randomness — this is the principle of conditioning-based control variates. Likewise for estimating \(\theta = \mathbb{P}(X \in A)\): \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(X \in A \mid \mathcal{G})] = \theta\) with a systematically smaller variance (the total variance decomposes into "noise eliminated by conditioning" plus a remainder).

4. Markov chains: stationarity, reversibility, ergodicity

Definition 4.1 — Markov kernel, Markov chain A Markov kernel on a measurable space \((E,\mathcal{E})\) is a map \(P : E \times \mathcal{E} \to [0,1]\) such that \(P(x,\cdot)\) is a probability for every \(x\) and \(P(\cdot,A)\) is measurable for every \(A\). A sequence \((X_k)_{k \geq 0}\) of r.v. is a Markov chain with kernel \(P\) if \(\mathbb{P}(X_{k+1} \in A \mid X_0, \dots, X_k) = P(X_k, A)\) (memory reduces to the present). The \(n\)-step distribution is \(P^n(x,A)\) (composition of kernels, Fubini for kernels — the analogue of matrix multiplication in finite dimension).
Definition 4.2 — Invariance, reversibility, irreducibility, periodicity
  • \(\pi\) is invariant if \(\pi P = \pi\), i.e. \(\int \pi(dx) P(x,A) = \pi(A)\) for every \(A\);
  • \(P\) is \(\pi\)-reversible if \(\pi(dx)P(x,dy) = \pi(dy)P(y,dx)\) (detailed balance); reversibility implies invariance (symmetry of the flow), the converse is false;
  • \(P\) is \(\pi\)-irreducible if for every \(x\) and every \(A\) with \(\pi(A) > 0\), there exists \(n\) (depending on \(x,A\)) such that \(P^n(x,A) > 0\): everything can be reached;
  • the period of \(x\) is the gcd of the \(n\) such that \(P^n(x,\{x\}) > 0\) (in a countable space); \(P\) is aperiodic if all periods equal 1.
Theorem 4.3 — Convergence and ergodicity (statement) Let \(P\) be \(\pi\)-reversible with \(\pi\) a probability, \(\pi\)-irreducible and aperiodic. Then:
  • \(\pi\) is the unique invariant distribution;
  • convergence to equilibrium: \(\|\lambda P^n - \pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \to 0\) for every initial distribution \(\lambda\), where \(\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \sup_A |\mu(A) - \nu(A)|\);
  • ergodic strong law: for \(f\) with \(\pi(|f|) < \infty\), a.s. $$\frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(X_k) \to \pi(f) = \int f\,d\pi \qquad (X_0 \sim \lambda \text{ arbitrary}).$$
It is this theorem that turns a dependent trajectory into a "usable average sample" — the engine of all the MCMC methods of chapter 8. Positive recurrence (existence of a return time with finite expectation) replaces irreducibility + recurrence in general spaces; complete proofs in Levin-Peres-Wilmer (reference [3]).
Example 4.4 — Two states, explicit computations On \(E = \{1,2\}\), \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}\). Invariance: \(\pi = \pi P\) gives \(0.3\pi_1 = 0.2\pi_2\), i.e. \(\pi = (0.4\,;\,0.6)\). Spectrum: \(1\) and \(0.5\), hence \(P^n = \Pi + 0.5^n\,R\) (spectral decomposition): the distance to equilibrium decreases exactly like \(0.5^n\) — mixing time (defined in ch. 8) \(t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon) \approx \lceil \log_2(1/\varepsilon)\rceil\). Check of reversibility: \(\pi_1 P_{12} = 0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.12 = 0.6 \times 0.2 = \pi_2 P_{21}\) ✓.
Example 4.5 — Gaussian AR(1) (general space) \(X_{k+1} = a X_k + \varepsilon_k\), \(|a| < 1\), \(\varepsilon_k\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)\). If \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2/(1-a^2))\) then all the \(X_k\) follow this distribution (stability under Gaussian convolution): it is a Markov chain on \(\mathbb{R}\) with an explicit invariant distribution, reversible (Gaussian kernel symmetric in the gap). It is the exact prototype of a well-tuned random walk Metropolis (ch. 8) and of the Gaussian ULA (ch. 9).
Connection with ML Markov chains appear everywhere: Gibbs samplers for Bayesian inference (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 8); latent Markov chains of state-space models; stochastic optimization trajectories; and the diffusion processes of generative models (ch. 9, see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3). The irreducibility/aperiodicity pair respectively guarantees accessibility of the whole space and the absence of locked oscillations.

5. Simulating: inverse, rejection, Box-Muller

Proposition 5.1 — Inverse transform method (proved) Let \(F\) be a cumulative distribution function and \(F^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \geq u\}\) (generalized inverse). If \(U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]\), then \(X = F^{-1}(U)\) has distribution \(F\).
\(F^{-1}(u) \leq x\) is equivalent to \(u \leq F(x)\) (property of the infimum); hence \(\mathbb{P}(F^{-1}(U) \leq x) = \mathbb{P}(U \leq F(x)) = F(x)\).∎
Application: \(X = -\ln U \sim \mathcal{E}(1)\) (distribution \(1-e^{-x}\)), and \(X = \lfloor nU \rfloor + 1\) is uniform on \(\{1,\dots,n\}\). Conversely, every algorithm producing "random reals" on a machine truncates a uniform distribution — the only primitive source (together with physical generators) of the whole chapter.
Proposition 5.2 — Rejection method (correction proved) Let \(f\) be the target density (with respect to \(\lambda\)), and \(g\) a proposal density such that \(f \leq M g\) with \(M < \infty\). Algorithm: draw independent \(X \sim g\), \(U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]\); accept if \(U \leq f(X)/(Mg(X))\), otherwise start over. Then:
  • the acceptance probability at each trial equals \(1/M\);
  • the distribution of \(X\) conditional on acceptance is exactly \(f\).
For every Borel set \(A\): $$\mathbb{P}(X \in A,\ \text{accepted}) = \int_A g(x)\, \frac{f(x)}{Mg(x)}\,dx = \frac{1}{M}\int_A f(x)\,dx = \frac{\pi_f(A)}{M},$$ where we used the independence of \(U\) and \(X\) and then Fubini. Taking \(A = \mathbb{R}\): \(\mathbb{P}(\text{accepted}) = 1/M\). By Bayes' formula, \(\mathbb{P}(X \in A \mid \text{accepted}) = \frac{\pi_f(A)/M}{1/M} = \pi_f(A)\).∎
The number of trials follows a geometric distribution with parameter \(1/M\) (expectation \(M\)): all the art lies in choosing \(g\) close to \(f\) to minimize \(M\).
Example 5.3 — Simulating \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) with a Laplace proposal (numbers) Proposal: \(g(x) = \tfrac12 e^{-|x|}\). The ratio \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\; e^{-x^2/2 + |x|}\) is maximal at \(|x|=1\), where \(-x^2/2+|x| = 1/2\): \(M = \sqrt{2e/\pi} \approx 1.3155\), acceptance rate \(1/M = \sqrt{\pi/(2e)} \approx 76\,\%\). On average 1.31 proposals per accepted sample: very efficient, and requiring no knowledge of the normalization constant of \(f\) (a truncated distribution, for instance). It is the mechanism behind most generators of "exotic" distributions.
Proposition 5.4 — Box-Muller (proved by change of variables) Let \(U,V\) be i.i.d. \(\mathcal{U}[0,1]\). Set \(R = \sqrt{-2\ln U}\) and \(\Theta = 2\pi V\). Then \(Z_1 = R\cos\Theta\) and \(Z_2 = R\sin\Theta\) are i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).
\(\mathbb{P}(R \leq r) = \mathbb{P}(U \geq e^{-r^2/2}) = 1 - e^{-r^2/2}\), density \(f_R(r) = r e^{-r^2/2}\) (Rayleigh), independent of \(\Theta\) (uniform). The pair has density \(f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta) = \frac{r}{2\pi} e^{-r^2/2}\) on \((0,\infty) \times (0,2\pi)\). Inverse polar change of variables \((r,\theta) \mapsto (x,y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\), with Jacobian \(\left|\det \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}\right| = r\) (direct computation): the density of the pair \((Z_1,Z_2)\) equals $$f_{Z_1,Z_2}(x,y) = \frac{f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)}{r} = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2},$$ which is the product of the two densities \(\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\): independence and Gaussian marginals.∎
Example 1.5 (the Gaussian integral) provides the constant \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\): the chapters echo one another. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def box_muller(n):                      # n standard Gaussians (n even)
    u = rng.random(n // 2); v = rng.random(n // 2)
    r = np.sqrt(-2.0 * np.log(u)); t = 2.0 * np.pi * v
    return np.concatenate([r * np.cos(t), r * np.sin(t)])

def gaussian_rejection(n, m=1.3155):    # N(0,1) by rejection with Laplace
    out = []
    while len(out) < n:
        x = rng.laplace(0.0, 1.0)       # density (1/2) exp(-|x|)
        u = rng.random()
        if u < np.exp(-x*x/2.0) / (m * 0.5 * np.exp(-abs(x))):
            out.append(x)
    return np.array(out)

z1, z2 = box_muller(200000), gaussian_rejection(200000)
print(z1.mean(), z1.var())              # ~0.000 , ~1.000
print(z2.mean(), z2.var())              # ~0.001 , ~1.000

def mixture(n):                          # 0.4 N(-2,0.5^2) + 0.6 N(2.5,0.8^2)
    j = rng.random(n) < 0.6
    return np.where(j, 2.5 + 0.8*rng.standard_normal(n),
                       -2.0 + 0.5*rng.standard_normal(n))
Practice In production one uses rng.standard_normal (ziggurat, an optimized rejection variant) — but Box-Muller and rejection remain the pedagogical building blocks and the fallback tools when the target is no longer standard: truncated distributions, distributions with an unknown normalization constant (typical in the Bayesian setting, ch. 8). All the methods in the rest of the module are generalizations of this program: target known up to a constant → i.i.d. samples (rejection, IS) or correlated ones (MCMC).

6. Monte Carlo and variance reduction

Definition 6.1 — Monte Carlo estimator To estimate \(\theta = \mathbb{E}[h(X)]\), \(X \sim \pi\), draw \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) i.i.d. from \(\pi\) and set \(\hat\theta_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n h(X_i)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n] = \theta\) (unbiased, LLN); \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n) = \sigma_h^2/n\) where \(\sigma_h^2 = \mathrm{Var}(h(X_1))\); and by the CLT (Thm 2.3), $$\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma_h}\big(\hat\theta_n - \theta\big) \approx \mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad \theta \in \left[\hat\theta_n \pm 1.96\,\frac{\hat\sigma_h}{\sqrt n}\right] \ \text{at 95\%},$$ with \(\hat\sigma_h^2 = \frac1{n-1}\sum_i (h(X_i) - \hat\theta_n)^2\). The (RMSE) error decreases like \(\sigma_h/\sqrt n\), that is like \(1/\sqrt n\) independently of the dimension of the space of \(X\): this is the strength of Monte Carlo over quadrature, whose cost grows exponentially with dimension.
Example 6.2 — Estimating \(\pi\), with error bars (numbers) \(X,Y\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal{U}[-1,1]\), \(h = \mathbf{1}_{X^2+Y^2 \leq 1}\), \(\theta = \pi/4\). Here \(\sigma_h^2 = \theta(1-\theta) \approx 0.1685\), \(\sigma_h \approx 0.4105\). For \(n = 10^4\): standard error \(\approx 0.0041\), interval \(\pm 0.0081\) at 95%; for three exact digits after the decimal point (\(\pm 5\times10^{-4}\) on \(\pi\), i.e. \(2\times10^{-4}\) on \(\theta\)) one needs \(n \approx (1.96 \times 0.4105 / 2\times 10^{-4})^2 \approx 1.6 \times 10^7\). The price of one additional decimal digit is \(\times 100\) samples: the only way out is to reduce \(\sigma_h\).
Proposition 6.3 — Antithetic variables (example computed exactly) For monotone \(f\), \(f(U)\) and \(f(1-U)\) are negatively correlated: $$\mathrm{Var}\Big(\frac{f(U)+f(1-U)}{2}\Big) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(f(U)) + \mathrm{Cov}(f(U),f(1-U))}{2} \leq \frac{\mathrm{Var}(f(U))}{2}.$$ Application to \(I = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1+x} = \ln 2\) with \(f(u) = \frac1{1+u}\): exact computations give \(\mathrm{Var}(f(U)) = \tfrac12 - (\ln 2)^2 \approx 0.0195\), \(\mathbb{E}[f(U)f(1-U)] = \tfrac23 \ln 2 \approx 0.4621\), hence \(\mathrm{Cov} \approx -0.0184\) and a per-pair variance \(\approx 5.5\times10^{-4}\) versus \(9.75\times 10^{-3}\) for two independent draws: a gain of a factor ~18, for free. In practice: uniforms (\(u, 1-u\)), Gaussians (\(z, -z\)), and more generally any measurable transformation with negative covariance.
Proposition 6.4 — Control variates (proved, worked example) Let \(C\) be an r.v. with known expectation \(\mu_C\), correlated with \(h(X)\). Estimator \(\hat\theta_\beta = \hat\theta_n - \beta(\bar C_n - \mu_C)\), unbiased for every \(\beta\). The variance \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_\beta) = \frac{1}{n}\big(\sigma_h^2 - 2\beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(h,C) + \beta^2 \sigma_C^2\big)\) is minimal at \(\beta^* = \mathrm{Cov}(h,C)/\sigma_C^2\), where it equals \(\frac{\sigma_h^2(1-\rho^2)}{n}\), with \(\rho\) the correlation: any nonzero correlation improves the estimator.
Quadratic polynomial in \(\beta\), minimum at \(\beta^*\); the minimal value rewrites as \(\sigma_h^2(1 - \rho^2)\) by definition of \(\rho\). The unknown \(\beta^*\) is estimated from the sample (small cost, negligible \(\mathcal{O}(1/n)\) bias).∎
Example: estimate \(\mathbb{E}[e^U] = e - 1 \approx 1.7183\) with the control \(C = U\) (\(\mathbb{E}C = 1/2\)): \(\mathrm{Cov}(e^U, U) = 1 - (e-1)/2 \approx 0.1409\), \(\mathrm{Var}(U) = 1/12\), \(\beta^* \approx 1.6905\); \(\mathrm{Var}(e^U) \approx 1.2420 \to 1.2420\,(1-\rho^2) \approx 1.0038\): 19% less variance. Conditioning (Example 3.5) is a special case where the control is \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\) itself. 
import numpy as np

7. Importance sampling

Definition 7.1 — Importance sampling estimator To estimate \(\theta = \int h(x) f(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_f[h(X)]\), draw \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) i.i.d. from a density \(g\) (the proposal) with \(g > 0\) everywhere \(hf \neq 0\), and weight by \(w(y) = f(y)/g(y)\): $$\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)\, h(Y_i), \qquad \mathbb{E}_g[\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}}] = \theta \ \text{(unbiased)}.$$ Exact variance: \(\mathrm{Var}_g(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{IS}}) = \frac1n\big(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2h^2] - \theta^2\big) = \frac1n\big(\int \tfrac{h^2 f^2}{g} - \theta^2\big)\), minimal (zero variance) for \(g^\star \propto |h| f\) — the optimal proposal concentrates the draws where the target and the function of interest meet. An indispensable variant: the self-normalized estimator \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{SNIS}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i h(Y_i)}{\sum_i w_i}\), usable when \(f\) is known only up to a constant (the Bayesian case), with bias of order \(\mathcal{O}(1/n)\).
Proposition 7.2 — Weight degeneracy and effective sample size (proved) Set \(\mathrm{cv}^2 = \mathrm{Var}_g(w)/(\mathbb{E}_g[w])^2\) (squared coefficient of variation of the weights; here \(\mathbb{E}_g[w] = 1\)). The effective sample size is $$\mathrm{ESS} = \frac{n}{1 + \mathrm{cv}^2}, \qquad \mathrm{cv}^2 = \chi^2(f \,\|\, g) = \int \frac{(f-g)^2}{g},$$ where \(\chi^2(f\|g)\) is the chi-squared divergence. In particular \(\mathrm{ESS} \leq n\), with equality if and only if \(f = g\), and \(\mathrm{ESS}/n \to 0\) as soon as \(f\) and \(g\) differ substantially.
\(\mathrm{Var}(\bar w) = \mathrm{cv}^2/n\) where \(\bar w\) is the empirical mean of the weights. For the \(\chi^2\) form: \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2] = \int f^2/g\) and \((\mathbb{E}_g w)^2 = 1\), hence \(\mathrm{cv}^2 = \int \frac{f^2}{g} - 1 = \int \frac{(f-g)^2}{g}\) (expand the square and use \(\int f = \int g = 1\)).∎
Gaussian case (exact computation): \(f = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(g = \mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) gives \(w(y) = e^{-\mu y + \mu^2/2}\) then \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2] = e^{\mu^2}\), hence $$\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-\mu^2} \qquad (\mu = 2:\ 1.8\%\ ;\ \mu = 3:\ 0.012\%\ ;\ \mu = 4:\ 1.1\times10^{-7}).$$ In dimension \(d\) with a per-coordinate mismatch \(\mu\), the effect multiplies: \(\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-d\mu^2}\) — the degeneracy of importance sampling is exponential in dimension, where only an adapted proposal (sequential adaptation, particle methods — SMC) survives.
Example 7.3 — Tail probability \(p = \mathbb{P}(Z > 4) = 3.17\times10^{-5}\) (numbers)
  • Crude MC: expected number of "hits" \(np\); for a 10% relative error one needs \(n \approx \frac{1-p}{p \times 0.01} \approx 3.2\times10^6\) draws;
  • IS, exponential tilt \(g = \mathcal{N}(4,1)\): exact computation \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2 \mathbf{1}_{Z>4}] = e^{16}\,\Phi(-8) \approx 5.9\times10^{-9}\), i.e. a per-draw standard deviation \(\approx 7\times10^{-5}\) versus \(p \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-5}\) for the estimator: relative error ~\(2.2/\sqrt n\) — 500 draws suffice where three million were needed;
  • Optimal IS: sample directly in the tail (\(g = f\) conditioned on \(Z>4\), by rejection): constant weight \(w = 1/p\), zero variance — a literal illustration of \(g^\star \propto |h| f\).
Moral: IS turns a rare-event problem into the sampling of typical events — a central technique in reliability, finance (VaR), and Bayesian inference (sequential methods). 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)

n = 200_000
p_exact = 1 - stats.norm.cdf(4.0)               # 3.167e-5

mc = rng.standard_normal(n) > 4.0               # crude Monte Carlo
g = rng.normal(4.0, 1.0, n)                     # exponential tilt
w = np.exp(-4.0*g + 8.0)                        # f/g, N(0,1)/N(4,1)
isw = w * (g > 4.0)

for name, est in [("crude", mc.mean()), ("IS", isw.mean())]:
    print(f"{name:5s} {est:.3e}  (exact {p_exact:.3e})")

ess = n / (1.0 + w.var(ddof=1))                 # ESS on the overall weights
print(f"ESS = {ess:.1f} / {n}")                 # weights highly degenerate outside the tail

Remedies: weight truncation, self-normalization, or moving to an adaptive method (SMC); in high dimension, prefer MCMC (ch. 8) or Langevin (ch. 9). ESS will reappear as a central diagnostic of MCMC chains in chapter 8.

8. MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, diagnostics, annealing

Definition 8.1 — Metropolis-Hastings kernel Let \(\pi\) be a target density on \(E\) (known up to a constant: only \(\pi(x)\) is involved, never its total mass) and \(q(x,dy)\) a proposal kernel. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm builds a Markov chain \((X_k)\): from \(X_k = x\), draw \(Y \sim q(x,\cdot)\) and \(U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]\); set $$X_{k+1} = \begin{cases} Y & \text{if } U \leq \alpha(x,Y),\\ x & \text{otherwise (rejection: the chain stays put),}\end{cases} \qquad \alpha(x,y) = \min\Big(1,\ \frac{\pi(y)\, q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\, q(x,y)}\Big),$$ where \(q(x,y)\) denotes the density of \(q(x,\cdot)\) at the point \(y\) (with respect to some fixed reference measure). Special cases: random walk MH (\(q(x,\cdot) = \mathcal{N}(x, s^2 I)\), \(q(y,x)=q(x,y)\) so \(\alpha = \min(1,\pi(y)/\pi(x))\)); independence sampler (\(q(x,\cdot) = g\) fixed); MALA (proposal derived from Langevin dynamics, ch. 9).
Theorem 8.2 — Detailed balance of the MH kernel (proved) The Metropolis-Hastings transition kernel, $$P(x,dy) = q(x,dy)\,\alpha(x,y) + \Big(1 - \int \alpha(x,z)\,q(x,dz)\Big)\,\delta_x(dy),$$ is \(\pi\)-reversible: \(\pi(dx)\,P(x,dy) = \pi(dy)\,P(y,dx)\). In particular \(\pi\) is invariant.
The rejection term charges only the diagonal \(\{x = y\}\), where the equality to be proved is trivial. For the acceptance term, set \(\rho(x,y) = \pi(x)\,q(x,y)\) (densities with respect to a common dominating measure, e.g. Lebesgue; the case of general kernels is written with the measures \(\pi(dx)q(x,dy)\) and treated identically). Let us verify that \(\rho(x,y)\,\alpha(x,y)\) is symmetric in \((x,y)\), by case analysis:
  • if \(\rho(y,x) \leq \rho(x,y)\): \(\alpha(x,y) = \rho(y,x)/\rho(x,y)\) and \(\alpha(y,x) = 1\), hence \(\rho(x,y)\alpha(x,y) = \rho(y,x) = \rho(y,x)\alpha(y,x)\);
  • if \(\rho(y,x) > \rho(x,y)\): symmetrically \(\alpha(x,y) = 1\) and \(\alpha(y,x) = \rho(x,y)/\rho(y,x)\), same conclusion.
In both cases \(\rho(x,y)\alpha(x,y) = \min(\rho(x,y), \rho(y,x))\), an expression symmetric in \((x,y)\): the detailed balance \(\pi(dx)P(x,dy) = \pi(dy)P(y,dx)\) is proved. Invariance: write \(\mu(dx,dy) = \pi(dx)\,q(x,dy)\,\alpha(x,y)\) (symmetric) and \(r(x) = \int q(x,dz)\alpha(x,z)\). For every Borel set \(A\), $$(\pi P)(A) = \mu(E \times A) + \int_A \big(1 - r(x)\big)\,\pi(dx).$$ By symmetry of \(\mu\), its marginal in the second variable equals \(\mu(E,dy) = \int \mu(dx,dy) = \int \mu(dy,dx) = \pi(dy)\,r(y)\), hence \(\mu(E\times A) = \int_A r\,d\pi\) and \((\pi P)(A) = \int_A r\,d\pi + \int_A (1-r)\,d\pi = \pi(A)\).∎
Only \(\pi\) being known up to a constant is required: if \(\pi = \tilde\pi/Z\), \(Z\) cancels in \(\alpha\) — hence the success of MH in Bayesian inference (see the Refresher Statistics module, chapter 8).
Proposition 8.3 — Gibbs = block-wise Metropolis-Hastings (proved) Let \(\pi\) be a density on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and, for \(i \in \{1,\dots,d\}\), the full conditional distribution \(\pi(x_i \mid x_{-i})\). The Gibbs kernel for "coordinate \(i\)": \(x \to y = (x_{-i}, y_i)\) with \(y_i \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x_{-i})\), is an MH kernel with acceptance probability 1.
This is MH with the degenerate proposal \(q_i(x,dy) = \pi(y_i \mid x_{-i})\, \delta_{x_{-i}}(dy_{-i})\). The acceptance ratio between \(x\) and \(y\) (which differ only in coordinate \(i\)) equals $$\frac{\pi(y)\,\pi(x_i \mid y_{-i})}{\pi(x)\,\pi(y_i \mid x_{-i})} = \frac{\pi(y_{-i})\,\pi(y_i \mid y_{-i})\,\pi(x_i \mid y_{-i})} {\pi(x_{-i})\,\pi(x_i \mid x_{-i})\,\pi(y_i \mid x_{-i})} = 1,$$ since \(y_{-i} = x_{-i}\) (decomposition \(\pi(x) = \pi(x_{-i})\pi(x_i\mid x_{-i})\)). Hence \(\alpha \equiv 1\): we always accept, and detailed balance (Thm 8.2) guarantees the invariance of \(\pi\) for each sweep.∎
The systematic scan (coordinates 1 to \(d\)) or random scan (uniform coordinate) composes these kernels: a composition of \(\pi\)-invariant kernels is a \(\pi\)-invariant kernel. Gibbs is the building block of conditional Bayesian sampling (graphical models, mixture models — see the Machine Learning module, chapter 6, for the EM side).
Proposition 8.4 — Applied ergodic theorem and chain CLT (statements) If the MH kernel is \(\pi\)-irreducible and aperiodic (checked as soon as \(q\) charges a neighborhood of 0 or the rejection probability is nonzero):
  • ergodicity (Thm 4.3): \(\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n h(X_k) \to \pi(h)\) a.s. for \(h \in L^1(\pi)\);
  • Markov chain CLT (under geometric reversibility): \(\sqrt n\,(\hat\mu_{\mathrm{MH}} - \pi(h)) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{MH}}^2)\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{MH}}^2 = \mathrm{Var}_\pi(h(X_0)) + 2\sum_{k\geq1} \mathrm{Cov}_\pi(h(X_0), h(X_k))\): positive autocorrelation inflates the effective variance — hence the correction factor \(\tau = 1 + 2\sum_k \rho_k\) ("integrated autocorrelation time").
Definition 8.5 — Convergence diagnostics and chain ESS
  • Trace (trajectory of \((X_k)\) per coordinate): visible mixing, mode trapping (the chain stays stuck in one mode of a multimodal target);
  • Autocorrelogram: \(\rho_k\) as a function of the lag \(k\); slow decay = slow exploration;
  • Chain ESS: \(\mathrm{ESS} = n/\tau\) with \(\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_k\) (truncation by Geyer's heuristic); MCMC error bar \(\approx \hat\sigma_h \sqrt{\tau/n}\);
  • Gelman-Rubin \(\hat R\): several chains started from overdispersed initializations; convergence when the between-chain and within-chain variances balance (\(\hat R \to 1\), usual threshold 1.01-1.1);
  • acceptance rate: for random walk MH, the theoretically optimal scale is ~0.234 in high dimension (0.44 in dimension 1); a step that is too small explores slowly, too large rejects everything.
Proposition 8.6 — Simulated annealing (statement) To maximize \(U\) (or minimize \(-U\)), one samples \(\pi_T(x) \propto e^{U(x)/T}\) with a temperature \(T\) decreasing like \(T_k \sim c/\log k\): under this schedule (Hajek's theorem, stated), the chain converges to the set of global maxima; in practice \(T_k = T_0 \times 0.95^k\) (geometric, no guarantee). Annealing is the interface between sampling and optimization: high temperatures to explore, low ones to exploit — the same trade-off as the temperature schedules of diffusion models (ch. 9). 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# target: bimodal mixture 0.5 N(-2.5, 0.7^2) + 0.5 N(2.5, 0.7^2)
logpi = lambda x: np.logaddexp(-((x+2.5)**2)/(2*0.49),
                               -((x-2.5)**2)/(2*0.49))

def rw_mh(x0, n, s):
    x = np.empty(n); x[0] = x0; acc = 0
    for k in range(n-1):
        y = x[k] + s * rng.standard_normal()
        if np.log(rng.random()) < logpi(y) - logpi(x[k]):
            x[k+1] = y; acc += 1
        else:
            x[k+1] = x[k]
    return x, acc/n

x, rate = rw_mh(0.0, 200_000, 4.0)     # large step to jump between modes
print(f"acceptance {rate:.3f}, mean {x[1000:].mean():+.3f} (exact 0)")

def ess(z):                            # truncated positive autocorrelations
    z = z - z.mean(); n = len(z)
    acf = [1.0] + [np.corrcoef(z[:-k], z[k:])[0,1] for k in range(1, 200)]
    tau, s = 1.0, 0.0
    for k in range(1, len(acf), 2):    # Geyer pairs
        pair = acf[k] + acf[k+1] if k+1 < len(acf) else acf[k]
        if pair <= 0: break
        s += pair
    return n / (1 + 2*s)

print(f"ESS = {ess(x[1000:]):.0f} / 199000")
Example 8.7 — Reading an experiment (numbers) On the mixture above with \(s=4\) and \(n=2\times10^5\): acceptance rate \(\approx 0.28\) (close to the 0.234 target in dimension 1-2), empirical mean \(\approx \pm 0.03\) around the exact value 0, ESS \(\approx 3\,000\) (strong autocorrelation because inter-mode jumps are rare). With \(s = 0.7\): acceptance ~0.7 but mode trapping — the chain spends 10⁴ iterations in one mode (flat trace), \(\hat R\) far above 1 between two chains initialized in opposite modes. Diagnosis: ESS alone is not enough; several overdispersed chains are needed (reference [5]).

9. Langevin dynamics and generative models

Definition 9.1 — Overdamped Langevin diffusion Let \(U : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) (the potential) be \(\mathcal{C}^1\), with \(e^{-U} \in L^1\). The Langevin diffusion is the SDE $$dX_t = -\nabla U(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dW_t,$$ where \((W_t)\) is a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion. Its invariant distribution (if it exists: this is the case if \(U\) is confining, e.g. \(U(x) \geq a\|x\| - b\)) is $$\pi(x) = \frac{e^{-U(x)}}{\int e^{-U}\,d\lambda},$$ the Gibbs distribution associated with \(U\). In ML, one rather writes \(U = -\log \pi\): the drift \(-\nabla U = \nabla \log \pi\) is the score — a central quantity in generative models (see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3).
Proposition 9.2 — \(\pi\) invariant for the Langevin diffusion (proved) For every test function \(\varphi \in \mathcal{C}^\infty_c\) and \(X \sim \pi\): \(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\varphi(X)] = 0\), where \(\mathcal{A}\varphi = -\nabla U \cdot \nabla \varphi + \Delta \varphi\) is the generator of the diffusion. This characterizes invariance (expectations are preserved along the trajectories).
With \(\pi = e^{-U}/Z\) and \(d\pi = Z^{-1}e^{-U(x)}dx\), integrations by parts (the boundary terms vanish, \(\varphi\) being compactly supported): $$\int (\Delta\varphi)\,e^{-U}\,dx = -\int \nabla\varphi \cdot \nabla(e^{-U})\,dx = \int \nabla\varphi \cdot \nabla U\; e^{-U}\,dx,$$ using \(\nabla(e^{-U}) = -(\nabla U)\,e^{-U}\). Substituting: $$Z\,\mathbb{E}_\pi[\mathcal{A}\varphi] = \int \big[-\nabla U\cdot\nabla\varphi + \Delta\varphi\big]\,e^{-U}dx = \int \big[-\nabla U\cdot\nabla\varphi + \nabla U \cdot \nabla\varphi\big]e^{-U}dx = 0.$$ ∎
Direct check in the Gaussian case \(U(x) = \|x\|^2/2\): the diffusion is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion \(dX_t = -X_t dt + \sqrt2\,dW_t\), and \(\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\) is stationary (chap. 4, Example 4.5).
Definition 9.3 — ULA and MALA The Euler-Maruyama discretization with step \(\gamma > 0\) gives the ULA algorithm (unadjusted, without correction): $$X_{k+1} = X_k - \gamma\,\nabla U(X_k) + \sqrt{2\gamma}\,Z_{k+1}, \qquad Z_{k+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d).$$ ULA is a biased Markov chain: its limiting distribution \(\pi_\gamma\) differs from \(\pi\) (an \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\) gap). MALA adds a Metropolis-Hastings step (Def. 8.1) with the Gaussian proposal above, removing the bias exactly (at the price of rejections); the bias/cost trade-off is the subject of the work described in chapter 10.
Example 9.4 — The ULA bias computed exactly (Gaussian case) For \(\pi = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (\(U(x)=x^2/2\)): \(X_{k+1} = (1-\gamma)X_k + \sqrt{2\gamma}Z_{k+1}\) — an AR(1) (Example 4.5). Its stationary distribution is \(\mathcal{N}\big(0,\ \sigma_\gamma^2\big)\) with $$\sigma_\gamma^2 = \frac{2\gamma}{1-(1-\gamma)^2} = \frac{2}{2-\gamma} \;\xrightarrow[\gamma \to 0]{}\; 1.$$ Numbers: \(\gamma = 0.5 \Rightarrow \sigma_\gamma^2 = 1.33\) (bias +33%); \(\gamma = 0.1 \Rightarrow 1.053\) (+5%); \(\gamma = 0.01 \Rightarrow 1.005\) (+0.5%). The variance bias is \(\gamma/(2-\gamma) \approx \gamma/2\): halving the bias doubles the number of iterations needed to reach a given region — paper [1] of chapter 10 precisely quantifies this trade-off (explicit convergence speed in \(\gamma\), \(d\), and the target error).
Theorem 9.5 — Non-asymptotic guarantees for ULA (statement, [1]-[2]) If \(U\) is \(L\)-smooth and strongly convex, Durmus & Moulines (2017) show that after \(k\) iterations with step \(\gamma\), in Wasserstein distance \(W_2\) (see the Optimal Transport module, chapter 2) and in total variation, $$W_2\big(\mathrm{law}(X_k),\,\pi\big) \;\leq\; \mathcal{O}\!\Big(e^{-k\gamma m/2}\,\sqrt{d/m} + \sqrt{\gamma d/m}\Big) \quad\text{(orders of magnitude, } m = \text{strong convexity constant)},$$ that is, exponential decay toward a bias plateau proportional to \(\sqrt\gamma\); with decreasing steps, ULA converges (ergodicity) but with degraded rates. These bounds, explicit in the dimension \(d\), ground the modern analysis of high-dimensional sampling (ch. 10).
Proposition 9.6 — The score / generative models connection Diffusion-based generative models (see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3; Deep Learning module, chapter 7, for VAE/GAN) learn a family of scores \(s_\theta(x,t) \approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) of the noised versions \(p_t\) of the data. Generation by annealed Langevin runs, for decreasing \(t\), $$X_{k+1} = X_k + \tfrac{\varepsilon_t}{2}\,s_\theta(X_k,t) + \sqrt{\varepsilon_t}\,Z_{k+1} \qquad (\text{ULA with potential } -\log p_t),$$ starting from white noise: decreasing temperature = simulated annealing (Prop. 8.6) applied to Langevin dynamics. The network learns the drift of the diffusion; the final randomness \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,Z\) does the rest. The quantities of this chapter — Euler bias, optimal step, noise calibration — are therefore the same tuning knobs as those of diffusion models. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

def ula(gamma, n=200_000):             # target N(0,1): U(x) = x^2/2
    x = 3.0
    for k in range(n):
        x = x - gamma*x + np.sqrt(2*gamma)*rng.standard_normal()
        if k >= n//2:                  # ignore burn-in
            yield x

for g in (0.5, 0.1, 0.01):
    v = np.fromiter(ula(g), float)
    print(f"gamma={g:4.2f}  stationary var {v.var():.4f}"
          f"  theory {2/(2-g):.4f}")

def mala(gamma, n=200_000):            # same proposal + MH acceptance
    x, out = 3.0, []
    U, dU = lambda z: z*z/2, lambda z: z
    q = lambda a, b: -(b - a + gamma*dU(a))**2 / (4*gamma)
    for k in range(n):
        y = x - gamma*dU(x) + np.sqrt(2*gamma)*rng.standard_normal()
        loga = U(x) - U(y) + q(x, y) - q(y, x)
        if np.log(rng.random()) < loga: x = y
        if k >= n//2: out.append(x)
    return np.array(out)

print(f"MALA gamma=0.5 : var {mala(0.5).var():.4f} (exact 1)")

10. Research of the teaching team: sampling in high dimension

Alain Durmus — research directions Alain Durmus (CMAP, École Polytechnique) works at the interface of probability/statistics/ML, on the non-asymptotic analysis of sampling methods and their applications to Bayesian inference and generative models. Three foundational papers literally structure chapters 8-9 of this module.
Paper 1 — "Non-asymptotic convergence analysis for the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" A. Durmus, É. Moulines, Annals of Applied Probability 27(3), 2017 — arXiv:1507.05021. The first paper to give explicit bounds for the convergence of ULA, in total variation distance and in Kullback-Leibler divergence, for convex (strongly or weakly) potentials: exponential decay toward a step-dependent plateau, total cost \(\mathcal{O}(d/\varepsilon^2)\) (strongly convex) and \(\mathcal{O}(d/\varepsilon^4)\) otherwise, for an error \(\varepsilon\). It is the source of Theorem 9.5: the discipline of "bounds in \((d, \varepsilon)\)" for sampling algorithms originates there.
Paper 2 — "High-dimensional Bayesian inference via the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" A. Durmus, É. Moulines, Annals of Statistics 45(4), 2017 — arXiv:1605.01559. The "dimension-optimized" version: bounds in Wasserstein \(W_2\) distance and in total variation, with a precise tracking of the dependence on \(d\) under strong log-concavity; the paper also studies a so-called EMA variant — an Euler-Maruyama approximation with corrected drift aimed at reducing the discretization bias in high dimension — and weighted empirical averages (exponential deviation bounds for averages along the chain, illustrated on Bayesian binary regression). This paper gives Example 9.4 its general framework: how to choose \(\gamma\) as \(d\) grows.
Paper 3 — "The Tamed Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" N. Brosse, A. Durmus, É. Moulines, S. Sabanis, Stochastic Processes and their Applications 129(10), 2019 — arXiv:1710.05559. What can be done when \(\nabla U\) grows super-linearly (potentials that are not globally Lipschitz: steep double wells, logistic priors…)? Euler-Maruyama becomes explosive; the authors "tame" the drift (\(\nabla U\) divided by \(1+\gamma\|\nabla U\|\)) and prove the convergence of TULA — a magnificent example of stability reasoning guided by non-asymptotic analysis. The name taming has become a whole family of algorithms.
Guiding thread for the student These three papers read in the order of the course: convergence of ULA (ch. 4, 8, 9); dimension and bias (ch. 6, 9); robustness to steep drifts (ch. 9). They underlie the modern literature on scores and diffusion models (see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3), where the same questions — step, bias, stability, dimension — arise for drifts learned by a neural network.

Exercises

  1. Tonelli and exact computation. Let \(U_1, U_2\) be i.i.d. \(\mathcal{U}[0,1]\). Writing \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^1 \mathbb{P}(Y > t)\,dt\) for \(Y \geq 0\) (layer-cake formula), compute \(\mathbb{E}[\min(U_1,U_2)]\) then \(\mathbb{E}[|U_1 - U_2|]\) via a double integral (Fubini, nonnegative or integrable function). Solution. \(\mathbb{P}(\min > t) = (1-t)^2\) so \(\mathbb{E}[\min] = \int_0^1 (1-t)^2 dt = \tfrac13\). For the difference: \(\mathbb{E}|U_1-U_2| = 2\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{u_1}(u_1-u_2)\, du_2\,du_1 = 2\int_0^1 \tfrac{u_1^2}{2}du_1 = \tfrac13\) (the factor 2 by symmetry of the two half-triangles); check via layers: \(\mathbb{P}(|U_1-U_2| > t) = (1-t)^2\), same integral.
  2. Control tower. Prove the tower property in the case \(\mathcal{H} = \sigma(Y)\), \(\mathcal{G} = \sigma(Y,Z)\) discrete: verify point by point that \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y,Z] \mid Y] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\) by summing over the values of \(Z\). Recover the law of total probability. Hint. Use Definition 3.1 with \(A = \{Y = y\}\) and the partition \(\{Y=y\} = \bigcup_z \{Y=y, Z=z\}\).
    \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y] = \sum_z \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y, Z=z]\,\mathbb{P}(Z=z \mid Y=y)\): the average of conditional averages, weighted by the distribution of \(Z\) given \(Y\). Trivial \(\mathcal{H}\) case: \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]] = \mathbb{E}[X]\) = total probability.
  3. Walk on the triangle. A random walk jumps uniformly to one of the two other vertices of a triangle (each step changes vertex). Show that the chain is irreducible but periodic (period 2), determine the invariant distribution, then describe the lazy version (stay in place with probability 1/2) and its convergence. Solution. Complete graph without loops: connected, hence irreducible; one returns to the starting point in an even number of steps (each step changes parity): period 2. Doubly stochastic matrix → uniform invariant distribution (reversible). The lazy version is aperiodic (the introduced loops "break" the gcd); by Thm 4.3, convergence in total variation to the uniform distribution.
  4. Optimal rejection (full computation). We want to simulate the distribution with density \(f(x) \propto e^{-x^2/2}\mathbf{1}_{x>0}\) (truncated positive Gaussian) by rejection with the standard exponential proposal \(g(x) = e^{-x}\mathbf{1}_{x>0}\). Compute the explicit \(M\), the acceptance rate, and the expected number of proposals. Solution. \(f(x) = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-x^2/2}\mathbf{1}_{x>0}\); \(\frac{f}{g} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-x^2/2+x}\), maximal at \(x=1\) where the exponent equals \(1/2\): \(M = \sqrt{2e/\pi} \approx 1.3155\); rate \(= 1/M \approx 76\%\) — the same constants as Example 5.3 (no coincidence: by symmetry, drawing the absolute value of a truncated N(0,1) is equivalent to the symmetric Gaussian/Laplace problem); expected number of trials \(= M \approx 1.32\).
  5. Gaussian degeneracy of IS. Redo the computation of Prop. 7.2: show \(\mathbb{E}_g[w^2] = e^{\mu^2}\) for \(f=\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(g=\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\), deduce \(\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-\mu^2}\), then comment on the \(d\)-dimensional case with a proposal centered at \(\mu\mathbf{1}\). Solution. \(w^2 = e^{-2\mu Y + \mu^2}\) and \(\mathbb{E}_g[e^{tY}] = e^{t\mu + t^2/2}\) with \(t = -2\mu\) gives \(\mathbb{E}_g[e^{-2\mu Y}] = e^{-2\mu^2+2\mu^2} = 1\), hence \(\mathbb{E}_g w^2 = e^{\mu^2}\). In \(d\) product dimensions, \(\mathbb{E}w^2 = e^{d\mu^2}\): \(\mathrm{ESS}/n = e^{-d\mu^2}\) — for \(\mu = 0.5\) and \(d = 50\), \(e^{-12.5} \approx 3.7\times10^{-6}\): one million draws yield ~4 effective points.
  6. MH-independence. Establish the acceptance probability of the independence sampler (\(q(x,\cdot) = g\)) and verify detailed balance directly. What happens when \(g\) has lighter tails than \(\pi\) (e.g. \(g\) Gaussian, \(\pi\) Cauchy)? Hint. Redo the proof of Thm 8.2 with \(\rho(x,y) = \pi(x)g(y)\).
    \(\alpha(x,y) = \min\big(1, \tfrac{\pi(y)g(x)}{\pi(x)g(y)}\big)\). Detail: \(\pi(x)g(y)\,\alpha(x,y) = \min(\pi(x)g(y), \pi(y)g(x))\) symmetric. If \(g\) decays faster than \(\pi\), the ratio \(\pi/g\) is unbounded: rejections dominate in the tails, the chain sticks to its states and is no longer geometrically ergodic — golden rule: heavier tails for the proposal.
  7. ULA on a Gaussian (proof to complete). For \(\pi = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), show that the stationary distribution of the AR(1) \(X_{k+1} = (1-\gamma)X_k + \sqrt{2\gamma}Z_{k+1}\) is \(\mathcal{N}(0, 2/(2-\gamma))\) (stationary variance computation), deduce the relative bias of the variance and the step needed for a bias < 1%. Solution. \(\sigma^2_\infty = (1-\gamma)^2\sigma^2_\infty + 2\gamma\) (independence of the innovations) gives \(\sigma^2_\infty = 2\gamma/(\gamma(2-\gamma)) = 2/(2-\gamma)\). Relative bias \(= \gamma/(2-\gamma) \approx \gamma/2\); < 1% requires \(\gamma < 0.02\). Generalization (bonus question): in \(d\) isotropic dimensions, same computation per axis; see paper [1] of ch. 10 for possible non-Lipschitz behavior.
  8. ESS and weights. Show that for normalized weights \(w_1,\dots,w_n > 0\), \(\bar w_i = w_i/\sum_j w_j\), the quantity \(1/\sum_i \bar w_i^2 \in [1,n]\) (participation index) coincides with the definition \(\mathrm{ESS} = n/(1+\mathrm{cv}^2)\) in expectation, and interpret the two extremes. Solution (sketch). Expanding \(\sum_i \bar w_i^2\) to first order around the mean weights, \(\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_i \bar w_i^2\big] \approx \frac1n\big(1 + \mathrm{cv}^2\big)\), hence the identification with the definition \(\mathrm{ESS} \approx 1/\sum_i \bar w_i^2\). Extremes: equal weights (\(\bar w_i = 1/n\)) give \(\sum \bar w_i^2 = 1/n\), i.e. \(\mathrm{ESS} = n\) (all draws count); if a single particle carries all the weight, \(\sum \bar w_i^2 = 1\) and \(\mathrm{ESS} = 1\). This index is the diagnostic displayed by all SMC software.
