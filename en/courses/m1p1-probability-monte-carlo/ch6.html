6. Monte Carlo and variance reduction

Definition 6.1 — Monte Carlo estimator To estimate \(\theta = \mathbb{E}[h(X)]\), \(X \sim \pi\), draw \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) i.i.d. from \(\pi\) and set \(\hat\theta_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n h(X_i)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n] = \theta\) (unbiased, LLN); \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n) = \sigma_h^2/n\) where \(\sigma_h^2 = \mathrm{Var}(h(X_1))\); and by the CLT (Thm 2.3), $$\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma_h}\big(\hat\theta_n - \theta\big) \approx \mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad \theta \in \left[\hat\theta_n \pm 1.96\,\frac{\hat\sigma_h}{\sqrt n}\right] \ \text{at 95\%},$$ with \(\hat\sigma_h^2 = \frac1{n-1}\sum_i (h(X_i) - \hat\theta_n)^2\). The (RMSE) error decreases like \(\sigma_h/\sqrt n\), that is like \(1/\sqrt n\) independently of the dimension of the space of \(X\): this is the strength of Monte Carlo over quadrature, whose cost grows exponentially with dimension.
Example 6.2 — Estimating \(\pi\), with error bars (numbers) \(X,Y\) i.i.d. \(\mathcal{U}[-1,1]\), \(h = \mathbf{1}_{X^2+Y^2 \leq 1}\), \(\theta = \pi/4\). Here \(\sigma_h^2 = \theta(1-\theta) \approx 0.1685\), \(\sigma_h \approx 0.4105\). For \(n = 10^4\): standard error \(\approx 0.0041\), interval \(\pm 0.0081\) at 95%; for three exact digits after the decimal point (\(\pm 5\times10^{-4}\) on \(\pi\), i.e. \(2\times10^{-4}\) on \(\theta\)) one needs \(n \approx (1.96 \times 0.4105 / 2\times 10^{-4})^2 \approx 1.6 \times 10^7\). The price of one additional decimal digit is \(\times 100\) samples: the only way out is to reduce \(\sigma_h\).
Proposition 6.3 — Antithetic variables (example computed exactly) For monotone \(f\), \(f(U)\) and \(f(1-U)\) are negatively correlated: $$\mathrm{Var}\Big(\frac{f(U)+f(1-U)}{2}\Big) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(f(U)) + \mathrm{Cov}(f(U),f(1-U))}{2} \leq \frac{\mathrm{Var}(f(U))}{2}.$$ Application to \(I = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1+x} = \ln 2\) with \(f(u) = \frac1{1+u}\): exact computations give \(\mathrm{Var}(f(U)) = \tfrac12 - (\ln 2)^2 \approx 0.0195\), \(\mathbb{E}[f(U)f(1-U)] = \tfrac23 \ln 2 \approx 0.4621\), hence \(\mathrm{Cov} \approx -0.0184\) and a per-pair variance \(\approx 5.5\times10^{-4}\) versus \(9.75\times 10^{-3}\) for two independent draws: a gain of a factor ~18, for free. In practice: uniforms (\(u, 1-u\)), Gaussians (\(z, -z\)), and more generally any measurable transformation with negative covariance.
Proposition 6.4 — Control variates (proved, worked example) Let \(C\) be an r.v. with known expectation \(\mu_C\), correlated with \(h(X)\). Estimator \(\hat\theta_\beta = \hat\theta_n - \beta(\bar C_n - \mu_C)\), unbiased for every \(\beta\). The variance \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_\beta) = \frac{1}{n}\big(\sigma_h^2 - 2\beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(h,C) + \beta^2 \sigma_C^2\big)\) is minimal at \(\beta^* = \mathrm{Cov}(h,C)/\sigma_C^2\), where it equals \(\frac{\sigma_h^2(1-\rho^2)}{n}\), with \(\rho\) the correlation: any nonzero correlation improves the estimator.
Quadratic polynomial in \(\beta\), minimum at \(\beta^*\); the minimal value rewrites as \(\sigma_h^2(1 - \rho^2)\) by definition of \(\rho\). The unknown \(\beta^*\) is estimated from the sample (small cost, negligible \(\mathcal{O}(1/n)\) bias).∎
Example: estimate \(\mathbb{E}[e^U] = e - 1 \approx 1.7183\) with the control \(C = U\) (\(\mathbb{E}C = 1/2\)): \(\mathrm{Cov}(e^U, U) = 1 - (e-1)/2 \approx 0.1409\), \(\mathrm{Var}(U) = 1/12\), \(\beta^* \approx 1.6905\); \(\mathrm{Var}(e^U) \approx 1.2420 \to 1.2420\,(1-\rho^2) \approx 1.0038\): 19% less variance. Conditioning (Example 3.5) is a special case where the control is \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\) itself. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

n = 500_000
x, y = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (2, n))
pi_hat = 4.0 * np.mean(x*x + y*y <= 1.0)
se = 4.0 * np.std(x*x + y*y <= 1.0, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"pi = {pi_hat:.5f} +/- {1.96*se:.5f}")

# antithetic for ln 2
u = rng.random(n // 2)
est = 0.5 * ((1/(1+u)) + (1/(2-u)))
print(f"ln2 = {est.mean():.6f} (variance per draw {est.var(ddof=1):.2e})")

# control variate for E[e^U]
u = rng.random(n)
e = np.exp(u); beta = np.cov(e, u, ddof=1)[0, 1] / u.var(ddof=1)
mc = e - beta * (u - 0.5)
print(f"E[e^U] = {mc.mean():.6f}, e-1 = {np.e-1:.6f}")
Overview of methods Three families to reduce \(\sigma_h\) or reach inaccessible distributions: antithetic/control (this chapter); importance sampling (ch. 7); conditioning (ch. 3). And when sampling \(X \sim \pi\) directly is impossible (unknown normalization constant): MCMC (ch. 8).
