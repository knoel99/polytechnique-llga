Introduction and overview

ModuleProbability Theory for ML: Applications to Monte Carlo Methods and Generative Models
CodeAPM_51056_EP
Team and workloadInstructor: Alain Durmus (École Polytechnique) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · from measure theory to Markov chains: the probabilistic foundation of Monte Carlo methods, MCMC, and generative models based on Langevin dynamics.
Status in the programP1 electiveOutside the recommended track

How do we compute an intractable expectation, sample from a complex distribution, or generate plausible data? This course builds the modern probabilistic answer: from measure theory to Markov chains, then to Monte Carlo methods, MCMC, and Langevin dynamics. Behind state-of-the-art generative models — diffusion, score-based models — hide exactly these objects: samplers, Markov processes, stochastic dynamics. This is the module that gives the LLGA master's program its mathematical depth on the randomness side.

What you will be able to do

  • Handle expectation and integration in the measure-theoretic sense (Tonelli-Fubini, conditional expectation) without loss of rigor.
  • State the convergence results (LLN, CLT, delta method) and use them to build confidence intervals by simulation.
  • Analyze a Markov chain: stationarity, reversibility, ergodicity, speed of convergence to the stationary distribution.
  • Implement the basic sampling methods: inverse, rejection, Box-Muller.
  • Build a Monte Carlo estimator and make it efficient: variance reduction, importance sampling.
  • Implement MCMC algorithms — Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs — with the diagnostics that certify convergence, and use simulated annealing.
  • Connect Langevin dynamics to generative models: sample a high-dimensional density by following a stochastic process.

Where this course fits in the program

Elective of Year 1 · Period 1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS), outside the recommended track: a choice of mathematical specialization rather than application. Its prerequisite is the Refresher in Statistics module, whose measure theory, random variables, and convergence it takes up to the level required by simulation: the review chapter ensures the transition.

Downstream, three modules build directly on this course. Computational Optimal Transport for ML and Fairness draws on stochastic calculus and sampling for transport problems; Multimodal Generative AI revisits the score/diffusion family whose Langevin dynamics, in chapter 9, is one of the foundations; Reinforcement Learning and Autonomous Agents builds on Markov chains and sampling (Monte Carlo methods for control and evaluation). For students aiming at a PhD in applied probability or in generative modeling, it is the most formative P1 elective.

Course outline

  • Review: measure, random variables, convergence: the foundation of the Refresher in Statistics, taken up at the theoretical level required afterwards.
  • Measure and integration: expectation, Tonelli-Fubini: constructing expectation properly and swapping limits and integrals safely.
  • Convergence: LLN, CLT, delta method: what simulation guarantees as the number of samples grows.
  • Conditional expectation: the best possible prediction in the least-squares sense — a central object for generative modeling and for RL alike.
  • Markov chains: stationarity, reversibility, ergodicity: the theory underlying all the samplers in the course.
  • Simulating: inverse, rejection, Box-Muller: generating standard distributions, with their proofs and their limits.
  • Monte Carlo and variance reduction: estimating an integral by simulation, then making it converge faster.
  • Importance sampling: targeting the regions that matter to reduce the variance of an estimator.
  • MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, diagnostics, annealing: sampling intractable distributions, and knowing when the algorithm has converged.
  • Langevin dynamics and generative models: from the stochastic process to sampling high-dimensional densities — the gateway to score-based models.
  • Research of the teaching team: sampling in high dimension: Alain Durmus's research on MCMC, Langevin, and generative modeling.

How to study this module

  • First secure the review material: the opening chapter states precisely what is taken up from the Refresher in Statistics module and at what level.
  • Do the proofs on scratch paper (Tonelli-Fubini, ergodicity): in this module, one proof understood saves ten computational exercises.
  • Implement inverse, rejection, and Metropolis-Hastings yourself before using any library: these algorithms fit in a few dozen lines and lend themselves well to code.
  • Practice MCMC diagnostics (trajectories, autocorrelation, multiple runs): being able to say "this sampler has not converged" is an exam and research skill.
  • Play with dimension: redo a Langevin experiment in increasing dimension to get a feel for the difficulty of high-dimensional sampling, the heart of the final chapter's work.
  • The module is demanding upstream (measure) and abstract downstream (Langevin): schedule review time rather than moving ahead superficially.
A word from the teaching team The generative wave — from score-based models to the multimodal systems of the LLGA master's program — is above all a matter of high-dimensional sampling. This module provides the mathematical keys to this revolution: the ones on which both research advances and the reliability of deployed systems depend.
