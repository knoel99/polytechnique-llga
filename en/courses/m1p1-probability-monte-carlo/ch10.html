10. Research of the teaching team: sampling in high dimension

Alain Durmus — research directions Alain Durmus (CMAP, École Polytechnique) works at the interface of probability/statistics/ML, on the non-asymptotic analysis of sampling methods and their applications to Bayesian inference and generative models. Three foundational papers literally structure chapters 8-9 of this module.
Paper 1 — "Non-asymptotic convergence analysis for the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" A. Durmus, É. Moulines, Annals of Applied Probability 27(3), 2017 — arXiv:1507.05021. The first paper to give explicit bounds for the convergence of ULA, in total variation distance and in Kullback-Leibler divergence, for convex (strongly or weakly) potentials: exponential decay toward a step-dependent plateau, total cost \(\mathcal{O}(d/\varepsilon^2)\) (strongly convex) and \(\mathcal{O}(d/\varepsilon^4)\) otherwise, for an error \(\varepsilon\). It is the source of Theorem 9.5: the discipline of "bounds in \((d, \varepsilon)\)" for sampling algorithms originates there.
Paper 2 — "High-dimensional Bayesian inference via the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" A. Durmus, É. Moulines, Annals of Statistics 45(4), 2017 — arXiv:1605.01559. The "dimension-optimized" version: bounds in Wasserstein \(W_2\) distance and in total variation, with a precise tracking of the dependence on \(d\) under strong log-concavity; the paper also studies a so-called EMA variant — an Euler-Maruyama approximation with corrected drift aimed at reducing the discretization bias in high dimension — and weighted empirical averages (exponential deviation bounds for averages along the chain, illustrated on Bayesian binary regression). This paper gives Example 9.4 its general framework: how to choose \(\gamma\) as \(d\) grows.
Paper 3 — "The Tamed Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm" N. Brosse, A. Durmus, É. Moulines, S. Sabanis, Stochastic Processes and their Applications 129(10), 2019 — arXiv:1710.05559. What can be done when \(\nabla U\) grows super-linearly (potentials that are not globally Lipschitz: steep double wells, logistic priors…)? Euler-Maruyama becomes explosive; the authors "tame" the drift (\(\nabla U\) divided by \(1+\gamma\|\nabla U\|\)) and prove the convergence of TULA — a magnificent example of stability reasoning guided by non-asymptotic analysis. The name taming has become a whole family of algorithms.
Guiding thread for the student These three papers read in the order of the course: convergence of ULA (ch. 4, 8, 9); dimension and bias (ch. 6, 9); robustness to steep drifts (ch. 9). They underlie the modern literature on scores and diffusion models (see the Multimodal GenAI module, chapter 3), where the same questions — step, bias, stability, dimension — arise for drifts learned by a neural network.
