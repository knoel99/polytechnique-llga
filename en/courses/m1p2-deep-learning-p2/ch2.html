2. Theoretical complexity of training

Before stacking GPUs, a theory question: is there a limit to what parallelism can buy? Answer: yes, and it is provable. This chapter is the module's signature (and the instructor's — ch. 8).

Definition 2.1 — Decentralized distributed optimization over a graph \(P\) machines, each with its own convex function \(f_i\), connected by a graph \(G\) (each machine talks only to its neighbors); common goal: $$\min_x\; f(x) = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P} f_i(x).$$ The execution time counts two currencies: the number of local iterations (compute: gradients up to the condition number — see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 2–3) and the communication rounds (one gossip round = mixing the vectors between neighbors). The quality of the network is summarized by the spectral gap \(\gamma \in [0,1)\) of the gossip matrix \(W\) (second largest eigenvalue in modulus): mixing converges as \(\gamma^t\) — the closer \(\gamma \to 1\), the slower the network reaches consensus.
Theorem 2.2 — Lower bounds and optimality (Scaman, Bach, Bubeck, Lee, Massoulié, ICML 2017; statement) For \(f = \frac1P \sum f_i\) \(\mu\)-strongly convex and \(L\)-smooth (\(\kappa = L/\mu\)), every distributed algorithm (in the sense of def. 2.1) requires a time $$T \;=\; \Omega\!\Big(\sqrt{\kappa}\;\cdot\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma}}\Big) \qquad\text{(up to constants, in homogeneous time)},$$ and this order is attained by the combination: Nesterov accelerated gradient descent (see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 3) + Chebyshev accelerated gossip (replacing \(W\) by a degree \(m\) polynomial in \(W\) chosen to flatten the spectrum over \([\gamma, 1]\) — the same trick that accelerates gradients, applied to the graph). The JMLR 2019 version extends the result to the non-strongly convex case: \(\Omega(\sqrt{L/\varepsilon})\) is compounded by a network factor.
Sketch of the lower bound (the "adversary" argument). One constructs a family of "local" quadratics at each node, whose solution \(x^\star\) depends on spread-out information: for machine \(i\) to learn what machine \(j\) knew, the information must travel along the graph, and the mixing of the graph imposes \(\Omega(1/\sqrt{1-\gamma})\) communication rounds (each gossip multiplies the imbalance by only \(\gamma\)); independently, even sequentially, a quadratic with condition number \(\kappa\) requires \(\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)\) gradient accesses (Nesterov's bound — Optimization for AI module, ch. 3). The construction crosses the two obstacles into a quadratic on the product graph × condition number: no algorithm can beat the product of the two bounds. ∎

Engineering consequence: adding machines does not reduce the time below the communication floor. This is the exact analogue of Amdahl's law (ch. 7) for optimization: the "sequential" part is no longer a program but the geometry of the network. All of ch. 4 (all-reduce, compression, overlap) is a war against this \(\sqrt{1/(1-\gamma)}\).
Proposition 2.3 — What parallelism can buy: the "finite sum" regime The bound of theorem 2.2 concerns the time to reach an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal point when the data are partitioned (each \(f_i\) is opaque). If instead all machines can evaluate the same function (replicated data), parallelism reduces the variance of SGD (mini-batching: Optimization for AI module, ch. 5) — but the optimal statistical rate bounds the gain (Dieuleveut et al., see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 11). In short: parallelism speeds up compute, never (or marginally) the statistical complexity — a distinction that commercial announcements like to forget.
