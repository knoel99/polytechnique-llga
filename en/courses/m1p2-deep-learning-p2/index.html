This module takes up the foundation of the P1 Deep Learning course and rereads it through the lens of cost: how many FLOPs, how many bytes, what communications — and what the complexity lower bounds say about what cannot be done better. Around this guiding thread, training parallelization, memory efficiency and inference efficiency become quantitative problems, down to the actual behavior of a 7B LLM on GPU. This is the course where deep learning meets systems.

What you will be able to do

  • Count the FLOPs and memory of a model, and connect these counts to scaling laws.
  • Explain the theoretical complexity bounds of training and what they rule out.
  • Choose a parallelism strategy — data, tensor, pipeline — and justify it quantitatively.
  • Analyze collective communications (all-reduce and friends) and their effective cost.
  • Reduce the memory footprint of a training run with checkpointing and ZeRO.
  • Make inference efficient and know at what performance price.
  • Read a roofline and predict the behavior of a real system, from GPU to 7B LLM.

1. Counting: FLOPs, memory, scaling laws

The module's first skill: exact counting. An engineer who can estimate the FLOPs of a training run by hand saves GPU-days — and catches configuration errors before they explode.

Definition 1.1 — Units: FLOP, FLOPs, FLOP/s A FLOP (floating-point operation) = one elementary operation (+, ×); we count a multiply-add \(a \cdot b + c\) as 2 FLOPs (the module's convention, aligned with the systems literature); FLOP/s = throughput. An A100 GPU: ≈ 19.5 TFLOP/s fp32, 312 TFLOP/s tensor-core bf16; an H100: ≈ 1000 TFLOP/s bf16 (sparse modes excluded). The MFU (model FLOPs utilization) = achieved FLOP/s ÷ peak FLOP/s: 35–50% is a good LLM training run; the rest is lost to memory, communication and subtasks (ch. 7).
Proposition 1.2 — Cost of a linear layer and a transformer (proved) (i) A matrix-matrix product \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) by \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) costs \(2mnp\) FLOPs. (ii) A linear layer \(d_\mathrm{in} \to d_\mathrm{out}\) applied to \(n\) tokens costs \(2\,n\,d_\mathrm{in}d_\mathrm{out}\) FLOPs and has \(d_\mathrm{in}d_\mathrm{out}\) parameters — that is, 2 FLOPs per parameter and per token. (iii) Transformer block of width \(d\), over a sequence of \(n\) tokens: QKVO \(4 \times 2d^2 = 8d^2\), scores \(QK^\top\) and product with \(V\): \(2 \times 2n^2 d\), MLP (width \(4d\)): \(2 \times 2 \times 4d^2 = 16d^2\) — that is, per token: \(\;24d^2 + 4nd\) FLOPs.
(i) Each of the \(mp\) entries of \(C=AB\) is a dot product of length \(n\) (n multiplications + n−1 additions ≈ 2n FLOPs). (ii) special case with \(m=n\) tokens, \(p = d_\mathrm{out}\). (iii): apply (ii) to each projection; for \(QK^\top\) (an \(n \times d\) by \(d \times n\) matrix): \(2n^2 d\), likewise for \((\cdot)V\) (\(n \times n\) by \(n \times d\)); MLP = two layers \(d \to 4d\) then \(4d \to d\). Divide the total by \(n\) tokens to express it per token: QKV projections (\(3 \times 2d^2\)) + output (\(2d^2\)) = \(8d^2\), quadratic part of attention \(4nd\), MLP \(16d^2\). ∎
Theorem 1.3 — The rule \(C \approx 6\,N\,D\) (proved) Pre-training a model with \(N\) parameters (dense transformer) on \(D\) tokens costs $$C \;\approx\; 6\,N\,D \;\;\text{FLOPs} \qquad (\text{forward} \approx 2ND,\; \text{backward} \approx 4ND).$$
Forward pass: the model is a composition of linear layers and negligible operations (normalizations, softmax: \(O(nd)\) or \(O(n^2)\) without a \(d^2\) factor); each parameter is used exactly once per token, at 2 FLOPs each (prop. 1.2 ii) — hence \(2ND\). Backward pass: for each layer, the gradient is computed by (a) applying the transpose of the weight to the upstream gradient (\(2ND\)) and (b) multiplying the upstream gradient by the local input to form the weight gradient (\(2ND\)) — that is \(2 \times 2ND\). Total \(6ND\). Experience (profiling of large training runs) confirms within ±10%, the gap coming from (shared) embeddings, the vocabulary and the heads. ∎

Order of magnitude to remember: 16 bytes per parameter of states + activations. A 7B model with Adam: \(7\times10^9 \times 16\) ≈ 112 GB — before any activation: a single 80 GB GPU is not enough (ch. 5 and 7).
Scaling laws: from Kaplan to Chinchilla (the budget as a primary dimension) Kaplan et al. 2020: over decades, the loss follows a power law \(L(N, D) \approx (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N} + (D_c/D)^{\alpha_D} + L_\infty\) — first reading: enlarging the model helps more than enlarging the data. Hoffmann et al. 2022 (Chinchilla): with the compute budget \(C = 6ND\) fixed, the optimum is reached for \(D \approx 20 N\) — the models of the Kaplan era were undertrained; GPT-3 (300B tokens for 175B parameters) was "too big" for its data, and a 70B on 1.5T tokens dominates it at equal budget. Method lesson for this module: the budget \(C\) is not a side constraint, it is the axis along which one designs the model. (The M2 LLMs module, ch. 3, revisits these laws in detail; the "energy" angle is in the Optimization and Responsible AI for Sustainability module, ch. 1.) 
def flops_transformer(N_params, D_tokens, fwd_ratio=2.0, bwd_ratio=4.0):
    return (fwd_ratio + bwd_ratio) * N_params * D_tokens

C = flops_transformer(6.7e9, 1e12)                  # LLaMA-7B ~ 6.7B params, 1T tokens
print(f"{C:.2e} FLOPs")                              # 4.02e22

2. Theoretical complexity of training

Before stacking GPUs, a theory question: is there a limit to what parallelism can buy? Answer: yes, and it is provable. This chapter is the module's signature (and the instructor's — ch. 8).

Definition 2.1 — Decentralized distributed optimization over a graph \(P\) machines, each with its own convex function \(f_i\), connected by a graph \(G\) (each machine talks only to its neighbors); common goal: $$\min_x\; f(x) = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P} f_i(x).$$ The execution time counts two currencies: the number of local iterations (compute: gradients up to the condition number — see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 2–3) and the communication rounds (one gossip round = mixing the vectors between neighbors). The quality of the network is summarized by the spectral gap \(\gamma \in [0,1)\) of the gossip matrix \(W\) (second largest eigenvalue in modulus): mixing converges as \(\gamma^t\) — the closer \(\gamma \to 1\), the slower the network reaches consensus.
Theorem 2.2 — Lower bounds and optimality (Scaman, Bach, Bubeck, Lee, Massoulié, ICML 2017; statement) For \(f = \frac1P \sum f_i\) \(\mu\)-strongly convex and \(L\)-smooth (\(\kappa = L/\mu\)), every distributed algorithm (in the sense of def. 2.1) requires a time $$T \;=\; \Omega\!\Big(\sqrt{\kappa}\;\cdot\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma}}\Big) \qquad\text{(up to constants, in homogeneous time)},$$ and this order is attained by the combination: Nesterov accelerated gradient descent (see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 3) + Chebyshev accelerated gossip (replacing \(W\) by a degree \(m\) polynomial in \(W\) chosen to flatten the spectrum over \([\gamma, 1]\) — the same trick that accelerates gradients, applied to the graph). The JMLR 2019 version extends the result to the non-strongly convex case: \(\Omega(\sqrt{L/\varepsilon})\) is compounded by a network factor.
Sketch of the lower bound (the "adversary" argument). One constructs a family of "local" quadratics at each node, whose solution \(x^\star\) depends on spread-out information: for machine \(i\) to learn what machine \(j\) knew, the information must travel along the graph, and the mixing of the graph imposes \(\Omega(1/\sqrt{1-\gamma})\) communication rounds (each gossip multiplies the imbalance by only \(\gamma\)); independently, even sequentially, a quadratic with condition number \(\kappa\) requires \(\Omega(\sqrt\kappa)\) gradient accesses (Nesterov's bound — Optimization for AI module, ch. 3). The construction crosses the two obstacles into a quadratic on the product graph × condition number: no algorithm can beat the product of the two bounds. ∎

Engineering consequence: adding machines does not reduce the time below the communication floor. This is the exact analogue of Amdahl's law (ch. 7) for optimization: the "sequential" part is no longer a program but the geometry of the network. All of ch. 4 (all-reduce, compression, overlap) is a war against this \(\sqrt{1/(1-\gamma)}\).
Proposition 2.3 — What parallelism can buy: the "finite sum" regime The bound of theorem 2.2 concerns the time to reach an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal point when the data are partitioned (each \(f_i\) is opaque). If instead all machines can evaluate the same function (replicated data), parallelism reduces the variance of SGD (mini-batching: Optimization for AI module, ch. 5) — but the optimal statistical rate bounds the gain (Dieuleveut et al., see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 11). In short: parallelism speeds up compute, never (or marginally) the statistical complexity — a distinction that commercial announcements like to forget.

3. Parallelism: data, tensor, pipeline

Three axes for splitting a training run — often combined (the "3D parallelism" of large systems). For each: the principle, the time decomposition, the communication cost.

Definition 3.1 — Data parallelism (DP) Each GPU holds a complete copy of the model, processes a different mini-batch (\(B/P\) examples), then the gradients are averaged by an all-reduce (ch. 4). Time per step (batch size \(B\), forward+backward time \(\tau_\mathrm{calc}(B)\), gradient of size \(S\) bytes): $$T_\mathrm{DP} \;=\; \underbrace{\tau_\mathrm{calc}(B/P)}_{\text{compute, divided by } P} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,(P-1)\,\alpha + \tfrac{2(P-1)}{P}\tfrac{S}{b}}_{\text{ring all-reduce (ch. 4)}}.$$ Strong scalability (at fixed \(B\)): the compute term shrinks, the communication term grows — the balance point defines the effective batch size; hence increasing \(B\) with the step (LARS: Optimization for AI module, ch. 10). This is the default mode of PyTorch DDP.
Definition 3.2 — Tensor parallelism (TP — Megatron, Shoeybi et al. 2019) One splits the matrices of the model: the layer \(Y = XW_1 W_2\) (MLP block) is distributed column/row-wise: each GPU holds a slice \(W_1^{[i]}, W_2^{[i]}\), computes \(X W_1^{[i]}\) (no communication: column split), then an all-reduce reassembles \(Y = \sum_i XW_1^{[i]} W_2^{[i]}\)... precisely: the column-then-row split makes the MLP block communication-frugal (1 all-reduce forward + 1 backward per block), attention is split per head. TP lives on the fastest links (intra-node NVLink): every layer pays a communication — this is the "mandatory high bandwidth" parallelism.
Theorem 3.3 — Pipeline parallelism: the bubble (proved) \(p\) stages (consecutive layers on each GPU), \(m\) micro-batches, each micro-batch costs \(\tau\) per stage. Under "1F1B" scheduling (one forward pass, one backward pass, staggered), the total time is $$T_\mathrm{pipe} \;=\; (m + p - 1)\,\tau, \qquad \eta \;=\; \frac{m}{m + p - 1} \;\xrightarrow[m \to \infty]{}\; 1,$$ where \(\eta\) is the efficiency; the "bubble" (stage idle time) accounts for \(\frac{p-1}{m+p-1}\) of the total.
Fill-up: the first micro-batch crosses the \(p\) stages in \(p\,\tau\) (stage \(j\) starts at \(j\tau\)); afterwards each following micro-batch exits every \(\tau\) units (the stages work staggered): the \(m\)-th exits at \((p + m - 1)\tau\). Ideal work (without bubble): each stage processes \(m\) micro-batches, i.e. \(m\tau\) busy; efficiency is the ratio \(m\tau / (m+p-1)\tau\). Each stage stays idle for \((p-1)\tau\) in total (fill-up and drain combined), i.e. a fraction \((p-1)/(m+p-1)\) of the total time. ∎

4. Communication: all-reduce and friends

The real bottleneck of distributed training is almost never compute: it is the exchange of gradients. A single operator to truly know — the all-reduce — and two reductions: bandwidth or latency.

Definition 4.1 — All-reduce \(P\) machines each hold a vector \(g_i\) of \(S\) bytes; all must receive \(\bar g = \frac1P\sum_i g_i\) (the gradient average in DP). Homogeneous network: latency \(\alpha\) per message, bandwidth \(b\) bytes/s.
Theorem 4.2 — Ring all-reduce: bandwidth-optimal (proved) Arranging the \(P\) machines in a ring and cutting the vector into \(P\) chunks of \(S/P\) bytes, the all-reduce proceeds in two phases (reduce-scatter then all-gather) of \(P-1\) steps each: $$T_\mathrm{ring} \;=\; 2\,(P-1)\,\alpha \;+\; \frac{2(P-1)}{P}\cdot\frac{S}{b} \;\xrightarrow[P \to \infty]{}\; 2\alpha P + \frac{2S}{b}.$$ The bandwidth term becomes independent of \(P\) — each machine never sends more than \(\approx 2S/P\) ... that is, at most \(2S(P-1)/P < 2S\) bytes in total: optimal up to constants.
Reduce-scatter. At step \(t\), machine \(i\) sends a chunk (already partially summed) to \(i+1\) and receives from \(i-1\) a different chunk which it adds to its own: after \(P-1\) steps, machine \(i\) holds the complete sum of chunk \(i\) — each machine has sent \((P-1)\cdot S/P\) bytes. All-gather. Same round in broadcast direction: the complete sums circulate, \((P-1)\cdot S/P\) bytes sent per machine. Total per machine: \(2(P-1)S/P\) bytes at rate \(b\), that is \(2(P-1)S/(Pb)\) — and the critical path is indeed the sequence of \(2(P-1)\) steps of latency \(\alpha\). ∎
Proposition 4.3 — Tree all-reduce: latency-optimal In a binary tree: reduction in \(\log_2 P\) steps then broadcast in \(\log_2 P\) steps, but each step transmits the entire vector: \(T_\mathrm{tree} = 2\log_2 P\,(\alpha + S/b)\). For small \(S\) (short gradients, many small tensors): the tree wins (latency); for large \(S\) (LLM gradients): the ring wins (bandwidth). Real libraries (NCCL) choose dynamically — and compose partial rings over the actual topology (intra NVLink, inter IB).
Three wars against \(\gamma\): overlap, bucketing, compression
  • Overlap: while the GPU computes layer \(k+1\), the gradient of layer \(k\) is already on the network (async by buckets); provided \(T_\mathrm{comm} \leq T_\mathrm{calc}\), communication disappears from the critical path — the raison d'être of PyTorch DDP's "bucketing".
  • Gradient compression: quantizing the gradients (8 bits, even 1 bit) before exchange divides \(S\) by 2–16; error feedback (compensating the quantization error at the next step) preserves convergence — at the price of a delicate variance/theory trade-off (see the Emerging ML module, ch. 2–3 for the federated case, where communication is the limiting factor).
  • Locality: reducing the number of communications (local steps, gossip) is exactly the strategy dictated by the bound of ch. 2: fewer rounds, but more steps — the optimum of Scaman et al. is a precise point on this frontier.

5. Memory efficiency: checkpointing, ZeRO, mixed precision

Theorem 5.1 — Activation checkpointing: \(O(\sqrt{L})\) memory (proved) A network of depth \(L\), activations \(a\) bytes per layer (per token/batch): (i) without precautions, the backward pass requires \(M_\mathrm{full} = L\,a\) bytes of activations; (ii) by storing only the inputs of a partition into \(\sqrt{L}\) segments of \(\sqrt{L}\) layers and recomputing each segment during the backward pass (gradient checkpointing, Chen et al. 2016): $$M_\mathrm{ckpt} \;\approx\; 2\sqrt{L}\,a \;+\; O(a), \qquad \text{additional cost} \approx +\tfrac{1}{3}\ \text{of time (one extra forward pass per segment).}$$
Memory. We store \(\sqrt L\) boundary activations; during the backward pass of a segment, we replay its local forward: at most \(\sqrt L\) simultaneous activations inside the segment (which can itself be recursively checkpointed: the memory/time trade-off can be tuned over the whole range \([a, La]\), see the \(\sqrt\) "compass"). Total \(\sqrt L + \sqrt L = 2\sqrt L\) live activations. Time. Each layer is recomputed exactly once more in the forward direction: the forward pass accounts for \(1/3\) of the total cost (theorem 1.3: 2ND vs 6ND), hence about +33%. ∎
Definition 5.2 — ZeRO (Rasley et al. 2020): partitioning the states Instead of replicating the training states on every GPU (classical DP: \(16N\) bytes per GPU), ZeRO partitions them:

Mixed precision: the "free" compression Computing in bf16 (2 bytes) instead of fp32 (4): half the memory and (on tensor cores) 2–8× the throughput; fp32 is kept for accumulators and master weights (long sums and small updates; historical fp16 additionally required loss scaling — see the Deep Learning module, ch. 10). Theorem 1.3 remains valid: we count FLOPs, precision changes the price of a FLOP. Integer quantization (int8/int4) only serves inference — ch. 6; its error analysis is in the Optimization and Responsible AI for Sustainability module, ch. 3 (proved proposition: error \(\leq \sum_\ell \frac{\Delta_\ell}{2}\prod_{m>\ell}\mathrm{Lip}_m\)).

6. Efficient inference

At scale (millions of requests), inference dominates the total cost: it deserves its own tools. Four levers, from the simplest to the deepest.

Definition 6.1 — The memory-bandwidth wall of autoregressive inference Generating one token requires rereading all the weights (2N bytes in bf16) and the KV cache — the keys/values of past layers, to be kept for attention: $$\text{KV cache} \;=\; 2\;(\text{K and V})\times n_\mathrm{layers}\times n_\mathrm{ctx}\times d_\mathrm{model}\times 2\ \text{bytes} \;=\; 4\,n_\mathrm{layers}\,n_\mathrm{ctx}\,d_\mathrm{model}\ \text{bytes (bf16)}.$$ For 7B (32 layers, \(d = 4096\), context 2048): \(4 \times 32 \times 2048 \times 4096 \approx 1{,}1\) GB — more than some believe, and growing with the context. Peak throughput (one GPU, memory bandwidth \(B_\mathrm{mem}\)): $$\text{tokens/s} \;\lesssim\; \frac{B_\mathrm{mem}}{2N\ \text{bytes}} \qquad (\text{e.g. } 2\,\text{TB/s} \div 14\,\text{GB} \approx 140\ \text{tok/s}).$$ This ceiling — and not FLOPs — governs token-by-token generation (ch. 7, roofline); batching fills the compute units without moving the wall.

7. Real systems: roofline, GPU, and the complete budget of a 7B LLM

Definition 7.1 — Roofline model (why a "312 TFLOP/s" GPU delivers 45%) An operation has an arithmetic intensity \(I = \mathrm{FLOPs}/\mathrm{bytes\ read+written}\). The achievable throughput is $$P_\mathrm{achievable} \;=\; \min\big(P_\mathrm{peak},\; B_\mathrm{mem}\cdot I\big)$$ with \(P_\mathrm{peak}\) the compute throughput and \(B_\mathrm{mem}\) the memory bandwidth. A huge GEMM (large \(I\)): compute-bound; an activation, a normalization, a tokenizer, token-by-token generation (low \(I\)): memory-bound — increasing the GPU's FLOP/s changes nothing there. The 35–50% MFU of LLMs is precisely the share of layers actually in the compute regime; FlashAttention (prop. 6.2) wins by reducing bytes, not FLOPs — a pure roofline move.
Proposition 7.2 — Amdahl's law (proved) — the ceiling of all parallelism If a fraction \(s\) of the work is sequential (inevitably so), the speedup on \(P\) processors is capped: $$A(P) \;=\; \frac{1}{s + (1-s)/P} \;\leq\; \frac{1}{s}.$$
Sequential time \(T_1 = T_s + T_p\) with \(s = T_s/T_1\). On \(P\) processors, the parallel part divides at best by \(P\): \(T_P = T_s + T_p/P\), hence \(A(P) = T_1/T_P = 1/(s + (1-s)/P)\), increasing in \(P\) and bounded above by \(1/s\). ∎

Complexity of neural networks: Lipschitz regularity
  • A. Virmaux, K. Scaman, Lipschitz regularity of deep neural networks: analysis and efficient estimation, NeurIPS 2018 — proving that computing exactly the Lipschitz constant of a deep network is NP-hard, and designing the estimator (SeqLip, layer-wise power iteration) that became standard. The link with this module: the propagation bound of prop. 6.2/Responsible AI 3.2 involves precisely these \(\mathrm{Lip}_m\) constants — and the paper says how far one can (not) compute them; on the robustness side, the Lipschitz constant is the certificate of adversarial guarantees (see the Emerging ML module, ch. 1).

Exercises

  1. Counting. Prove the "per token" formula \(24d^2 + 4nd\) of a transformer block (prop. 1.2), then verify theorem 1.3 on a GPT-2 small (\(N \approx 124\)M, 12 blocks, \(d = 768\)): compute \(C\) for \(D = 3\)B tokens and compare with a torch.profiler (or flops-profiler) profile within ±10%.   3. All-reduce. Prove theorem 4.2 on the reduce-scatter (induction on the steps); then plot \(T_\mathrm{ring}\) and \(T_\mathrm{tree}\) (prop. 4.3) for \(S = 1\) GB, \(\alpha = 2\,\mu s\), \(b = 25\) GB/s, \(P = 2 \dots 512\) on a log scale. Crossover? Which mode for a bf16 LLM-13B gradient? Partial solution: the ring wins as soon as \(S/P \gg \alpha b\).
  4. Checkpointing. A model with 30 blocks, activations 1.2 GB per block, 10 GB of memory available for activations. (a) Verify that full-recompute does not fit; (b) apply theorem 5.1: how many segments, what memory, what time overhead? (c) Generalize: for a memory budget \(M\), show that the time overhead is \(\Theta(L\,a/M)\) recomputed activations — and not \(+\tfrac13\) — when \(M \ll \sqrt{L}\,a\).
  5. Inference memory wall. (a) Recover the KV cache formula (def. 6.1) for a model with grouped-query attention (GQA: \(g\) head groups instead of \(h\)) — what saving? (b) Compute the peak throughput of a 13B fp16 on an A100 (2 TB/s) and on a 1 TB/s GPU; (c) show that int4 exactly doubles this ceiling (and why batching changes nothing there). Solution: (a) factor \(g/h\); (b) ≈ 77 then 38 tok/s; (c) in int4, the weights go from \(2N\) to \(N/2\) bytes — the wall thus exactly doubles.
  6. Roofline. A kernel reads \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) and \(w \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and computes \(Xw\) (\(2mn\) FLOPs, \((mn + n + m)\times4\) bytes). (c) Deduce why autoregressive decoding is memory-bound and what batching changes there (hint: \(I\) grows with batch size — but not the wall per token).
