This module is the “research” continuation of the M1 Graph ML course: beyond message passing, it questions what GNNs can and cannot express — the Weisfeiler-Lehman test —, what gets squashed when they go deeper — over-squashing —, and how research responds: positional encodings, graph transformers, temporal GNNs, up to scaling and robustness. Each chapter starts from a limitation of the foundation and builds the answer that recent years of research have given it.   • Decide between a message passing GNN and a graph transformer, and justify the choice.

0. Background: the message passing foundation

Review of the Graph ML module (M1 P2) — what is assumed known
  • Generic layer (M1, ch. 5): \(h_v^{(\ell+1)} = \phi\big(h_v, \mathrm{AGG}_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}\{\psi(h_v, h_u)\}\big)\); GCN (renormalized averaging, spectral derivation), GraphSAGE (sampling, inductive), GAT (edge attention);
  • Spectral (M1, ch. 4): Laplacian \(L = D - A\), variational characterization of \(\lambda_2\), spectral clustering as a RatioCut relaxation, convolution by Chebyshev polynomials — filtering = a polynomial of \(L\);
  • Embeddings (M1, ch. 3): DeepWalk/node2vec and their NetMF reading (factorization of random-walk matrices); the PathNNs of ch. 8 are their generalization;
  • Link prediction / GAE (M1, ch. 6) and scale (M1, ch. 7: NeighborSampler, Cluster-GCN, GraphSAINT) — the ch. 6 below extends the latter;
  • Over-smoothing (M1, ch. 5): after many layers, all embeddings converge (proved on a regular graph: geometric convergence to the mean) — why one stops at 2-4 layers… and why this module exists.
The module's four research questions (1) How far can a GNN see? — expressive power (ch. 1); (2) How far can information travel? — over-squashing and depth (ch. 2); (3) Where am I in the graph? — position and structure (ch. 3); (4) At what scale and under what attacks? — systems (ch. 6) and robustness (ch. 7). Transformers (ch. 4) and the temporal dimension (ch. 5) answer "all-all" and "as time goes by" to these same questions.

1. Expressive power: the Weisfeiler-Lehman test

Definition 1.1 — 1-WL (color refinement) Initialize a color \(c_v^{(0)}\) from the features; iterate: $$c_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{HASH}\Big(c_v^{(k)},\ \mathrm{SORT}\big\{c_u^{(k)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\big\}\Big).$$ Two graphs are 1-WL-equivalent if the multisets of colors coincide at convergence. 1-WL distinguishes almost all random graphs (Babai-Erdős-Selkow); but fails on simple families: regular graphs of the same degree, cycles, pairs of molecules discussed since the 1960s (CIR: chemical identity recognition).
Theorem 1.2 — GNN ≤ 1-WL (Xu et al., 2019; Morris et al., 2019) Any message passing GNN whose aggregation and update are functions (continuous, computable) cannot distinguish two 1-WL-equivalent graphs: if the initial colors coincide and the layers saturate the test, the embeddings coincide at every depth.
By induction on \(\ell\). Initialization: initial colors = features, identical by assumption, so \(h_v^{(0)} = h_{v'}^{(0)}\) for every pair \((v, v')\) of the same color. Heredity: if all nodes of color \(c\) have the same representation \(h_c\), then the input of the aggregation at \(v\) is the multiset \(\{h_c : c \in \mathrm{multicol}_v\}\); now 1-WL-equivalence = equality of color multisets and of neighbor color multisets at each iteration — so AGG receives identical inputs, and \(h_v^{(\ell+1)} = h_{v'}^{(\ell+1)}\). In the end, the readouts (permutation-invariant by construction, M1 ch. 2) see only the multiset of the \(h_v\) — identical on both graphs. ∎
Theorem 1.3 — GIN reaches exactly 1-WL (Xu et al., 2019) With sum + MLP and discrete features, $$h_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\Big((1 + \epsilon^{(k)})\, h_v^{(k)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(k)}\Big)$$ the GNN distinguishes exactly what 1-WL distinguishes.
The ingredient: on a countable domain, there exists an injective function \(f : \text{finite multisets} \to \mathbb{R}\): encoding each element by an integer (canonical log of a prime: \(c \mapsto \log p_c\)) gives \(\sum_i \log p_{c_i}\), and the uniqueness of prime factorization ensures injectivity; a universal MLP approximates it. Then the sum+MLP layer is injective in \((h_v, \mathrm{multiset}(h_u))\) — the pair (own color, neighbor multiset) is encoded without collision: by induction, two nodes have the same embedding \(\iff\) same 1-WL color. The injective sum readout concludes: GIN = 1-WL. ∎
Immediate consequences: mean and max are not injective — \(\{a, a, a\}\) and \(\{a\}\) have the same mean; \(\{a, a\}\) and \(\{a\}\) the same max — so GCN/GraphSAGE (mean) are strictly less powerful than GIN on categorical features; in practice the difference shows up mainly on tasks where counting neighbors matters (molecules: \(\mathrm{CH}_4\) vs \(\mathrm{CH}_3\)-\(\mathrm{CH}_3\)).
Example 1.4 — C6 vs 2×C3: 1-WL fails, 2-WL separates (demonstration) 1-WL: all nodes have degree 2; by induction, at each round each node sees (its color, {its color, its color}) — the colors remain uniform on each graph, and identical between the two graphs: indistinguishable. 2-WL (pair coloring): consider an adjacent pair \((u, v)\). In the triangle, \(\{w : w \sim u, w \sim v\}\) counts 1 common vertex; in C6, this count is 0 (no triangles); the 2-WL update rule, which aggregates for the pair \((u,v)\) the colors of the pairs \((u, w)\) and \((w, v)\) for all \(w\), encodes the number of triangles — the pair histograms differ: separated. ∎ Moral: 2-WL (hence a 2-GNN) "counts the moments" that the local sum cannot see. To code in the lab (exercise 1).
k-WL and k-GNN hierarchy k-WL colors k-tuples of nodes: \(k = 2\): pairs (above); the hierarchy is strict: (k+1)-WL distinguishes pairs that k-WL confuses (for \(k \geq 1\), 2-WL already being strictly stronger than 1-WL); at the top, k = n+1 → exact isomorphism test (exponential). The k-GNNs (Morris et al., 2019) implement (a local variant of) k-WL: messages between neighboring k-tuples — memory and compute cost \(\mathcal{O}(n^k)\): k = 2 practical, k = 3 reserved for small graphs (molecules). Morris et al.'s survey (2023) draws the complete map (Weisfeiler-Lehman "go machine learning").

Pragmatic alternatives: subgraphs and motif counting
  • ESGNN (Equivariant Subgraph Aggregation, Bevilacqua et al., 2022): each node is described by the collection of subgraphs (ego-nets of increasing radius, or "node-deletion" — the graph deprived of \(v\)); a shared 1-WL GNN encodes them, aggregation across subgraphs adds expressive power without full \(\mathcal{O}(n^k)\) cost — strictly between 1-WL and 3-WL;
  • Motif counting (Bouritsas et al., 2020): precompute counts of isomorphic subgraphs (triangles, paths, cycles) and inject them as features — "moments matter": raising expressive power by preprocessing rather than by architecture; direct link with Example 1.4 (triangles) and with the MMDs of generative evaluation (M1, ch. 9.6);
  • Practical limits: more expressive power = more estimation variance and cost; on real benchmarks (Dwivedi et al., 2023), GIN vs GCN is decided by tenths — expressive power is the bottleneck only on structural tasks (counting, distinguishing) — cf. ch. 6.

2. Over-squashing and deep GNNs

Definition 2.1 — Over-squashing (Alon & Yahav, 2021) — formal definition via Jacobians Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the computation graph of a GNN; the influence of a node \(u\) on the final representation of a node \(v\) is measured by the Jacobian \(\big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\|\). There is over-squashing when this quantity is crushed exponentially with the distance \(d(u, v)\) — precisely, when the bound \(\big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\| \leq c\, (f \, r(\lambda_{\max}))^{-d(u,v)}\) becomes small because the radius of convergence \(r(\lambda_{\max})\) of the propagation (an increasing function of the largest eigenvalue of the propagation operator, e.g. \(2\tilde L_{\mathrm{sym}} / \lambda_{\max}\)) is close to 1: an exponentially large quantity of information (\(\sim\) growth rate \(\mu\) of the neighborhood) must pass through a channel that contracts exponentially fast. Bottlenecks (cut edges, bridges between communities) are the physical points of the phenomenon.
Proposition 2.2 — The expansion / \(\lambda_{\max}\) link (proof sketch) The edges crossed by the information from \(u\) to \(v\) form a cut; the "flow" of message passing across a cut is bounded by the conductance of the cut, itself controlled by the spectrum: by Cheeger (inverted for the top of the spectrum, Tanner's bound for \(\lambda_{\max}\)), a weak conductance forces a large \(\lambda_{\max}\)… that is, highly oscillating components which, in the Chebyshev recurrence (M1, ch. 4), make the factor \(\big( \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{2} \big)^k\) diverge and crush the radius of convergence: topological bottleneck ⇔ poor spectral conditioning. Hence the two families of remedies: change the topology (rewiring, below) or change the operator (normalization, implicit propagation, ch. 2.4).
2.3 — Discrete Ricci curvature and SDRF (Topping et al., 2022) The Ollivier-Ricci curvature of an edge \((u,v)\) compares neighborhoods by optimal transport: $$\kappa(u, v) = 1 - \frac{W_1(\mu_u, \mu_v)}{d(u, v)}, \qquad \mu_u = \text{uniform law on } \mathcal{N}(u),$$ \(W_1\) = Wasserstein-1 distance (Optimal Transport module): "locally dense" edges (triangles) → large \(\kappa\); bridges → negative \(\kappa\). Worked example (by hand):
  • edge of a triangle: \(\mu_u = \{\frac12 v, \frac12 w\}\), \(\mu_v = \{\frac12 u, \frac12 w\}\); transport plan: keep \(w\) (cost 0), transport \(\frac12\) from \(v\) to \(u\) (cost 1) → \(W_1 = \frac12\), \(\kappa = +\frac12\);
  • edge of a hexagon C6: the best plan transports \(v \to t\), \(s \to u\) (cost 1 each) → \(W_1 = 1\), \(\kappa = 0\);
  • bridge edge between two triangles (barbell): the best plan pays \(W_1 = \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 + 3) = \frac53\), so \(\kappa = -\frac23 < 0\) — the bridge is "hyperbolic": it is the bottleneck.
SDRF (Spectral-De-Ricci-Flattening): at each iteration, (1) find the edge \((u,v)\) of minimal curvature; (2) add the edge \((x, y)\) between two neighbors of the edge (not yet present) that maximizes the sum of curvatures — triangulate the bottleneck; (3) remove the edge of maximal curvature if it exceeds a threshold (avoid densification); effect: the curvature distribution flattens, \(\lambda_{\max}\) drops, the bound of Definition 2.1 improves. Looking at the graph as a geometric object — curvatures, distances, volumes — is one of the field's great turns (cf. ch. 9 and the Geometric DL module).
DIGL — diffusion rewiring (Gasteiger et al., 2021) A "filtering" alternative: replace \(A\) by \(\tilde A = \mathrm{top}\text{-}k\big(\sum_{i=0}^{K} \beta_i T_i(L)\big)\) — a sparsified heat kernel (sum of Chebyshev polynomials, M1 ch. 4): each node gains "virtual" edges toward its multihop neighbors weighted by the diffusion; simple, differentiable, and the final GNN trains on the rewired graph. Comparison: SDRF is topological (local curvature, targeted additions); DIGL is spectral (global diffusion); both target \(r(\lambda_{\max})\) — and both may destroy motif information: measure the trade-off in the lab (exercise 4).
2.4 — Deep GNNs: APPNP and GCNII APPNP (Klicpera et al., 2019) separates predicting from propagating: a network (often simple) predicts \(H = f_\theta(X)\); propagation follows personalized PageRank: $$Z^{(k+1)} = (1 - \alpha)\, \hat P\, Z^{(k)} + \alpha H, \qquad \hat P = \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}.$$ Fixed point: \(Z^* = \alpha \big(I - (1 - \alpha)\hat P\big)^{-1} H\) — the series expansion \(\sum_{k} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k \hat P^k\) shows that the effective depth is infinite without stacking parameters; the teleport \(\alpha H\) bounds the gains (like PageRank damping, M1 ch. 0) and kills over-smoothing (M1, ch. 5.3); \(K\) iterations = truncation of the series. GCNII (Chen et al., 2020) makes GCN truly deep through two mechanisms: $$h^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big( \big[(1-\gamma_\ell) I + \gamma_\ell \hat P\big]\big[(1-\beta_\ell) I + \beta_\ell W_\ell\big] h^{(\ell)} + \gamma_\ell \hat P h^{(0)} \Big)$$ — initial residual toward \(h^{(0)}\) (the same remedy as APPNP's teleport) and mapped identity \((1 - \beta_\ell) I\) (as in ResNet); with \(\beta_\ell\) increasing with depth, 64 layers become trainable. Positioning: rewiring (2.3) changes the channel; APPNP/GCNII change the dynamics; graph transformers (ch. 4) remove the bottleneck by making the channel all-all; over-smoothing (representation) and over-squashing (channel) remain two distinct limits that must be diagnosed separately.

3. Positional and structural encodings

Problem: GNNs "do not know where they are" Message passing is equivariant (M1, ch. 2): two nodes with isomorphic neighborhoods have the same embedding — correct… but insufficient: the global position (distance to the center, membership in a cycle, bridge role) is informative. Transformers had the same problem (token order) and solved it with positional encodings — we re-import the idea (and its pathologies).

3.1 — LapEig: the ambiguities, and SignNet to lift them Taking \(p_v = [u_2(v), \dots, u_k(v)]\) (coordinates of \(v\) on the eigenvectors) suffers from three pathologies: (i) sign: \(-u_i\) is also an eigenvector — two runs give opposite encodings; (ii) basis: if \(\lambda_i\) is multiple, any rotation of the basis is admissible; (iii) instability: a small perturbation of the graph crosses the eigenvalues and makes the basis jump. SignNet (Lim et al., 2022) builds functions invariant to sign and basis: $$\rho(V) = h\Big( \sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi(v_i) + \phi(-v_i) \Big),$$ with \(\phi, h\) MLPs: enforcing the parity \(\phi(v) + \phi(-v)\) makes \(\rho\) even (sign invariance), and the sum over the basis gives invariance under rotation; the completeness theorem (in the spirit of results on orbit invariants): SignNet universally represents the continuous functions invariant in the eigenvectors — the exact analogue of Deep Sets' "sort before sum" (ch. 1). In practice: LapEig + SignNet is the default PE of spectral graph transformers.
Example 3.2 — RWPE computed by hand (and why it matters) The random-walk positional encoding (RWSE, Dwivedi et al., 2020) of a node \(v\) is the vector of return probabilities \(\big[ (P)_{vv}, (P^2)_{vv}, \dots, (P^K)_{vv} \big]\), \(P = D^{-1} A\). Computation:
  • node of a triangle: \((P^2)_{vv} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac12\) (back-and-forths); \((P^3)_{vv} = 2 \times (\frac12)^3 = \frac14\) (going around the triangle, in both directions);
  • node of a hexagon C6: \((P^2)_{vv} = \frac12\) — identical (same back-and-forths); but \((P^3)_{vv} = 0\): a return of odd length requires an odd cycle, and C6 has none; the first "full tour" appears only at \(k = 6\), with \((P^6)_{vv} = 2 \times (1/2)^6 = \frac{1}{32}\).
Now C3 and C6 are exactly the pair that 1-WL cannot distinguish (Example 1.4): the RWSE — a single scalar, \(p_3\), suffices here (\(\frac14\) versus \(0\)) — separates them immediately. Structural PEs "buy" expressive power at cost \(\mathcal{O}(|E| K)\); this is why the winning models of positional benchmarks (ch. 4) systematically carry a PE.
3.3 — SE(3) invariants for molecules, and the theory of positions In 3D (ch. 9), the encoding must respect rotations/translations/reflections: invariants = interatomic distances \(\|x_i - x_j\|\), angles, dihedrals; it is the continuous version of the principle "use only what is invariant". The theoretical question: how many coordinates to inject without breaking isomorphism invariance? A perfect positional encoding should distinguish the nodes of a regular graph (where 1-WL is constant) while remaining invariant under automorphism — canonical encodings (canonical numbering) make the learning variance explode; the practical compromise: relative encodings (RWSE, anchor distances, the SPD bias of ch. 4) — never absolute coordinates.
The inductive vs expressive debate Positional encodings partially "break" equivariance — a delicate choice between positional information and invariance guarantees. In industry (evolving graphs), one favors inductive PEs (generalizable to new nodes/graphs — RWSE, anchoring); in chemistry, exact invariants dominate. Cross-reference: Graphormer's SPD bias (ch. 4) is a relative PE — the only category that survives both constraints.

4. Graph transformers

Idea: all-all attention + structural bias Global attention \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) removes bottlenecks and over-squashing at once (ch. 2); but without an encoding of the graph, a transformer on features alone is blind to structure: the topology must be reinjected via (i) positional encodings (ch. 3) and (ii) additive attention biases in the scores — structure steers attention rather than masking it.
4.1 — Graphormer (Ying et al., 2021) — the three structural encoders
  1. centrality encoding: an embedding of the degree (in/out if directed) added to the features — degree is the first "where am I";
  2. spatial encoding: a bias learned per shortest-path distance: $$\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K)_{ij} = \frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d}} + b_{\phi(d_{ij})}, \qquad d_{ij} = \mathrm{SPD}(i, j), \quad b_{\phi(\cdot)} \text{ learned (table)},$$ with a dedicated value for \(d_{ij} = \infty\) (disconnected nodes); the bias is additive before the softmax — structure conditions attention;
  3. edge encoding: on the shortest path \(i \rightsquigarrow j\), the mean of the embeddings of the crossed edges is added to the score — for molecules, the type of the bonds along the path matters.
Plus a global virtual node (connected to all) for the readout. Result: 1st rank on OGB-LSC PCQM4M (3.7M molecules) at its release. Implementation: 
import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch_geometric.utils import shortest_path_distance   # PyG >= 2.6 (or homemade BFS)

class GraphormerLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, h, max_spd=5):
        super().__init__()
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, h, batch_first=True)
        self.bias = nn.Embedding(max_spd + 2, h)     # b_phi table: SPD -> h heads
        self.ffn = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, 2*d), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(2*d, d))
    def forward(self, x, spd):                       # spd: (n, n) integer distances
        b = self.bias(spd.clamp(max=5).long()).permute(2, 0, 1)  # (h, n, n)
        x = self.attn(x, x, x, attn_bias=b)[0] + x
        return self.ffn(x) + x
PyG trick: the per-head bias is built with an embedding table indexed by the distance matrix (exercise 5).
4.2 — GPS (Rampasek et al., 2022): the two channels, layer by layer $$H^{(\ell+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}\Big( \mathrm{BN}\big(\underbrace{\mathrm{MSA}(H^{(\ell)} + \mathrm{PE})}_{\text{global channel}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{MPNN}(H^{(\ell)}, A)}_{\text{local channel}}\big) \Big)$$ — global attention (all-all; kills over-squashing) + local message passing (locality bias, convolution); PE injected at the input (ch. 3); graph batches by concatenation-padding. Per-layer complexity: \(\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)\) (attention) + \(\mathcal{O}(|E| d)\) (MPNN) — the global channel dominates. GPS is a recipe: any MPNN (GCN, GINE, GAT) and any PE (RWSE, LapEig+SignNet) plug into it — hence its status as the reference framework.
4.3 — Efficient transformers: Exphormer and sampled attention For large \(n\) (ogbn-products…), \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) no longer fits; Exphormer (Shirzad et al., 2023) replaces full attention with a sparse attention graph: (i) the edges of the real graph; (ii) the neighbors of a random \(d\)-regular expander graph — an expander has logarithmic diameter: information crosses the graph in \(\mathcal{O}(\log n)\) layers, precisely what ch. 2 demands (expansion!); (iii) global virtual nodes. Complexity linear in \(n + |E|\); combined with the local MPNN, this gives "Exphormer-GPS", the scalable state of the art. Generalization: sampled attention (neighborhood, kernels, hashing) — the same recipes as the long-context transformers of the M2 LLMs module, transposed: the graph transformer and the LLM live the same algorithmic life.
4.4 — When is an MPNN enough? (decision guide)
  • homophilous graph, local dependencies (Cora, transactional fraud): GCN/GAT 2-3 layers — no point paying \(n^2\);
  • motifs, distances, long-range dependencies (molecules, QA over KGs): transformer or ESGNN (ch. 1) — structure exceeds the neighborhood;
  • large \(n\): MPNN + sampling (ch. 6) or Exphormer;
  • empirical benchmark rule (Dwivedi et al., 2023): at equal size, the gain of the transformer over MPNN+PE is real but modest — the hybrid (GPS) wins most often; the PE is the dominant factor, the architecture second.
LLGA connection: "attention = complete dynamic graph" versus "GNN = attention on a given graph" (M1, ch. 5); Graphormer is the synthesis — a given and learned attention — the transformers ↔ GNN convergence is THE guiding thread of the master.

5. Dynamic and temporal graphs

Two formalisms: snapshots vs event-based
  • snapshots: \(G_1, G_2, \dots, G_T\) (graph aggregated per window); models: GCN+RNN (encode each snapshot, make recurrent); simple, but the granularity loses the order within the window;
  • event-based: the stream of timestamped interactions \((u, v, t_1), (u, w, t_2), \dots\) — the "true" formulation of the problem; it is the regime of fraud (millisecond) and sequential recommendation.
5.1 — TGAT (Xu et al., 2020): encoding time The first notable event-based architecture injects time into attention: a learned sinusoidal temporal encoding (in the spirit of Time2Vec) $$\phi(\Delta t) = \big[\cos(\omega_1 \Delta t), \sin(\omega_1 \Delta t), \dots, \cos(\omega_d \Delta t), \sin(\omega_d \Delta t)\big], \qquad \omega_i \text{ learned},$$ concatenated with the features; attention is computed over the strictly past neighborhood \(\mathcal{N}(v; t) = \{u : (u, v, t') \in E, t' \leq t\}\) — a causal GAT; inductive by construction (new nodes = new times).
5.2 — TGN (Rossi et al., 2020): the modules and their equations TGN factorizes temporal memory:
  1. memory \(s_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\): a recurrent state per node, summarizing its history;
  2. message: at each event \((i, j, t)\), \(m_i^t = \mathrm{msg}(s_i(t^-), s_j(t^-), \Delta t, e_{ij})\) (likewise for \(j\), symmetric);
  3. update: \(s_i(t) = \mathrm{GRU}\big(s_i(t^-),\ \mathrm{agg}(\{m_i^{t'} : t' \leq t\})\big)\) — only the nodes touched by the batch are updated; "at rest" nodes receive a placeholder message to age their memory;
  4. embedding: \(z_i(t)\) computed by a TGAT-type module over the memory \(\{s_u(t^-) : u \in \mathcal{N}(i; t)\}\) + the time encoding \(\phi\) — memory is a compressed summary, the embedding an attentive reading.
Training: strictly causal — batches are ordered by time, and the masked teacher forcing technique (memorizing states between batches without backprop through the whole history, but "replaying" the stored states) makes the gradient practical; future link prediction loss: score \((i, j, t)\) against negatives (M1, ch. 6, temporal version). The paper's ablation: every module counts — remove memory, and everything collapses on distant interactions.
5.3 — LLGA application: real-time fraud The transaction graph is event-based by nature: a fraudulent card must be blocked in seconds; the pipeline: (i) TGN encodes the accounts (memory = past behavior); (ii) a head classifier reads \(z_i(t)\) at the moment of the transaction; (iii) the negatives are old legitimate transactions — beware temporal contamination (train strictly before \(t\): the temporal-split hygiene of the Emerging ML module); (iv) in production: incremental memory update, model drift to monitor. Lab datasets: Wikipedia edits / Reddit (TGN standards), and anonymized transaction banks. Variants: CAWN (attention by causal walks), compressed memorization; the state-of-the-art survey lives on the temporal graph learning side.

6. Scale: sampling, systems

What is assumed known (M1, ch. 7)
NeighborSampler (fanouts, expansive neighborhood \(1 + s_L(1 + s_{L-1}(\dots))\)), Cluster-GCN (METIS partitioning, partition bias), GraphSAINT (subgraphs + bias-free coverage normalization), and the bias/variance/memory triangle. Here: the systems layer and experimental hygiene.
  • Multi-GPU and partitioning: beyond one GPU, partition the graph (METIS/vertex-cut) across several machines; boundary edges are duplicated (shadow nodes) — the distributed training of the Emerging ML module applies almost as is (parameter server vs all-reduce); PinSage (M1, ch. 6) is the industrial reference;
  • Caching and pipelining: neighborhood sampling is a system problem (I/O, reuse of subgraphs across batches — hence PinSage's importance sampling that freezes the entry points); the frameworks: DGL, PyG + torch_geometric.sampler, Quiver;

7. Robustness and privacy on graphs

7.1 — Nettack (Zügner et al., 2018): gradient attack, in detail The scenario: evasion (the attacker modifies the graph at test time) on a target \(u\) and its class \(y_u\); budget: \(\Delta\) modifications (edge additions/removals, feature changes). The attacker's dilemma: the model is not differentiable in the discrete structure. Nettack's solution:
  1. train a linearized surrogate (one-layer GCN, softmax); its output \(Z_u(A)\) is then polynomial in \(A\) — continuous, differentiable;
  2. first-order score: for each candidate modification \(\delta\) (flip of an edge \((a,b)\) or of a feature), $$\mathrm{score}(\delta) = -\nabla_{A}\, \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(Z_u(A)\big)\big|_{(a,b)} \cdot \big(\delta_{ab} - A_{ab}\big),$$ the Taylor approximation of the loss change (product of the gradient with the direction of the change); keep the modification with maximal score;
  3. iterate (modify, re-score) until the budget; "discretion" constraints: add only plausible edges (degrees, common features).
Historic result: a few flips suffice to flip the prediction — and the attacks transfer to the true GCN (the surrogate suffices). The LLGA lesson: in fraud detection, the attacker knows the model (or its shadow); robustness is a design requirement, not an option.
7.2 — Poisoning: Meta-Self (Zügner & Günnemann, 2019) An attack at training time (poisoning): the graph is corrupted before learning. Bilevel: $$\min_{\delta}\ \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(f_{\theta^*}(A + \delta)\big) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{train}\big(f_\theta(A + \delta)\big),$$ solved by meta-gradient: a few inner gradient steps are unrolled (differentiable), the outer gradient traces back to \(\delta\) — the same machinery as the MAML of the RL/Deep Learning module. A "self-labeled" variant (Meta-Self): the attacker also controls a few labels. Structural defenses: pruning (attack edges are often "suspicious" — large feature distances); purification (Pro-GNN: learn a clean graph \(A'\) regularized close to \(A\) while training the GNN); robust GCN (RGCN, Zhu et al., 2019): Gaussian layers whose variance absorbs the attack + attention over reliable neighbors.
7.3 — Certification: sketch Guarantee that no attack of budget \(\Delta\) changes the prediction: by randomized smoothing applied to graphs (majority over graphs perturbed by random flips) or by spectral bounds (certificates tied to \(\lambda_{\max}\) of ch. 2 — same currency); the state of the art certifies small budgets on moderate GCNs: in practice, one combines partial certification + the defenses above + monitoring (detecting edge-addition anomalies).
7.4 — Privacy: node-DP, edge-DP, reidentification
  • edge-DP: the basic mechanism is randomized response — flip each edge with probability \(p\), calibrated for \((\varepsilon, 0)\)-DP; counting queries (how many edges between two groups?) receive Laplace noise of sensitivity 1;
  • node-DP: much harder — sensitivity explodes with the degree (removing a node removes \(d\) edges); recipe: project the graph onto a graph of bounded degree \(k\) (keep the \(k\) "most important" neighbors), then apply the edge mechanisms; the price: bias on hubs;
  • reidentification: graph embeddings (M1, ch. 3) are fingerprints — re-routing an "anonymized" node into a public graph by comparing neighborhoods (attacks by degrees, by subgraphs — the Narayanan-Shmatikov attack transposed); GraphRAG (M1, ch. 11) aggregates scattered facts: the reidentification risk grows with the quality of the graph;
  • evaluation: measure the utility-privacy trade-off (AUC vs \(\varepsilon\)); cross-reference with the M2 security module for LLMs (prompt injection over the KG, leakage by generation).
7.5 — Structural fairness Degree bias: low-degree nodes (minorities in social networks, new customers) are predicted less well — fewer messages = less signal; remedies: structural features (RWSE, ch. 3), virtual edges, degree-based resampling, reweighted losses; open question: structural fairness (communities) vs individual fairness (nodes) can conflict — the exact counterpart of the fairness tensions of the Optimal Transport module (transporting node distributions; homophily bias: a homophilous GNN propagates label biases).

8. Research of the teaching team

The DaSciM laboratory (LIX, École Polytechnique — M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer) is the master's host laboratory: this chapter studies its papers in depth, positioning them relative to the program. General-audience introductions were given in M1 (ch. 10); here we go into the machinery.

8.1 — Path Neural Networks, in depth (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Starting point: the canonical GNN layer aggregates 1-hop neighbors (M1, ch. 5); stacking \(L\) layers amounts — when all goes well — to aggregating walks of length \(L\); but (i) walks repeat nodes (backtracking — redundant information); (ii) composing layers degrades (over-smoothing/squashing, ch. 2); (iii) 1-WL — hence the GNN (th. 1.2) — aggregates only multisets of colors, without the sequence of the route. The path operator: for each node \(v\), enumerate simple paths (without repetition) starting from \(v\), of length \(\ell\); encode each path with a GRU, aggregate: $$m_p = \mathrm{GRU}\big(h_{u_\ell}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{GRU}(\dots \mathrm{GRU}(h_{u_1}^{(0)}, h_v^{(0)}) \dots )\big), \qquad h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\big\{ m_p : p \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} \big\}.$$ Three variants bound the enumeration cost:
  • PathNN-s (shortest paths): \(\mathcal{P}_v\) = shortest paths to all destinations (at most \(n - 1\) paths, \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) GRUs per node); captures distances — two nodes "close to everyone" versus "peripheral" separate;
  • PathNN-r (random walks): \(\mathcal{P}_v\) = sampled walks — the bridge with DeepWalk/NetMF (M1, ch. 3);
  • PathNN-n (neighborhood sampling): locally sampled simple paths — the practical compromise for large graphs.
Complexity: \(\mathcal{O}(P \cdot \ell \cdot d^2)\) per node (\(P\) sampled paths) — of the same order as a GAT if \(P \sim \bar d\). Results: on graph classification (standard benchmarks including molecular ones — ZINC and TU datasets), PathNN matches or exceeds GIN/GCN; on tasks where the geometry of routes carries the signal (molecules: paths are chemical chains!), the gain is clear. Theoretical positioning: simple paths carry information that walk multisets ignore (recall: C6 vs 2C3, Example 1.4 — triangles are read in paths of length 3); ordered GRU aggregation is a special case of GOAT's "order + sequence" family (below); and PathNN is the subject of the "paper" exercise (exercise 7).
8.2 — GOAT (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) Seen in M1 (ch. 10) on the generation side; on the expressive power side: the invariant aggregator \(\mathrm{AGG}\) (th. 1.2-1.3) bounds the GNN to 1-WL precisely because it is invariant; GOAT learns a local order by attention (scoring neighbors by \((h_v, h_u)\), sorting), then processes the sequence with an RNN — a sequence model inside the graph: strictly more expressive than invariant aggregation (it distinguishes identical distributions of a multiset as soon as the order carries signal), while remaining globally equivariant (the order is a learned function of the representations, not an external numbering). Positioning in M2: an architectural answer to the question of ch. 1 — gain expressive power without paying \(\mathcal{O}(n^k)\); and an answer to ch. 3 — the local order is a learned positional encoding.
8.3 — NGG (Evdaimon et al., 2024): conditioned latent generation The bridge M1-ch. 9 ↔ diffusion: a GNN encoder projects graphs into a compact latent space; a diffusion model (M1, ch. 9.5 — Multimodal GenAI) learns the distribution there conditioned on structural features (spectrum, degrees, counts); the decoder regenerates graphs exhibiting the requested features. The interest for M2: latent diffusion bypasses discretization (continuous diffusion in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) rather than over DiGress's categories), and the spectral conditioning connects generation to ch. 2 (the spectrum governs communication — here, it governs the shape of the generated graphs). LLGA applications: faithful synthetic data (pre-training!), distributional anonymization, anomaly detection (a real graph far from the latent support).
8.4 — Pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024) Presented in M1 (ch. 11) as a GraphRAG tool; the M2 reading: a benchmark of algorithmic reasoning — LLMs execute graphs described in pseudo-code better than in natural language (systematic gains); to be connected with neural algorithmic reasoning (ch. 9): two ways of making algorithms run on graphs — by a prompted LLM, or by a trained GNN.

9. LLGA bridges: 3D, algorithmic reasoning, GNN × LLM

9.1 — 3D molecules: EGNN and E(n) equivariance (Satorras, Hoogeboom & Welling, 2021) The positions \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) make the molecular graph geometric; physics is invariant under rotations/translations (E(3)); a GNN that respects this symmetry by construction learns better with less data (Bronstein et al., the Geometric DL "charter"). EGNN: $$m_{ij} = \phi_e\big(h_i, h_j, \|x_i - x_j\|^2, e_{ij}\big),$$ $$x_i^{(\ell+1)} = x_i^{(\ell)} + C \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} (x_i^{(\ell)} - x_j^{(\ell)})\, \phi_x(m_{ij}),$$ $$h_i^{(\ell+1)} = \phi_h\big(h_i^{(\ell)}, \textstyle\sum_{j} m_{ij}\big).$$ Equivariance proof (sketch): under \(x \mapsto Rx + t\) (\(R\) orthogonal), the distances \(\|x_i - x_j\|\) are invariant, hence so are the \(m_{ij}\); the position step \(\sum_j (x_i - x_j)\phi_x\) transforms into \(R \sum_j (x_i - x_j)\phi_x\) — exact equivariance; the factor \(C\) bounds the step. Applications: 3D property prediction, learned molecular dynamics, 3D generation (EDM — equivariant diffusion; the M2 Geometric DL module generalizes to groups; M1 ch. 9 covers the 2D version).
9.2 — Neural algorithmic reasoning (Veličković et al.) Learning to execute algorithms with GNNs: encode inputs as a graph (sorting: a list = a comparison graph; shortest paths: the graph itself), execute via message passing layers, decode outputs — the black-box replaces the code. The CLRS benchmark (Veličković et al., ICML 2023) tests faithful execution of classical algorithms (CLRS = Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein); results: GNN processors generalize to unseen sizes better than transformers on certain families (structural induction); the LLGA lesson: a learned algorithm is differentiable (it can be inserted into an end-to-end trained pipeline: routing, scheduling, matching) and approximately robust (noisy inputs); at the price of exactness — the trade-off to master. Loop with Refresher CS: what more can a "learned algorithm" do than an algorithm? Answer: train on data, slot into gradient descent — and the pseudo-code prompting of ch. 8.4 is the dual path (the LLM simulates the execution; NAR embodies it).
9.3 — GNN × LLM: injected encoders and benchmarks Three injection architectures (M1, ch. 11.2, deepened):
  • structural tokens: the outputs of a GNN (node/graph embeddings) are projected into the LLM's embedding space as soft tokens; the LLM reads structure as a language;
  • structural recall: the GNN (or the graph traversal) retrieves the relevant facts, the LLM writes — RAG becomes "graph-RAG"; in both cases, the question of the alignment of the spaces arises (contrastive: bring the GNN embedding closer to the text describing the same node);
  • LLM annotator: the LLM enriches node features (summaries, types) for the GNN — the graph becomes text-attributed (the "TAG" regime); benchmarks: NLGraph (serialized reasoning — M1, ch. 11.2), Talk like a Graph (encodings: none dominates everywhere), and the industrial GraphRAG suites.
Industrial GraphRAG (summary; details in M1, ch. 11): LLM extraction → entity graph → Leiden communities + summaries → local/global queries; the M2's role: the quality of the graph (robust communities — ch. 7; KGC completion — M1 ch. 6) is the system's bottleneck; the rare specialty the master builds: both GNN theory and the LLM pipeline.
9.4 — What the master expects of you here Knowing how to choose: MPNN vs transformer (ch. 4), with or without PE (ch. 3), deep or rewired (ch. 2), certified or not (ch. 7) — and knowing how to prove the theorems of ch. 1, reproduce the experiments of ch. 8, and read a paper of the field in one hour. This is the "entry-level research / advanced R&D" level.

Exercises

  1. 1-WL by hand. Run 1-WL on C6 and 2×C3 and verify indistinguishability (detail Example 1.4); then on the "house" graph (square + roof) versus the pentagon; conclude on the exact family of indistinguishable pairs. Partial solution: house vs pentagon — all nodes of degree 2 except the roof top (degree 3); the pentagon is 2-regular, the house is not → 1-WL distinguishes them from iteration 1 (initial color = degree); generalization: 1-WL fails exactly on certain pairs of regular graphs with the same degree sequence (more finely: same iteration tree; connected 2-regular graphs = cycles, all indistinguishable from one another).
  2. GIN vs GCN. Show that the mean is not injective on multisets (give the counterexamples {a, a, a} vs {a} and {a, a, b, b} vs {a, b}) and that sum+MLP is (discrete features); check experimentally: train GIN and GCN on a 1-WL-indistinguishable pair and measure. Partial solution: mean: {a, a, a} → a and {a} → a (collision); discrete sum: encode a by \(\log 2\), b by \(\log 3\) — \(\{a,a,b,b\} \mapsto 2\log 2 + 2\log 3 = \log 36\) and \(\{a, b\} \mapsto \log 6\) — no collision by uniqueness of factorization (th. 1.3); in practice, the mean does better on continuous real features — discuss why (estimation variance).
  3. Minimal 2-GNN. Implement a 2-GNN on pairs (representation \(h_{ij}\), messages between adjacent pairs) and verify that it separates C6 and 2C3 (disjoint readout outputs) where GIN fails. Hint: start with hashed colors (exact 2-WL) before learning.
  4. Over-squashing. Build two "communities" connected by a single bottleneck node; measure the classification performance of a GCN as a function of the distance to the bottleneck; compare GPS (ch. 4) and GCN + virtual edges (DIGL); plot \(\lambda_{\max}\) before/after rewiring. Partial solution (expected): gradient of accuracy with distance for the GCN; GPS nearly uniform; after DIGL, \(\lambda_{\max}\) drops by several percent and the gap narrows — the \(\lambda_{\max}\) vs accuracy curve is the graph to produce.
  5. LapEig. Compute the first 2 nontrivial eigenvectors of the Laplacian of the karate club graph; project the nodes; verify that the visualized separation ≈ the actual partition; rerun 5 times and observe the sign flips; implement the SignNet correction (ch. 3.1) and measure stability (distance between the encodings of two runs). Partial solution: \(\rho(V) = \mathrm{MLP}(\sum_i \phi(v_i) + \phi(-v_i))\) — the sum of the two parities is invariant under flips; compare with the baseline "sign fixed by the first positive component" (unstable at nodes of near-zero value).
  6. RWSE by hand and in code. Recover the computations of Example 3.2 (triangle vs hexagon), then compute the RWSEs (\(K = 4\)) of all nodes of the karate club graph; show that two 1-WL-equivalent nodes of a regular graph get the same RWSE — and explain why this is both a feature (invariance under automorphism) and a bug (expressive power).
  7. Graphormer-PE. Implement the attention bias by shortest-path distance (distance matrix by BFS; learned bias table — skeleton in ch. 4.1); train on ogbg-molhiv and compare GCN / GIN / Graphormer-mini; report means ± standard deviations over 5 seeds. Partial solution: BFS \(\mathcal{O}(n(|V| + |E|))\) per graph — precompute; clamp distances to 5 (Pareto law of molecular SPDs); expected: a small but systematic gain of the SPD bias over GCN.
  8. Temporal. On a standard TGN dataset (Wikipedia/Reddit): train a TGN (pytorch_geometric.nn.models.TGNMemory library or the paper's code); ablation: remove memory (keep the TGAT embedding); plot the AUC of future link prediction as a function of the horizon \(\Delta t\). Partial solution (expected): memory dominates on recent interactions; without memory, the AUC drops by several points at small \(\Delta t\) (the information of the last events is no longer compressed).
  9. Attack. Implement an attack by removing/adding \(k\) edges targeting a node (gradient over nonexistent edges — ch. 7.1, linearized surrogate); measure the prediction flip rate vs budget; test a defense (RGCN or training on perturbed graphs). Partial solution: score = |gradient of the attack loss per edge|; take the top-k; the surrogate suffices (transfer); with RGCN, the required budget roughly doubles — never zero; conclude on certification (ch. 7.3).
  10. Edge-DP. Prove that randomized response (flipping each edge with probability \(p\)) is \((\varepsilon, 0)\)-DP with \(\varepsilon = \log \frac{p}{1-p}\) (analyze the two output mechanisms per edge); deduce the equivalent Laplace noise for an edge count. Solution: for two graphs neighboring in one edge, the likelihood ratio of any output is bounded by \(\frac{p}{1-p}\) (worst case: the flipped edge); hence the \(\varepsilon\); counting: each edge contributes Bernoulli(\(1-p\)) vs Bernoulli(\(p\)), the sum of \(m\) Bernoullis has binomial noise — approximated by Laplace of variance \(m\,p(1-p)\), i.e. scale \(\sqrt{m\,p(1-p)}\).
  11. Research project. Reproduce an experiment from one of (PathNN / GOAT / GPS / TGN / NGG) on OGB or TU; report: protocol, seeds, comparison to the published numbers, failure analysis — the M2's "paper" exercise; for PathNN: compare the three variants (s/r/n) on a molecular dataset and explain the observed hierarchy via ch. 8.1.
