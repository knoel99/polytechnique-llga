7. Robustness and privacy on graphs

7.1 — Nettack (Zügner et al., 2018): gradient attack, in detail The scenario: evasion (the attacker modifies the graph at test time) on a target \(u\) and its class \(y_u\); budget: \(\Delta\) modifications (edge additions/removals, feature changes). The attacker's dilemma: the model is not differentiable in the discrete structure. Nettack's solution:
  1. train a linearized surrogate (one-layer GCN, softmax); its output \(Z_u(A)\) is then polynomial in \(A\) — continuous, differentiable;
  2. first-order score: for each candidate modification \(\delta\) (flip of an edge \((a,b)\) or of a feature), $$\mathrm{score}(\delta) = -\nabla_{A}\, \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(Z_u(A)\big)\big|_{(a,b)} \cdot \big(\delta_{ab} - A_{ab}\big),$$ the Taylor approximation of the loss change (product of the gradient with the direction of the change); keep the modification with maximal score;
  3. iterate (modify, re-score) until the budget; "discretion" constraints: add only plausible edges (degrees, common features).
Historic result: a few flips suffice to flip the prediction — and the attacks transfer to the true GCN (the surrogate suffices). The LLGA lesson: in fraud detection, the attacker knows the model (or its shadow); robustness is a design requirement, not an option.
7.2 — Poisoning: Meta-Self (Zügner & Günnemann, 2019) An attack at training time (poisoning): the graph is corrupted before learning. Bilevel: $$\min_{\delta}\ \mathcal{L}_{att}\big(f_{\theta^*}(A + \delta)\big) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{train}\big(f_\theta(A + \delta)\big),$$ solved by meta-gradient: a few inner gradient steps are unrolled (differentiable), the outer gradient traces back to \(\delta\) — the same machinery as the MAML of the RL/Deep Learning module. A "self-labeled" variant (Meta-Self): the attacker also controls a few labels. Structural defenses: pruning (attack edges are often "suspicious" — large feature distances); purification (Pro-GNN: learn a clean graph \(A'\) regularized close to \(A\) while training the GNN); robust GCN (RGCN, Zhu et al., 2019): Gaussian layers whose variance absorbs the attack + attention over reliable neighbors.
7.3 — Certification: sketch Guarantee that no attack of budget \(\Delta\) changes the prediction: by randomized smoothing applied to graphs (majority over graphs perturbed by random flips) or by spectral bounds (certificates tied to \(\lambda_{\max}\) of ch. 2 — same currency); the state of the art certifies small budgets on moderate GCNs: in practice, one combines partial certification + the defenses above + monitoring (detecting edge-addition anomalies).
7.4 — Privacy: node-DP, edge-DP, reidentification
  • edge-DP: the basic mechanism is randomized response — flip each edge with probability \(p\), calibrated for \((\varepsilon, 0)\)-DP; counting queries (how many edges between two groups?) receive Laplace noise of sensitivity 1;
  • node-DP: much harder — sensitivity explodes with the degree (removing a node removes \(d\) edges); recipe: project the graph onto a graph of bounded degree \(k\) (keep the \(k\) "most important" neighbors), then apply the edge mechanisms; the price: bias on hubs;
  • reidentification: graph embeddings (M1, ch. 3) are fingerprints — re-routing an "anonymized" node into a public graph by comparing neighborhoods (attacks by degrees, by subgraphs — the Narayanan-Shmatikov attack transposed); GraphRAG (M1, ch. 11) aggregates scattered facts: the reidentification risk grows with the quality of the graph;
  • evaluation: measure the utility-privacy trade-off (AUC vs \(\varepsilon\)); cross-reference with the M2 security module for LLMs (prompt injection over the KG, leakage by generation).
7.5 — Structural fairness Degree bias: low-degree nodes (minorities in social networks, new customers) are predicted less well — fewer messages = less signal; remedies: structural features (RWSE, ch. 3), virtual edges, degree-based resampling, reweighted losses; open question: structural fairness (communities) vs individual fairness (nodes) can conflict — the exact counterpart of the fairness tensions of the Optimal Transport module (transporting node distributions; homophily bias: a homophilous GNN propagates label biases).
