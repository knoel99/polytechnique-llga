3. Positional and structural encodings

Problem: GNNs "do not know where they are" Message passing is equivariant (M1, ch. 2): two nodes with isomorphic neighborhoods have the same embedding — correct… but insufficient: the global position (distance to the center, membership in a cycle, bridge role) is informative. Transformers had the same problem (token order) and solved it with positional encodings — we re-import the idea (and its pathologies).

3.1 — LapEig: the ambiguities, and SignNet to lift them Taking \(p_v = [u_2(v), \dots, u_k(v)]\) (coordinates of \(v\) on the eigenvectors) suffers from three pathologies: (i) sign: \(-u_i\) is also an eigenvector — two runs give opposite encodings; (ii) basis: if \(\lambda_i\) is multiple, any rotation of the basis is admissible; (iii) instability: a small perturbation of the graph crosses the eigenvalues and makes the basis jump. SignNet (Lim et al., 2022) builds functions invariant to sign and basis: $$\rho(V) = h\Big( \sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi(v_i) + \phi(-v_i) \Big),$$ with \(\phi, h\) MLPs: enforcing the parity \(\phi(v) + \phi(-v)\) makes \(\rho\) even (sign invariance), and the sum over the basis gives invariance under rotation; the completeness theorem (in the spirit of results on orbit invariants): SignNet universally represents the continuous functions invariant in the eigenvectors — the exact analogue of Deep Sets' "sort before sum" (ch. 1). In practice: LapEig + SignNet is the default PE of spectral graph transformers.
Example 3.2 — RWPE computed by hand (and why it matters) The random-walk positional encoding (RWSE, Dwivedi et al., 2020) of a node \(v\) is the vector of return probabilities \(\big[ (P)_{vv}, (P^2)_{vv}, \dots, (P^K)_{vv} \big]\), \(P = D^{-1} A\). Computation:
  • node of a triangle: \((P^2)_{vv} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac12\) (back-and-forths); \((P^3)_{vv} = 2 \times (\frac12)^3 = \frac14\) (going around the triangle, in both directions);
  • node of a hexagon C6: \((P^2)_{vv} = \frac12\) — identical (same back-and-forths); but \((P^3)_{vv} = 0\): a return of odd length requires an odd cycle, and C6 has none; the first "full tour" appears only at \(k = 6\), with \((P^6)_{vv} = 2 \times (1/2)^6 = \frac{1}{32}\).
Now C3 and C6 are exactly the pair that 1-WL cannot distinguish (Example 1.4): the RWSE — a single scalar, \(p_3\), suffices here (\(\frac14\) versus \(0\)) — separates them immediately. Structural PEs "buy" expressive power at cost \(\mathcal{O}(|E| K)\); this is why the winning models of positional benchmarks (ch. 4) systematically carry a PE.
3.3 — SE(3) invariants for molecules, and the theory of positions In 3D (ch. 9), the encoding must respect rotations/translations/reflections: invariants = interatomic distances \(\|x_i - x_j\|\), angles, dihedrals; it is the continuous version of the principle "use only what is invariant". The theoretical question: how many coordinates to inject without breaking isomorphism invariance? A perfect positional encoding should distinguish the nodes of a regular graph (where 1-WL is constant) while remaining invariant under automorphism — canonical encodings (canonical numbering) make the learning variance explode; the practical compromise: relative encodings (RWSE, anchor distances, the SPD bias of ch. 4) — never absolute coordinates.
The inductive vs expressive debate Positional encodings partially "break" equivariance — a delicate choice between positional information and invariance guarantees. In industry (evolving graphs), one favors inductive PEs (generalizable to new nodes/graphs — RWSE, anchoring); in chemistry, exact invariants dominate. Cross-reference: Graphormer's SPD bias (ch. 4) is a relative PE — the only category that survives both constraints.
