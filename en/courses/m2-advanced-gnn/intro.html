This module is the “research” continuation of the M1 Graph ML course: beyond message passing, it questions what GNNs can and cannot express — the Weisfeiler-Lehman test —, what gets squashed when they go deeper — over-squashing —, and how research responds: positional encodings, graph transformers, temporal GNNs, up to scaling and robustness. Each chapter starts from a limitation of the foundation and builds the answer that recent years of research have given it. Official sheet not published (SynapseS S3/S4 2026-2027 catalog empty): this material deepens the brochure announcement, and its own additions are flagged as deepenings.

What you will be able to do

  • Determine the expressive power of a GNN via the Weisfeiler-Lehman test and deduce what it cannot distinguish.
  • Diagnose over-squashing in deep GNNs and choose the appropriate remedies.
  • Choose a positional or structural encoding depending on the task and the graph.
  • Decide between a message passing GNN and a graph transformer, and justify the choice.
  • Handle dynamic and temporal graphs.
  • Scale a GNN: sampling, systems and trade-offs.
  • Analyze the robustness and privacy specific to relational data.
  • Situate the open bridges: 3D, algorithmic reasoning, GNN × LLM.

Where this course fits in the program

An M2 module (period 1), whose official teaching volume is not communicated. It builds directly on the message passing foundation of Graph Machine and Deep Learning for Generative AI (M1 P2), reactivated in chapter 0. Downstream, it prepares Graph Generative Models and Applications, which builds graphs rather than analyzing them, and Fundamentals of Security and Robustness for AI, whose adversarial attacks on graphs — like Nettack — extend the robustness chapter.

Course outline

  • Background: the message passing foundation — the M1 base GNN restated, to expose its limits.
  • Expressive power: the Weisfeiler-Lehman test — the exact bound of what a standard GNN can distinguish.
  • Over-squashing and deep GNNs — why information compresses poorly in deep architectures, and how to remedy it.
  • Positional and structural encodings — giving the graph what transformers have beyond message passing.
  • Graph transformers — attention applied to vertices: promises, costs and conditions of use.
  • Dynamic and temporal graphs — handling structures that evolve over time.
  • Scale: sampling, systems — making training fit on industrial graphs.
  • Robustness and privacy on graphs — attacking and defending models whose data is relational.
  • Research of the teaching team — the laboratory's papers, read with the module's grid.
  • LLGA bridges: 3D, algorithmic reasoning, GNN × LLM — where this research meets the master's other axes.
  • Exercises — expressiveness tests, encodings and scaling to carry out yourself.
  • References — the research papers underlying each chapter.

How to study this module

  • First secure the foundation: the review chapter 0 conditions everything else in the module.
  • Implement the Weisfeiler-Lehman test on small graphs: nothing makes expressive power as concrete as two indistinguishable graphs.
  • Read each founding paper after the corresponding chapter: the course is its map, not its substitute.
  • Connect the robustness chapter to the M2 security module: Nettack and its successors are studied there for their own sake.
  • Experiment with scaling on large datasets, and measure what sampling costs in quality.
  • Explore the bridges of the last chapter: this is where internship and PhD topics are recruited.
