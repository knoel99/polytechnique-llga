8. Research of the teaching team

The DaSciM laboratory (LIX, École Polytechnique — M. Vazirgiannis, J. Lutzeyer) is the master's host laboratory: this chapter studies its papers in depth, positioning them relative to the program. General-audience introductions were given in M1 (ch. 10); here we go into the machinery.

8.1 — Path Neural Networks, in depth (Michel, Nikolentzos, Lutzeyer & Vazirgiannis, ICML 2023) Starting point: the canonical GNN layer aggregates 1-hop neighbors (M1, ch. 5); stacking \(L\) layers amounts — when all goes well — to aggregating walks of length \(L\); but (i) walks repeat nodes (backtracking — redundant information); (ii) composing layers degrades (over-smoothing/squashing, ch. 2); (iii) 1-WL — hence the GNN (th. 1.2) — aggregates only multisets of colors, without the sequence of the route. The path operator: for each node \(v\), enumerate simple paths (without repetition) starting from \(v\), of length \(\ell\); encode each path with a GRU, aggregate: $$m_p = \mathrm{GRU}\big(h_{u_\ell}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{GRU}(\dots \mathrm{GRU}(h_{u_1}^{(0)}, h_v^{(0)}) \dots )\big), \qquad h_v^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{AGG}\big\{ m_p : p \in \mathcal{P}_v^{(\ell)} \big\}.$$ Three variants bound the enumeration cost:
  • PathNN-s (shortest paths): \(\mathcal{P}_v\) = shortest paths to all destinations (at most \(n - 1\) paths, \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) GRUs per node); captures distances — two nodes "close to everyone" versus "peripheral" separate;
  • PathNN-r (random walks): \(\mathcal{P}_v\) = sampled walks — the bridge with DeepWalk/NetMF (M1, ch. 3);
  • PathNN-n (neighborhood sampling): locally sampled simple paths — the practical compromise for large graphs.
Complexity: \(\mathcal{O}(P \cdot \ell \cdot d^2)\) per node (\(P\) sampled paths) — of the same order as a GAT if \(P \sim \bar d\). Results: on graph classification (standard benchmarks including molecular ones — ZINC and TU datasets), PathNN matches or exceeds GIN/GCN; on tasks where the geometry of routes carries the signal (molecules: paths are chemical chains!), the gain is clear. Theoretical positioning: simple paths carry information that walk multisets ignore (recall: C6 vs 2C3, Example 1.4 — triangles are read in paths of length 3); ordered GRU aggregation is a special case of GOAT's "order + sequence" family (below); and PathNN is the subject of the "paper" exercise (exercise 7).
8.2 — GOAT (Chatzianastasis, Lutzeyer, Dasoulas & Vazirgiannis, AAAI 2023) Seen in M1 (ch. 10) on the generation side; on the expressive power side: the invariant aggregator \(\mathrm{AGG}\) (th. 1.2-1.3) bounds the GNN to 1-WL precisely because it is invariant; GOAT learns a local order by attention (scoring neighbors by \((h_v, h_u)\), sorting), then processes the sequence with an RNN — a sequence model inside the graph: strictly more expressive than invariant aggregation (it distinguishes identical distributions of a multiset as soon as the order carries signal), while remaining globally equivariant (the order is a learned function of the representations, not an external numbering). Positioning in M2: an architectural answer to the question of ch. 1 — gain expressive power without paying \(\mathcal{O}(n^k)\); and an answer to ch. 3 — the local order is a learned positional encoding.
8.3 — NGG (Evdaimon et al., 2024): conditioned latent generation The bridge M1-ch. 9 ↔ diffusion: a GNN encoder projects graphs into a compact latent space; a diffusion model (M1, ch. 9.5 — Multimodal GenAI) learns the distribution there conditioned on structural features (spectrum, degrees, counts); the decoder regenerates graphs exhibiting the requested features. The interest for M2: latent diffusion bypasses discretization (continuous diffusion in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) rather than over DiGress's categories), and the spectral conditioning connects generation to ch. 2 (the spectrum governs communication — here, it governs the shape of the generated graphs). LLGA applications: faithful synthetic data (pre-training!), distributional anonymization, anomaly detection (a real graph far from the latent support).
8.4 — Pseudo-code prompting (Skianis, Nikolentzos & Vazirgiannis, 2024) Presented in M1 (ch. 11) as a GraphRAG tool; the M2 reading: a benchmark of algorithmic reasoning — LLMs execute graphs described in pseudo-code better than in natural language (systematic gains); to be connected with neural algorithmic reasoning (ch. 9): two ways of making algorithms run on graphs — by a prompted LLM, or by a trained GNN.
