2. Over-squashing and deep GNNs

Definition 2.1 — Over-squashing (Alon & Yahav, 2021) — formal definition via Jacobians Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the computation graph of a GNN; the influence of a node \(u\) on the final representation of a node \(v\) is measured by the Jacobian \(\big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\|\). There is over-squashing when this quantity is crushed exponentially with the distance \(d(u, v)\) — precisely, when the bound \(\big\| \partial h_v^{(L)} / \partial h_u^{(0)} \big\| \leq c\, (f \, r(\lambda_{\max}))^{-d(u,v)}\) becomes small because the radius of convergence \(r(\lambda_{\max})\) of the propagation (an increasing function of the largest eigenvalue of the propagation operator, e.g. \(2\tilde L_{\mathrm{sym}} / \lambda_{\max}\)) is close to 1: an exponentially large quantity of information (\(\sim\) growth rate \(\mu\) of the neighborhood) must pass through a channel that contracts exponentially fast. Bottlenecks (cut edges, bridges between communities) are the physical points of the phenomenon.
Proposition 2.2 — The expansion / \(\lambda_{\max}\) link (proof sketch) The edges crossed by the information from \(u\) to \(v\) form a cut; the "flow" of message passing across a cut is bounded by the conductance of the cut, itself controlled by the spectrum: by Cheeger (inverted for the top of the spectrum, Tanner's bound for \(\lambda_{\max}\)), a weak conductance forces a large \(\lambda_{\max}\)… that is, highly oscillating components which, in the Chebyshev recurrence (M1, ch. 4), make the factor \(\big( \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{2} \big)^k\) diverge and crush the radius of convergence: topological bottleneck ⇔ poor spectral conditioning. Hence the two families of remedies: change the topology (rewiring, below) or change the operator (normalization, implicit propagation, ch. 2.4).
2.3 — Discrete Ricci curvature and SDRF (Topping et al., 2022) The Ollivier-Ricci curvature of an edge \((u,v)\) compares neighborhoods by optimal transport: $$\kappa(u, v) = 1 - \frac{W_1(\mu_u, \mu_v)}{d(u, v)}, \qquad \mu_u = \text{uniform law on } \mathcal{N}(u),$$ \(W_1\) = Wasserstein-1 distance (Optimal Transport module): "locally dense" edges (triangles) → large \(\kappa\); bridges → negative \(\kappa\). Worked example (by hand):
  • edge of a triangle: \(\mu_u = \{\frac12 v, \frac12 w\}\), \(\mu_v = \{\frac12 u, \frac12 w\}\); transport plan: keep \(w\) (cost 0), transport \(\frac12\) from \(v\) to \(u\) (cost 1) → \(W_1 = \frac12\), \(\kappa = +\frac12\);
  • edge of a hexagon C6: the best plan transports \(v \to t\), \(s \to u\) (cost 1 each) → \(W_1 = 1\), \(\kappa = 0\);
  • bridge edge between two triangles (barbell): the best plan pays \(W_1 = \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 + 3) = \frac53\), so \(\kappa = -\frac23 < 0\) — the bridge is "hyperbolic": it is the bottleneck.
SDRF (Spectral-De-Ricci-Flattening): at each iteration, (1) find the edge \((u,v)\) of minimal curvature; (2) add the edge \((x, y)\) between two neighbors of the edge (not yet present) that maximizes the sum of curvatures — triangulate the bottleneck; (3) remove the edge of maximal curvature if it exceeds a threshold (avoid densification); effect: the curvature distribution flattens, \(\lambda_{\max}\) drops, the bound of Definition 2.1 improves. Looking at the graph as a geometric object — curvatures, distances, volumes — is one of the field's great turns (cf. ch. 9 and the Geometric DL module).
DIGL — diffusion rewiring (Gasteiger et al., 2021) A "filtering" alternative: replace \(A\) by \(\tilde A = \mathrm{top}\text{-}k\big(\sum_{i=0}^{K} \beta_i T_i(L)\big)\) — a sparsified heat kernel (sum of Chebyshev polynomials, M1 ch. 4): each node gains "virtual" edges toward its multihop neighbors weighted by the diffusion; simple, differentiable, and the final GNN trains on the rewired graph. Comparison: SDRF is topological (local curvature, targeted additions); DIGL is spectral (global diffusion); both target \(r(\lambda_{\max})\) — and both may destroy motif information: measure the trade-off in the lab (exercise 4).
2.4 — Deep GNNs: APPNP and GCNII APPNP (Klicpera et al., 2019) separates predicting from propagating: a network (often simple) predicts \(H = f_\theta(X)\); propagation follows personalized PageRank: $$Z^{(k+1)} = (1 - \alpha)\, \hat P\, Z^{(k)} + \alpha H, \qquad \hat P = \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}.$$ Fixed point: \(Z^* = \alpha \big(I - (1 - \alpha)\hat P\big)^{-1} H\) — the series expansion \(\sum_{k} \alpha(1-\alpha)^k \hat P^k\) shows that the effective depth is infinite without stacking parameters; the teleport \(\alpha H\) bounds the gains (like PageRank damping, M1 ch. 0) and kills over-smoothing (M1, ch. 5.3); \(K\) iterations = truncation of the series. GCNII (Chen et al., 2020) makes GCN truly deep through two mechanisms: $$h^{(\ell+1)} = \sigma\Big( \big[(1-\gamma_\ell) I + \gamma_\ell \hat P\big]\big[(1-\beta_\ell) I + \beta_\ell W_\ell\big] h^{(\ell)} + \gamma_\ell \hat P h^{(0)} \Big)$$ — initial residual toward \(h^{(0)}\) (the same remedy as APPNP's teleport) and mapped identity \((1 - \beta_\ell) I\) (as in ResNet); with \(\beta_\ell\) increasing with depth, 64 layers become trainable. Positioning: rewiring (2.3) changes the channel; APPNP/GCNII change the dynamics; graph transformers (ch. 4) remove the bottleneck by making the channel all-all; over-smoothing (representation) and over-squashing (channel) remain two distinct limits that must be diagnosed separately.
