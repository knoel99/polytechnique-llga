1. Simplices and simplicial complexes

A graph can only represent binary relations. To encode ternary relations (three points close to one another), quaternary ones… at the end of which live loops and cavities, we generalize edges and triangles: these are the simplices. The whole rest of the course rests on this single generalization.

Definition 1.1 — Geometric simplex Let \(k + 1\) points \(v_0, \dots, v_k\) of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) be affinely independent (no plane of dimension \(k - 1\) contains them all). The geometric \(k\)-simplex they span is their convex hull: $$\sigma = [v_0, \dots, v_k] = \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i v_i \;:\; \lambda_i \geq 0, \; \sum_i \lambda_i = 1 \Big\}.$$ The integer \(k\) is the dimension of \(\sigma\); the \(\lambda_i\) are the barycentric coordinates; the point \(\sum_i \frac{1}{k+1} v_i\) is the barycenter. Vocabulary: 0-simplex = point, 1-simplex = segment, 2-simplex = filled triangle, 3-simplex = filled tetrahedron.
Definition 1.2 — Faces and oriented boundary Any subfamily \(\{v_{i_0}, \dots, v_{i_{\ell}}\} \subseteq \{v_0, \dots, v_k\}\) spans a face of \(\sigma\), of dimension \(\ell\) — a proper face if \(\ell < k\). The oriented boundary of the \(k\)-simplex is the alternating formal sum of its codimension-1 faces: $$\partial [v_0, \dots, v_k] \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{k} (-1)^i\, [v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, v_k],$$ where the hat marks omission. Example: \(\partial[a, b] = b - a\) and \(\partial[a, b, c] = [b, c] - [a, c] + [a, b]\). Over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), all signs equal \(+1\): the boundary is the unoriented sum of the faces.
Proposition 1.3 — The boundary of a boundary is zero: \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\) For every \(k\)-simplex, \(\partial(\partial \sigma) = 0\), over \(\mathbb{Z}\) as well as over \(\mathbb{F}_2\).
Over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) (counting argument). Each \((k-2)\)-face \(\tau = [v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, \widehat{v_j}, \dots, v_k]\) of \(\sigma\) (with \(i < j\)) appears exactly twice in \(\partial(\partial \sigma)\): once as a face of face \(i\) (with \(v_j\) subsequently omitted) and once as a face of face \(j\) (with \(v_i\) subsequently omitted). Since \(1 + 1 = 0\) in \(\mathbb{F}_2\), all contributions cancel in pairs.

Over \(\mathbb{Z}\) (sign argument). Let us copy the two occurrences: in \(\partial[v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, v_k]\), the face \(\tau\) receives the sign \((-1)^{j-1}\) (the index of \(v_j\) has been shifted by one after the omission of \(v_i\), since \(i < j\)), and in \(\partial[v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_j}, \dots, v_k]\), it receives \((-1)^{i}\). The total contribution is \((-1)^i (-1)^{j-1} + (-1)^j (-1)^i = (-1)^i\big((-1)^{j-1} + (-1)^j\big) = 0\). ∎
The physical intuition of \(\partial^2 = 0\) The boundary of a segment is the difference of its endpoints; the boundary of a triangle walks around its oriented contour — each edge of the contour, taken in both directions, ends up canceled: the boundary of a boundary bounds nothing. This seemingly innocuous identity manufactures all of homology: it makes possible the question "which cycles are not boundaries?" — that is, "which loops genuinely enclose a hole?".
Definition 1.4 — Simplicial complex (geometric and abstract) A geometric simplicial complex \(K\) is a finite set of simplices of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) such that (i) every face of a simplex of \(K\) is in \(K\), and (ii) the intersection of two simplices of \(K\) is a common face (possibly empty). Its realization \(|K|\) is the union of its simplices, endowed with the induced topology. An abstract complex is a finite set \(K\) of finite subsets of a vertex set, stable under taking subsets: \(\sigma \in K\) and \(\tau \subseteq \sigma\) imply \(\tau \in K\). The \(k\)-skeleton of \(K\) gathers its simplices of dimension at most \(k\): the 1-skeleton is a graph (cf. Graph ML module, ch. 0).
Example 1.5 — Three complexes to know by heart
  1. The hollow triangle \(S\): 3 vertices \(a, b, c\), 3 edges, no face — it is a circle: \(|S| \simeq \mathbb{S}^1\).
  2. The filled triangle \(D\): we add the face \([a, b, c]\): \(|D|\) is a disk, contractible (\(|D| \simeq \{\ast\}\)). Same 1-skeleton, different shapes: the difference resides in dimension 2.
  3. The hollow sphere \(K_{\mathbb{S}^2}\): the boundary of the tetrahedron — 4 vertices, 6 edges, 4 triangular faces, nothing above. It is the most economical complex representing the sphere \(\mathbb{S}^2\); it possesses a cavity. We compute it entirely in ch. 3.
Proposition 1.6 — Every abstract complex admits a realization Every abstract complex \(K\) of dimension \(k\) admits a geometric realization in \(\mathbb{R}^{2k+1}\), unique up to homeomorphism.
(Sketch.) Embed the vertices of \(K\) on the moment curve \(t \mapsto (t, t^2, \dots, t^{2k+1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{2k+1}\). Any family of at most \(2k + 2\) points of this curve is affinely independent (a polynomial of degree \(2k\) having \(2k + 1\) roots is zero); two simplices of \(K\), which together have at most \(2(k+1)\) distinct vertices, are thus automatically in general position: their intersection is exactly the simplex spanned by the common vertices — a face of both. Uniqueness up to homeomorphism is more delicate (simplicial approximation theorem) and out of scope. ∎
Combinatorial control A complex with \(n\) vertices and dimension \(k\) has at most \(\binom{n}{k+1}\) simplices of dimension \(k\). This is the first practical difficulty of TDA: the number of simplices explodes with the dimension and density of the cloud; ch. 10 (software) is a battle against this explosion, which ripser and GUDHI win on the 1-skeleton (flag complexes, sparse boundary matrix).
