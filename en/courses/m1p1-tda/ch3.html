3. Simplicial homology

Program: turning geometry into algebra. At each dimension \(k\), we build a vector space of \(k\)-chains (formal combinations of \(k\)-simplices); the boundary operator \(\partial_k\) satisfies \(\partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0\) (Proposition 1.3). Homology measures the failure of exactness: cycles that are not boundaries.

Definition 3.1 — Chains, cycles, boundaries, homology Let \(K\) be a finite complex, \(\mathbb{F}\) a field (\(\mathbb{F}_2\) unless otherwise stated). For \(k \geq 0\), the group of \(k\)-chains is \(C_k(K) = \mathbb{F}^{f_k(K)}\), generated by the \(k\)-simplices. The boundary map \(\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}\) is the linear map that sends a simplex to the sum of its faces (signed over \(\mathbb{Z}\), raw over \(\mathbb{F}_2\)). We set: $$Z_k = \ker \partial_k \;(\text{cycles}), \qquad B_k = \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{k+1} \;(\text{boundaries}), \qquad H_k(K) = Z_k / B_k.$$ The inclusion \(B_k \subseteq Z_k\) follows from \(\partial_k \circ \partial_{k+1} = 0\). The dimension \(\beta_k = \dim H_k\) is the \(k\)-th Betti number. Two cycles are homologous if their difference is a boundary.
Reading the Betti numbers \(\beta_0\) = number of connected components (how many pieces); \(\beta_1\) = number of independent loops (how many "one-dimensional" holes: annulus, torus handle, hierarchy of cyclic communities); \(\beta_2\) = number of cavities (the interior of a hollow sphere, of a bubble); beyond that, \(\beta_k\) counts the \(k\)-holes. Over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), "cycle" means: every vertex (resp. edge…) is covered an even number of times — a union of closed polygons, without orientation.
Example 3.2 — Complete computation no. 1: the hollow then filled triangle Hollow triangle \(S\) (vertices \(a, b, c\), edges \(ab, bc, ca\), no face). \(C_0 = \mathbb{F}_2^3\), \(C_1 = \mathbb{F}_2^3\), \(C_2 = 0\). In the vertex basis: \(\partial_1(ab) = a + b\), \(\partial_1(bc) = b + c\), \(\partial_1(ca) = c + a\). The matrix of \(\partial_1\) (columns = edges, rows = vertices) is $$\partial_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathrm{rg}(\partial_1) = 2.$$ Indeed \(\mathrm{col}_1 + \mathrm{col}_2 + \mathrm{col}_3 = (0, 0, 0)^\top\) (each vertex appears twice) and any two columns are independent. Hence: $$\dim Z_1 = 3 - 2 = 1, \quad B_1 = 0 \Rightarrow H_1 \cong \mathbb{F}_2, \qquad \dim H_0 = 3 - 2 = 1.$$ Reading: one component, one loop — the circle. The generator cycle is \(\gamma = ab + bc + ca\).

Filled triangle \(D\) (we add the face \(abc\)). \(C_2 = \mathbb{F}_2\) and \(\partial_2(abc) = ab + bc + ca = \gamma\): the rank of \(\partial_2\) is 1, so \(B_1 = \langle \gamma \rangle\) and $$H_1(D) = Z_1 / B_1 = \langle \gamma \rangle / \langle \gamma \rangle = 0, \qquad H_0(D) \cong \mathbb{F}_2.$$ Reading: the disk has no hole — the loop that bounds it has become a… boundary. The whole topology of the "hollow → filled" passage lies in the rank of a \(1 \times 3\) matrix.
Example 3.3 — Complete computation no. 2: the hollow sphere (boundary of the tetrahedron) \(K_{\mathbb{S}^2}\): 4 vertices, 6 edges, 4 faces, no 3-simplex. Step by step over \(\mathbb{F}_2\):
  1. \(\partial_1 : C_1 = \mathbb{F}_2^6 \to C_0 = \mathbb{F}_2^4\). The complete graph \(K_4\) is connected: \(\mathrm{rg}\, \partial_1 = 3\), so \(\dim Z_1 = 6 - 3 = 3\) and \(H_0 \cong \mathbb{F}_2\) (a single component).
  2. \(\partial_2 : C_2 = \mathbb{F}_2^4 \to C_1 = \mathbb{F}_2^6\). Dimension-2 cycles. Let us look for families of faces in which every edge is covered an even number of times. Each edge of the tetrahedron belongs to exactly 2 faces. An isolated face leaves its 3 edges odd: forbidden. Two faces share one edge (even) but their 4 other edges remain odd: forbidden. Three faces: the edge carried by the two faces among the three that contain it… remains odd: forbidden. All 4 faces together: every edge covered twice — allowed. So \(Z_2 = \{\emptyset, \Sigma\}\) where \(\Sigma\) is the sum of the 4 faces: \(\dim Z_2 = 1\).
  3. \(B_2 = \mathrm{im}\, \partial_3 = 0\) (no 3-simplex), so \(H_2 \cong \mathbb{F}_2\) — the cavity.
  4. \(\mathrm{rg}\, \partial_2 = \dim C_2 - \dim Z_2 = 4 - 1 = 3\), so \(B_1\) has dimension 3 and \(H_1 = Z_1/B_1\) has dimension \(3 - 3 = 0\): no loop — on a sphere, every loop tightens to a point.
Balance sheet: \(\beta_0 = 1\), \(\beta_1 = 0\), \(\beta_2 = 1\); check by the characteristic: \(\chi = 4 - 6 + 4 = 2 = \beta_0 - \beta_1 + \beta_2\). The same computation on Császár's "minimal" torus (7 vertices, 21 edges, 14 faces) would give \((1, 2, 1)\).
Proposition 3.4 — Hole formula for graphs For a graph \(G\) with \(n\) vertices, \(m\) edges and \(c\) connected components, viewed as a 1-complex: \(\beta_0 = c\), \(\beta_1 = m - n + c\) (the nullity or cyclomatic rank — cf. Graph ML module, ch. 0).
\(\beta_0 = c\): two vertices are homologous (\(a + b = \partial(ab)\)) within the same component, and never across components. For \(\beta_1\): \(Z_1 = \ker \partial_1\) and \(\mathrm{rg}\, \partial_1 = n - c\) (one relation per spanning tree of each component — each connected component contributes exactly \(-1\) to the rank via its spanning tree), hence \(\beta_1 = m - (n - c) - 0\), since \(B_1 = 0\) for a 1-complex. ∎
Theorem 3.7 (Euler–Poincaré) — The characteristic is topological For every finite complex \(K\): $$\chi(K) \;=\; \sum_{k} (-1)^k \beta_k.$$ In particular \(\chi\) depends only on the homotopy type of \(|K|\) — Euler had \(\chi(\text{polyhedron}) = s - a + f\) for the sphere, Poincaré showed that the alternating sum of the Betti numbers always suffices.
For each \(k\), the rank theorem applied to \(\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}\) gives \(f_k = \dim C_k = \mathrm{rg}\, \partial_k + \dim \ker \partial_k = \mathrm{rg}\, \partial_k + \dim Z_k\). Now \(\dim Z_k = \dim B_k + \beta_k\) and \(\mathrm{rg}\, \partial_k = \dim B_{k-1}\); hence \(f_k = \dim B_{k-1} + \dim B_k + \beta_k\). Summing with the signs \((-1)^k\), the terms \(\dim B_k\) appear twice, with opposite signs, and cancel (telescoping); what remains is \(\sum_k (-1)^k \beta_k\). ∎

Integer coefficients and torsion Computing over \(\mathbb{Z}\) (signs active) restores information that \(\mathbb{F}_2\) hides: torsion, e.g. \(H_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\) whereas \(\beta_1 = 0\) over \(\mathbb{F}_2\). The price: replacing Gaussian elimination with the Smith normal form. In practice, applied TDA almost always works over a field; we will remember that the Betti numbers over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) underestimate the integral homological information.
Definition 3.8 — Singular homology: the general theory Simplicial homology assumes a finite complex. For an arbitrary topological space \(X\), one defines singular homology: a \(k\)-singular simplex is any continuous map \(\sigma : \Delta^k \to X\) from the standard simplex to \(X\); singular \(k\)-chains are the finite formal combinations of such simplexes, the boundary operator is computed by restricting \(\sigma\) to the faces of \(\Delta^k\), and \(H_k(X)\) follows as in 3.1. The price of generality is an infinite chain space — no direct computation possible; the gain is twofold. On the one hand \(H_k(X)\) depends only on the homotopy type of \(X\): it is singular homology that makes the Betti numbers true topological invariants, independent of the choice of triangulation. On the other hand, the simplicial approximation theorem guarantees that for \(X = |K|\), singular homology and simplicial homology coincide: TDA therefore always computes the finite (simplicial) version, by virtue of the general theory — and the official syllabus of the course cites both.
