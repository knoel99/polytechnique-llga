2. From point cloud to complex: Rips and Čech

Given a cloud \(\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) sampled "on" an unknown shape \(M\), how do we build a complex whose realization has the same shape as \(M\)? The idea: grow balls around the points and connect the balls that intersect. Two families stand out, with an accuracy/cost trade-off that Proposition 2.4 quantifies.

Definition 2.1 — Čech complex For \(r \geq 0\), the Čech complex \(\check{C}_r(\mathcal{X})\) contains the subsets \(\sigma \subseteq \mathcal{X}\) such that the intersection of the open balls is non-empty: $$\sigma = \{x_{i_0}, \dots, x_{i_k}\} \in \check{C}_r(\mathcal{X}) \iff \bigcap_{j=0}^{k} B(x_{i_j}, r) \neq \emptyset.$$ A simplex is in the complex as soon as its vertices share a common point; the parameter \(r\) is the radius.
Definition 2.2 — Vietoris–Rips complex For \(\varepsilon \geq 0\), the Vietoris–Rips complex \(\mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon(\mathcal{X})\) contains the subsets \(\sigma\) of diameter at most \(\varepsilon\): $$\sigma \in \mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon(\mathcal{X}) \iff \max_{x, y \in \sigma} \|x - y\| \leq \varepsilon.$$ It is the flag complex (or clique complex) of the scale-\(\varepsilon\) neighborhood graph: its simplices are exactly the cliques of this graph (cf. Graph ML module, ch. 0). The parameter \(\varepsilon\) is a distance threshold.
Theorem 2.3 (nerve theorem) — Čech ≃ union of balls Let \(\mathcal{U} = \{U_i\}_{i \in I}\) be a finite family of convex open sets of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (or more generally with contractible intersections). Then the nerve of the family — the abstract complex \(\mathrm{Nerf}(\mathcal{U}) = \{\sigma \subseteq I : \bigcap_{i \in \sigma} U_i \neq \emptyset\}\) — has the same homotopy type as the union: $$\big| \mathrm{Nerf}(\mathcal{U}) \big| \;\simeq\; \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i.$$ In particular \(\check{C}_r(\mathcal{X}) \simeq \bigcup_i B(x_i, r)\): the Čech complex is the inflated shape, in the homotopy sense.
(Idea, out of scope.) One constructs a partition-of-unity map \(\phi : \bigcup_i U_i \to |\mathrm{Nerf}(\mathcal{U})|\) which, to each point, associates the barycenter of the \(U_i\) containing it, weighted by continuous functions \(\psi_i\) vanishing outside \(U_i\). When the \(U_i\) and their intersections are contractible, Borsuk's theorem (or Weil's nerve lemma) ensures that \(\phi\) is a homotopy equivalence. Exercise 5 proves the one-dimensional case (intervals of \(\mathbb{R}\)). ∎
Proposition 2.4 — Rips and Čech bracket each other (Jung inequalities) In \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with the Euclidean norm, for every \(\varepsilon \geq 0\) and with the above conventions: $$\check{C}_{\varepsilon/2}(\mathcal{X}) \;\subseteq\; \mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon(\mathcal{X}) \;\subseteq\; \check{C}_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}}(\mathcal{X}).$$
First inclusion. If \(\sigma \in \check{C}_{\varepsilon/2}\), the balls \(B(x, \varepsilon/2)\), \(x \in \sigma\), have a common point \(p\); by the triangle inequality, \(\|x - y\| \leq \|x - p\| + \|p - y\| < \varepsilon\) for all \(x, y \in \sigma\), so \(\mathrm{diam}(\sigma) < \varepsilon\) and \(\sigma \in \mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon\).

Second inclusion. If \(\mathrm{diam}(\sigma) \leq \varepsilon\), Jung's theorem (1901) — every set of diameter \(\delta\) of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is included in a ball of radius \(\delta \sqrt{\frac{d}{2(d+1)}} \leq \frac{\delta}{\sqrt 2}\) — provides a point \(p\) at distance at most \(\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}\) from all vertices: \(\sigma \in \check{C}_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}}\). The constant \(\sqrt{d/(2(d+1))}\) is attained by the regular simplex of dimension \(d\) and tends toward \(1/\sqrt{2}\): the bracket therefore holds in every dimension with \(\sqrt{2}\), and is asymptotically optimal. ∎
Practical consequences
  1. Čech is exact, Rips is computable. Čech benefits from the nerve theorem (2.3); but testing whether \(k+1\) balls intersect is an optimization problem of dimension \(d\). Rips is content with the graph of \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhoods and then enumerates its cliques: the whole computation is combinatorial, independent of \(d\). Bracket 2.4 says that the price to pay for going from one to the other is a factor of at most \(\sqrt{2}\) on the parameter.
  2. \(\ell_\infty\) norm: de Silva and Ghrist (2007) show that under \(\ell_\infty\), \(\mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon = \check{C}_{\varepsilon/2}\) exactly — cubes lend themselves better than balls to the flag construction.
  3. Filtration, not complex: the right object is not a complex at one fixed \(\varepsilon\) but the increasing family \(\{\mathrm{Rips}_\varepsilon\}_{\varepsilon \geq 0}\) — the object of chapter 4. The reader will verify that \(\varepsilon \leq \varepsilon'\) implies the inclusion of the complexes: this is a filtration in the sense of Definition 4.1.
Example 2.5 — Three equidistant points Let \(\mathcal{X}\) consist of three points of an equilateral triangle of side \(s\). For \(\varepsilon < s\) and \(r < s/2\): three isolated vertices, three components. For \(s \leq \varepsilon < \frac{2s}{\sqrt 3}\) and \(\frac{s}{2} \leq r < \frac{s}{\sqrt 3}\): the hollow triangle — a loop \(\mathbb{S}^1\). For \(\varepsilon \geq \frac{2s}{\sqrt 3}\) (circumradius: \(s/\sqrt{3}\), hence \(\varepsilon = 2r\)): the filled triangle, contractible. The Rips threshold \(\varepsilon = 2s/\sqrt 3\) indeed corresponds to the \(\check{C}\) of radius \(s/\sqrt{3} = \varepsilon/\sqrt 2\)… and the example attains exactly bracket 2.4: the loop is born in Rips before existing in Čech.
Definition 2.6 — Euler–Poincaré characteristic The Euler characteristic of a finite complex \(K\) is the alternating sum $$\chi(K) \;=\; \sum_{k \geq 0} (-1)^k\, f_k(K),$$ where \(f_k\) counts the simplices of dimension \(k\) (the \(f\)-vector). It is the most rustic topological invariant: \(\chi(\text{point}) = 1\), \(\chi(\mathbb{S}^1) = 0\), \(\chi(\mathbb{S}^2) = 2\), \(\chi(\text{torus}) = 0\). Theorem 3.7 will show that \(\chi\) depends only on the homotopy type of \(|K|\), and will relate it to the Betti numbers.
