5. Diagrams, barcodes and landscapes

The barcode is a combinatorial object; to confront it with statistics — comparing, testing, averaging — it must be given a geometric habitat. Two representations dominate: the persistence diagram (point cloud of the half-plane, carrier of Wasserstein-type distances) and the landscape (a function from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\), which transports diagrams into a Hilbert space).

Definition 5.1 — Persistence diagram The diagram of a barcode \(\mathcal{B}\) is the multiset of points of the plane \(\{(b, d) : [b, d) \in \mathcal{B}\} \cup \Delta_\infty\), where each bar becomes the point with coordinates (birth, death); we add the diagonal \(\Delta = \{(x, x)\}\) with infinite multiplicity — bars of zero length live on it. The persistence of a class is \(d - b\); essential classes are sent to the line \(d = \infty\). By Theorem 4.2, diagram and barcode contain exactly the same information, and we will use them interchangeably.
Example 5.2 — The noisy circle, archetype of the module 200 points sampled on a circle of radius 1 plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.05. At scale \(\varepsilon \approx 0\): dust (200 H0 bars). When \(\varepsilon\) reaches the local spacing, the H0 bars die in a cascade; around \(\varepsilon \approx 1\), a long H1 bar is born — the central hole — which dies around \(\varepsilon \approx 2\), when the balls close up. Signature: one circle = one very long H1 bar + short dust. Two concentric circles: two long H1 bars; a sphere: no H1, one long H2 bar. This is the sanity check of the lab (ch. 10).
Definition 5.3 — Bottleneck distance Let \(\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2\) be two diagrams. For \(\delta \geq 0\), a \(\delta\)-matching is a partial bijection \(\pi\) between \(\mathcal{D}_1\) and \(\mathcal{D}_2\) such that each matched point moves by \(\|p - \pi(p)\|_\infty \leq \delta\), the unmatched points having to be at distance at most \(\delta\) from the diagonal (we match them with it). The bottleneck distance is $$d_B(\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2) \;=\; \inf_{\pi}\; \sup_{p}\; \|p - \pi(p)\|_\infty.$$ The Wasserstein-\(p\) distance replaces the sup by the \(\ell^p\) mean of the displacements — \(p = 2\) in practice. These are exactly optimal transport distances between measures (cf. Optimal Transport module): the diagonal plays the role of an infinite mass sink at the cost of distance-to-the-boundary.
Example 5.4 — Bottleneck by hand \(\mathcal{D}_1 = \{(0.3, 1.1)\}\) and \(\mathcal{D}_2 = \{(0.4, 1.0)\}\). Two options: match the two points (displacement \(\|(0.1, -0.1)\|_\infty = 0.1\)), or send everything onto the diagonal (cost \(\frac{1.1 - 0.3}{2} = 0.4\)). So \(d_B = 0.1\). Let us add to \(\mathcal{D}_1\) the ephemeral point \((2, 2.05)\): it matches the diagonal at cost \(0.025\), and \(d_B = 0.1\) still. Moral: the bottleneck ignores ephemeral features by construction — an \(L^\infty\) distance on the persistent holes.
Definition 5.5 — Persistence landscapes (Bubenik, 2015) For a diagram point \((b, d)\), set the "tent" function $$\Lambda_{(b, d)}(t) \;=\; \max\big( \min(t - b,\; d - t),\; 0 \big),$$ zero outside \((b, d)\), peaking at \(\frac{d-b}{2}\) at \(t = \frac{b+d}{2}\). The \(k\)-th landscape is the \(k\)-th largest of these tents at point \(t\) (and 0 if there are fewer than \(k\)): $$\lambda_D(k, t) \;=\; k\text{-th largest value of } \{\Lambda_{(b,d)}(t) : (b, d) \in D\}.$$ The sequence \((\lambda(k, \cdot))_k\) is a sequence of Lipschitz functions of \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\): the diagram now lives in a Hilbert space — means, covariances, PCA, tests become available.
Proposition 5.6 — The landscape is 1-Lipschitz and stable Each \(\lambda(k, \cdot)\) is \(\frac{1}{2}\)-Lipschitz, and for two diagrams $$\| \lambda_{\mathcal{D}_1}(k, \cdot) - \lambda_{\mathcal{D}_2}(k, \cdot) \|_\infty \;\leq\; d_B(\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2) \quad \text{for all } k.$$
(Sketch.) Each tent \(\Lambda_{(b,d)}\) is 1-Lipschitz: slopes \(\pm 1\) on \((b, d)\), zero elsewhere, peak \(\frac{d-b}{2}\) reached at \(\frac{b+d}{2}\); a \(k\)-th largest value of Lipschitz families is again Lipschitz (the curves cross at most once per pair, the order of the tents changes only at intersections). For stability: a bottleneck \(\delta\)-matching moves each tent by at most \(\delta\) in sup norm (moving a vertex from \((b, d)\) to \((b', d')\) with \(\|(b,d)-(b',d')\|_\infty \leq \delta\) moves the tent by \(\leq \delta\) everywhere), and the "\(k\)-th largest" operation is 1-Lipschitz in the sense of Young diagrams (monotone exchange). ∎
Other vectorizations Persistence images (Adams et al., 2017) sample the transform \((b, d) \mapsto (b, d - b)\) on a Gaussian grid — differentiable, hence pluggable into a network. Betti curves and persistence entropies are cheap summaries. The choice of vectorization is a choice of model: the landscape favors the median of persistences, the image the complete field, the kernel of ch. 8 the geometry of transport.
