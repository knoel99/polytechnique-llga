6. Stability and statistics

The whole practical value of persistence lies in one theorem: two close functions have close diagrams. Without it, the diagram of a noisy cloud would mean nothing — an infinitesimal perturbation could change it utterly. With it, TDA becomes a statistic: one can bound the sampling error, build confidence intervals by bootstrap, and test.

Theorem 6.1 (bottleneck stability — Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer, 2007) Let \(K\) be a finite complex and \(f, g : |K| \to \mathbb{R}\) two tame functions (finitely many critical values). Then the persistence diagrams of the sublevel filtrations \(K_t = \{\sigma : f(\sigma) \leq t\}\) satisfy: $$d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}(f), \; \mathrm{Dgm}(g) \big) \;\leq\; \|f - g\|_\infty.$$
Idea of the proof (interpolation). Set \(f_t = (1 - t) f + t g\) for \(t \in [0, 1]\): \(\|f_t - f_{t'}\|_\infty \leq |t - t'| \cdot \|f - g\|_\infty\). One shows (scene lemma — any small \(L^\infty\) perturbation moves the pairs of the diagram by as much) that for \(t' - t\) small, \(d_B(\mathrm{Dgm}(f_t), \mathrm{Dgm}(f_{t'})) \leq (t' - t) \|f - g\|_\infty\) up to a coupling near the diagonal. By subdividing \([0, 1]\) and composing the successive matchings, one obtains a matching of total cost \(\|f - g\|_\infty\). The pairs that are "born and die" during the interpolation correspond to excursions touching the diagonal — they are matched to it for free. The complete proof requires the decomposition theorem 4.2 to give a continuous meaning to the pairs of \(f_t\) (the "space of diagrams" endowed with the bottleneck is geodesic); the book by Chazal et al. (2016) gives the modern algebraic version — cf. ch. 9. ∎
Theorem 6.2 (algebraic stability — Chazal, de Silva, Glisse, Oudot, 2009/2016) The bound 6.1 remains valid for two tame persistence modules \(\mathbb{V}, \mathbb{W}\) that are \(\delta\)-interleaved (there exist \(u_t : V_t \to W_{t+\delta}\) and \(v_t : W_t \to V_{t+\delta}\) commuting with the arrows, with \(v_{t + \delta} u_t\) equal to the arrow \(V_t \to V_{t + 2\delta}\)): $$d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}(\mathbb{V}), \mathrm{Dgm}(\mathbb{W}) \big) \;\leq\; 2\,\delta \;=\; 2\, d_I(\mathbb{V}, \mathbb{W}).$$ Interleaving is the right "distance" between modules; the factor 2 is optimal. Consequence: stability is not a property of functions but of algebra — two filtrations close in the sense of approximate inclusions have close barcodes.
What 6.1–6.2 buy
  1. Sampling noise. Two samples \(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{X}'\) with \(\max_i \|x_i - x'_i\| \leq \eta\) give \(\eta\)-interleaved filtrations (each simplex appears at a level shifted by at most \(\eta\)): diagrams within \(2\eta\).
  2. Bootstrap. We resample the points, compute the diagrams of the replicas, and the quantiles of \(d_B\) around the observed diagram provide confidence bands (Fasy et al.; convergence of empirical landscapes: Chazal et al., SoCG 2013). Cf. Refresher Statistics module, ch. 5 (estimation) and ch. 6 (tests).
  3. Model comparison. Two experimental conditions (healthy subjects vs patients, ch. 8): test statistic = landscape or bottleneck distance, calibrated by permutation.
The Achilles heel: the sup norm The bound \(\|f - g\|_\infty\) is sensitive to a single outlier — one outlier shifts the distance to the sample everywhere around it. The standard fix: the distance-to-measure (DTM); for a measure \(\mu\) and a fraction \(0 < k \leq 1\) (or a radius \(h\)), one replaces \(d(x, \mathcal{X})\) by \(\delta_{\mu, k}(x) = \big( \frac{1}{k} \int_0^k \delta_t(x)^2 \,\mathrm{d}t \big)^{1/2}\), a local average of the distances to the \(k\) nearest points; its diagrams are Lipschitz with respect to \(\mu\) in Wasserstein-\(2\) distance (cf. Optimal Transport module); an isolated outlier now costs only the weight it carries in the measure, and no longer the sup distance. The DTM and its estimators are developed in the book by Boissonnat, Chazal and Yvinec (references).
Proposition 6.3 — Distances between clouds: Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff The Hausdorff distance \(d_H(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}) = \max\big( \sup_x d(x, \mathcal{Y}), \sup_y d(y, \mathcal{X}) \big)\) controls the interleaving of the Rips complexes: \(d_B(\mathrm{Dgm}\,\mathrm{Rips}(\mathcal{X}), \mathrm{Dgm}\,\mathrm{Rips}(\mathcal{Y})) \leq 2\, d_H(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y})\). For two abstract metric spaces (not embedded in a common one), the Gromov–Hausdorff distance plays this role — it is what makes persistence comparable across graphs (ch. 8).
(Sketch.) If \(d_H(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}) \leq \eta\), every edge of \(\mathrm{Rips}_t(\mathcal{X})\) corresponds to a pair of points of \(\mathcal{Y}\) at distance \(\leq t + 2\eta\): the inclusion sending \(x_i\) to a nearby \(y_i\) is a \(2\eta\)-interleaving of the filtrations — we apply 6.2. ∎
Proposition 6.4 — Topological inference: guaranteeing the homology of the hidden space Let \(X \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) be a compact positively reached space, with reach \(\tau > 0\) (the maximal size of the medullary balls: a quantity measuring curvature and proximity of sheets), and \(\mathcal{X} \subset X\) an \(\varepsilon\)-dense sample. There exist explicit bounds of the form \(\varepsilon, \alpha = O(\tau)\) — in the spirit of Niyogi–Smale–Weinberg — such that, for a radius \(\alpha\) lying between the sampling scale and a fraction of \(\tau\), the union of balls \(\bigcup_{x \in \mathcal{X}} B(x, \alpha)\) has the homotopy type of \(X\), hence its homology; and by the nerve theorem (th. 2.3), so does the Čech complex \(\check{C}_\alpha(\mathcal{X})\).
(Sketch.) On a compact of reach \(\tau\), the nearest-point projection is well defined on the tubular neighborhood of radius \(\tau\) around \(X\). For \(\alpha < \tau\), the union of balls stays inside this tubular neighborhood; the density of the sample guarantees that no "branch" of \(X\) is missed at scale \(\alpha\), and the nearest-point projection provides the homotopy equivalence between the union and \(X\). The nerve inherits the equivalence through the nerve theorem. ∎
In practice, neither \(\tau\) nor \(\varepsilon\) is known: it is the persistence diagram of the Čech complex — or of the Rips complex, bracketed by prop. 2.4 — that reveals the interval of scales where the homology remains stable. Read the longest bar in dimension \(k\): there lies the whole topological inference, official topic of the course, put to work.
