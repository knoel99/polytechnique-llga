8. Controlling graph generators

Synthesis chapter: the Graph Generative Models module (M2) develops each family in detail — here we reread its chapters 3, 4, and 5 through the grid of ch. 2, to show that the theory of control there is identical to that of text and images.

Theorem 8.1 — Three controls, three mechanisms, one grid
  1. Hard constraints: chemical valence. Each decoding step masks the bonds that would exceed the remaining valence \(r_v\) of atom \(v\) — NeVAE (AAAI 2019) pushes validity above 90% without post-processing (versus a few percent otherwise); GraphAF masks likewise and reaches 100% with rules (68% without). This is word for word theorem 2.2: renormalization over the legal set \(\mathcal{L}\), guaranteed by construction;
  2. Gradient guidance: GraphAF (ICLR 2020) — each decision starts from a \(z_t \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\) passed through a flow; to maximize a property \(Q(G)\) (solubility, docking), one moves \(z_t\) along \(\nabla_{z_t} Q(G_t)\) during generation — the guidance strength is the gradient step, the fidelity/diversity trade-off is measured there (the graph module ch. 4 derives it);
  3. Conditioning + inpainting: DiGress (ICLR 2023) trains the denoiser with a descriptor \(c\) sometimes masked (classifier-free: thm 7.1 transposed) and freezes partial scaffolds; the discrete posterior allows generating around an imposed motif — the exact analogue of the image null-text/inpainting of ch. 7.

What graphs teach control Two specific lessons. (i) Local constraints (valence) are trivially masked, global constraints (connectivity, ring size) are not: the corresponding automaton would be exponential — the search for "tractable global constraints" is open; (ii) the evaluation of control is the most mature there (validity/uniqueness/novelty + FCD; graph module ch. 6) — it is the template of ch. 9.
