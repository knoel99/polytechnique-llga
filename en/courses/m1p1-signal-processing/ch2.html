2. The Fourier series

Theorem 2.1 — Fourier series decomposition (coefficients by projection) Let \(f\) be \(T_0\)-periodic, square integrable over one period. Then $$f = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} c_k\, e^{ik\omega_0 t} \quad \text{in } L^2, \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f(t)\, e^{-ik\omega_0 t}\, dt,$$ the convergence taking place in \(L^2\) norm (and pointwise toward \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}{2}\) at discontinuity points if \(f\) is piecewise \(C^1\): Dirichlet's theorem). The \(c_k\) form the spectrum: \(|c_k|\) magnitude, \(\arg c_k\) phase.
The \(e_k = e^{ik\omega_0t}\) are orthogonal (prop. 0.1) and form a Hilbert basis (completeness, th. 0.3). The decomposition in an infinite orthonormal basis follows the logic of finite dimension: the partial sum \(S_N f = \sum_{|k|\leq N} c_k e_k\) is the orthogonal projection of \(f\) onto \(\mathrm{Vect}\{e_k\}_{|k|\leq N}\), since \(\langle f - S_Nf, e_j\rangle = 0\) for \(|j| \leq N\). Since the basis is complete, \(\|f - S_N f\|_{L^2} \to 0\). Explicitly: for every trigonometric polynomial \(p = \sum_{|k|\leq N} d_k e_k\), Pythagoras gives \(\|f - p\|^2 = \|f - S_Nf\|^2 + \|S_Nf - p\|^2 \geq \|f - S_Nf\|^2\): the projection is the best approximation — and the \(d_k\) that cancel the cross terms are exactly \(d_k = \langle f, e_k\rangle / \|e_k\|^2 = c_k\).∎
Theorem 2.2 — Parseval's identity (proved by density) $$\frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} |f(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} |c_k|^2.$$
Pythagoras on \(f = S_N f + (f - S_N f)\) with \(\langle S_Nf, f - S_Nf\rangle = 0\): \(\|f\|^2 = \|S_Nf\|^2 + \|f - S_Nf\|^2\). Now \(\|S_Nf\|^2 = \sum_{|k|\leq N}|c_k|^2\|e_k\|^2 = T_0 \sum_{|k|\leq N}|c_k|^2\), and \(\|f - S_Nf\|^2 \to 0\) by theorem 2.1 (whose proof rests on Fejér density, th. 0.3). Dividing by \(T_0\) and passing to the limit, the "time" energy equals the "frequency" energy.∎
Lemma 2.3 — Regularity and decay of the coefficients (by integrations by parts) If \(f\) is \(T_0\)-periodic, piecewise \(C^p\), with \(f^{(p)}\) integrable, then $$c_k = \frac{1}{(ik\omega_0)^p}\, c_k\big(f^{(p)}\big), \qquad k \neq 0.$$ In particular: a jump discontinuity \([f]\) ⇒ \(c_k \sim \frac{[f]}{T_0\,ik\omega_0} = \frac{[f]}{2i\pi k}\) (decay in \(1/k\)); \(f\) piecewise \(C^1\) ⇒ \(c_k = O(1/k^2)\); \(f\) \(C^p\) ⇒ \(c_k = o(1/k^p)\).
Integration by parts over one period (\(f\) piecewise \(C^1\), the boundary terms cancel by periodicity): \(c_k(f) = \frac{1}{T_0}\big[ f e^{-ik\omega_0t}\big]_0^{T_0} + \frac{ik\omega_0}{T_0}\int f(t)\,e^{-ik\omega_0t}dt = \frac{1}{ik\omega_0}c_k(f')\). One iterates \(p\) times. The last term is a Fourier coefficient of an \(L^2\) function (or of a jump if \(f\) is discontinuous); Parseval (th. 2.2) applied to \(f^{(p)}\) bounds \(\sum |c_k(f^{(p)})|^2\), hence \(c_k = o(k^{-p})\) since the series \(\sum 1/k^{2p}\) must converge… and a residual jump imposes the exact \(1/k\).∎
Example 2.4 — The square wave: explicit coefficients and the Gibbs phenomenon For the odd square wave \(f = \mathrm{sign}(\sin)\) (value ±1, jump of height 2): the direct computation (exercise 1, with solution) gives $$c_k = 0 \ (k \text{ even}), \qquad c_k = \frac{-2i}{\pi k} \ (k \text{ odd}) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{m \geq 0} \frac{\sin\big((2m+1)\omega_0 t\big)}{2m+1}.$$ Decay in \(1/k\), consistent with lemma 2.3 (discontinuity). The Gibbs phenomenon: near the jump, the partial sum \(S_N f\) overshoots the target by about $$\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin u}{u}\,du\Big) - \frac12 \approx 8.95\,\%$$ of the height of the jump, whatever \(N\): the oscillation concentrates (width in \(O(1/N)\)) but its amplitude does not tend to 0. Origin: the Dirichlet kernel changes sign (ch. 0); by averaging (Fejér) or by windowing (ch. 5), the overshoot disappears — at the price of a widened transition.
Why these three results structure the whole course Parseval: the energy can be read in the spectrum (noise, filtering, SNR). Lemma 2.3: a smooth signal has a fast-decaying spectrum — the foundation of compression (one throws away the high frequencies) and of sampling (a fast-decaying spectrum is quickly negligible: ch. 4). Gibbs: any abrupt cutoff in frequency costs oscillations in time — hence the soft windows of ch. 5.
