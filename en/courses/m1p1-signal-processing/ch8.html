8. The signal for modern ML

Mel spectrogram and MFCC: the standard pipeline
  1. Frames: 25 ms cuts every 10 ms, Hann window (a Gabor frame, ch. 6); STFT.
  2. Mel scale (perceptive, linear < 1 kHz then logarithmic): $$m = 2595\,\log_{10}\Big(1 + \frac{f}{700}\Big), \qquad f = 700\,\big(10^{m/2595} - 1\big).$$
  3. Mel filter bank: 20-80 triangular filters with edges uniformly spaced in Mel — band-pass filters of increasing width in Hz: \(E_m = \sum_\nu W_m(\nu)\,|\mathrm{STFT}(\nu)|^2\) (a sparse weighting of the spectrogram).
  4. Log (amplitude perception); DCT along the Mel axis (a DCT = a "real FT"): the \(c_j\) are the MFCC; one keeps 13-20 coefficients + derivatives \(\Delta, \Delta\Delta\), and normalizes in mean/variance per speaker.
Historically features for lightweight models (GMM-HMM), the MFCC remain the compact entry point; deep networks rather consume the raw Mel spectrogram.
Networks on spectrograms The Mel spectrogram is the image that 2D CNNs and audio transformers consume: the first layers of audio CNNs learn oriented frequency detectors — Gabor filters (ch. 6), see the Deep Learning module. The emblematic data augmentation (masking of frequency bands and time frames) operates directly in the time-frequency plane: the spectrogram is treated as a texture to be repaired. Speech technology, transcription, audio-multimodal transformers: this whole pipeline is the signal preprocessing of modern ML.
Distances between spectral distributions: FID and OT spectral loss Comparing two sets of signals = comparing two distributions of spectrograms (or of features). Standard: fit a Gaussian to the features of each set and measure the Fréchet distance $$d^2\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\Sigma_1), \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\Sigma_2)\big) = \|\mu_1 - \mu_2\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\Big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2\big(\Sigma_1^{1/2}\Sigma_2\Sigma_1^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big).$$ This is nothing but \(W_2^2\) between Gaussians (the Bures metric): the FID of image generators and the "audio Fréchet distance" of audio generators are Wasserstein distances in disguise. The OT spectral loss of audio generative models generalizes the idea: comparing real/generated batches by Gaussian \(W_2\) (closed form above, differentiable) or by Sinkhorn on the spectra: the complete proof of the Bures formula and the Sinkhorn algorithm are on the syllabus of the Computational Optimal Transport module (M1P2, ch. 2 and 3, same instructor) — this chapter is its preparation. In 1D (spectra or one-dimensional signals), everything reduces to the quantile coupling: \(W_2^2 = \int_0^1\big(F_1^{-1}(u) - F_2^{-1}(u)\big)^2du\) (proved there).
Example 8.1 — 1D domain adaptation on signals Two sensors measure the same phenomenon with different drifts (offset, gain, frequency response): the distributions of the frames differ, not the semantics. Alignment by 1D OT: transport the quantiles of sensor A onto those of sensor B (monotone transport, computable by sorting); the same principle at d-dimensional scale (feature clouds) is the optimal transport domain adaptation detailed in the OT module (ch. 4), whose instructor is a co-author. The signal provides the spectrum; OT provides the geometry.
Fourier in the architectures: convolutions and positions
  • Convolution of CNNs = the discrete convolution of ch. 1: the learned kernel is a filter whose frequency response (DTFT, ch. 5) explains its behavior — CNNs learn banks of Gabor filters; convolution via FFT in \(\mathcal{O}(n\log n)\) when the kernels are large (th. 5.3).
  • Convolution on graphs: the GNN message passing \(h \leftarrow \sigma(\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2} h W)\) is a convolution in the spectral sense — the Kipf & Welling filter is a degree-1 polynomial of the Laplacian; expanded in the Graph ML module.
  • Positional encodings: the sine/cosine of transformers (\(\mathrm{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin(p/10000^{2i/d})\)…) = Fourier bases; positional encodings on graphs (LapEig) reuse the eigenvectors of the Laplacian — Fourier again, see the Graph ML module.
Complement to the official syllabus: source separation and the learning dictionary The "Signal representation and learning dictionary" block of the detailed syllabus of the course unit names source separation and the learning dictionary: here is the principle. The idea — officially that of the course — is to move from fixed bases (Fourier, wavelets, ch. 2 and 6) to a learned dictionary: a signal is represented as \(x \approx D\alpha\) with \(D = [d_1 | \dots | d_K]\) a dictionary of waveforms and \(\alpha\) sparse (few active coefficients). Learning \(D\) (for instance by non-negative matrix factorization — NMF — or K-SVD-type sparse least squares) on spectrograms of mixtures separates the sources: each component \(d_k\alpha_k\) concentrates on one source (a speaker, an instrument), since a mixture is a sum of sources and sparsity forces the atoms to specialize. This is the first historical bridge between signal processing and unsupervised learning: a learned dictionary is a latent representation, \(\alpha\) a code — the same grammar as the autoencoders of the Deep Learning module, with the added spectral interpretability of the atoms. The "deep learning" signal reconstruction announced by the sheet is the generalization of this scheme in which the decoder becomes a network trained on degraded/clean pairs (denoising, super-resolution).
