4. Sampling: the Shannon theorem

Theorem 4.1 — Shannon-Nyquist (proved) Let \(x\) be band-limited: \(\hat x(\nu) = 0\) for \(|\nu| \geq B\), and \(f_s = 1/T_e > 2B\). Then the samples \(x_n = x(nT_e)\) suffice to reconstruct exactly \(x\): $$x(t) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} x_n\, \mathrm{sinc}\Big(\frac{t}{T_e} - n\Big), \qquad \mathrm{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}.$$
Step 1 (sampling = periodizing the spectrum). Ideal sampling is written \(x_s(t) = x(t)\cdot\Sha_{T_e}(t) = \sum_n x_n\,\delta(t - nT_e)\). Product in time ⇒ convolution in frequency (prop. 3.2): $$\hat x_s(\nu) = \hat x * \widehat{\Sha_{T_e}}(\nu) = \frac{1}{T_e}\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \hat x\Big(\nu - \frac{k}{T_e}\Big).$$ The spectrum of \(x_s\) is the periodization of the spectrum of \(x\), of period \(f_s\). Step 2 (no overlap). If \(f_s > 2B\), the copies (centered at \(0, \pm f_s, \pm 2f_s, \dots\), each of half-width \(B\)) are disjoint: in the central band \(|\nu| < f_s/2\) we read \(\hat x = T_e\,\hat x_s\). Step 3 (ideal low-pass filtering). Let us multiply by \(R(\nu) = T_e\,\mathbf{1}_{|\nu| < f_s/2}\): \(\hat x = R\,\hat x_s\), that is, \(x = r * x_s\) where \(r = \check R\) is the inverse transform of the rectangle — computed by direct integration: \(r(t) = T_e\int_{-f_s/2}^{f_s/2} e^{i2\pi\nu t}d\nu = \frac{\sin(\pi f_s t)}{\pi f_s t}\cdot f_s T_e = \mathrm{sinc}(f_s t)\). Step 4 (interpolation formula). Convolving sinc with the comb of samples gives, since \(\mathrm{sinc}\big(f_s(t - nT_e)\big) = \mathrm{sinc}(f_st - n)\) and since the sinc equals 1 at 0 and 0 at the other integers (the samples do not "talk" to each other): $$x(t) = \sum_n x_n\, \mathrm{sinc}(f_s t - n).\qquad\qquad \square$$ If \(f_s \leq 2B\), the copies overlap: the information is lost without return (two different signals have the same samples).∎
Proposition 4.2 — Aliasing, in formulas After sampling at \(f_s\), every frequency \(\nu\) is seen at the apparent frequency $$\nu_{\text{app}} = \min_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}\ \big|\nu - k f_s\big| \ \in\ \Big[0, \frac{f_s}{2}\Big],$$ the image of \(\nu\) in the fundamental band \([-f_s/2, f_s/2]\) modulo \(f_s\). Example: \(f_s = 80\) Hz, \(\nu = 60\) Hz ⇒ \(\nu_{\text{app}} = |60 - 80| = 20\) Hz (the line "moves backward"); a rising chirp passes \(f_s/2\), then goes back down — the ghost trace of the spectrogram of exercise 6. Wheels that seem to turn backward in the movies, moiré of images: the same phenomenon.
Bandpass sampling For a signal modulating only a narrow band \([f_c - B, f_c + B]\) (radio, sensors), the useful condition bears on the width \(2B\) and not on the highest frequency: it suffices to choose \(f_s\) such that the copies of the band do not overlap. Example: band \([19, 21]\) MHz (\(f_c = 20\), \(B = 1\)), \(f_s = 4\) MHz: every frequency \(f\) folds back to \(|f - 20|\) MHz, without collision — the spectrum is transposed to baseband, possibly flipped (spectral reversal depending on the parity of the multiple of \(f_s\)). This is the basis of software receivers (SDR).
Example 4.3 — Acquisition chain and practical reconstruction Chain: analog signal → analog anti-aliasing filter (cuts at \(f_s/2\), indispensable before sampling) → sampling \(f_s\) → quantization (\(b\) bits: quantization noise at \(\approx 6b\) dB of dynamic range) → digital. Audio CD: \(B = 20\) kHz ⇒ \(f_s = 44.1\) kHz, 16 bits. The Shannon sinc is perfectly localized in frequency but infinitely long in time: one truncates and windows it (sinc × Hann over \(M\) points) — hence a slight aliasing and a soft transition, quantifiable with the tools of ch. 5; linear interpolation (triangle) is its cheap version. 
import numpy as np
fs = 200                       # sampling frequency
t  = np.arange(0, 1, 1/fs)
f0 = 60                        # signal 60 Hz
x  = np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*t)

X = np.fft.rfft(x)             # spectrum (positive frequencies)
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1/fs)
peak = freqs[np.argmax(np.abs(X))]
print("frequence dominante :", peak, "Hz")

# Aliasing: sampling a 60 Hz signal at 80 Hz "sees" it at 20 Hz
fs2 = 80; t2 = np.arange(0, 1, 1/fs2)
x2 = np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*t2)
print("vu a :", np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x2),1/fs2)[np.argmax(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x2)))], "Hz")

# Shannon reconstruction on a fine grid (sinc truncated to the 21 neighbors)
tr = np.arange(0, 1, 1/2000); xr = np.zeros_like(tr)
for n in range(len(x)):
    xr += x[n]*np.sinc(fs*tr - n)
print("erreur max apres reconstruction :", np.abs(np.sin(2*np.pi*f0*tr) - xr).max())
