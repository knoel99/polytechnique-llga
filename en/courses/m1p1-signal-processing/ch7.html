7. Random signals and spectral estimation

Definition 7.1 — Process, wide-sense stationarity (WSS) A random signal is a family \(X(t, \omega)\) of random variables. It is wide-sense stationary if its mean is constant \(m = \mathbb{E}[X(t)]\) and if its autocorrelation $$R_X(t, t+\tau) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X(t)-m)\,\overline{(X(t+\tau)-m)}\big]$$ depends only on the lag \(\tau\) (written \(R_X(\tau)\)). This is the right framework for noise, speech, sensors: each realization is a signal of this course, but the interesting quantities are expectations.
Proposition 7.2 — Properties of the autocorrelation \(R_X(0) = \mathbb{E}|X - m|^2\) is the average power; \(|R_X(\tau)| \leq R_X(0)\) (Cauchy-Schwarz: \(|\mathbb{E}[X_0\overline{X_\tau}]| \leq \sqrt{R(0)R(0)}\)); \(R_X\) is hermitian (\(R_X(-\tau) = \overline{R_X(\tau)}\)) and positive definite. "Noise" is decorrelated at a distance: \(R(\tau) \to 0\).
Theorem 7.3 — Wiener-Khinchin (statement) and the spectral density For a WSS process, there exists a positive measure \(S_X\), the power spectral density, such that $$R_X(\tau) = \int e^{i2\pi\nu\tau}\, dS_X(\nu), \qquad\text{and if } R_X \in L^1:\quad S_X(\nu) = \widehat{R_X}(\nu).$$ The power is distributed across the spectrum: \(\mathbb{E}|X-m|^2 = R_X(0) = \int dS_X\): Parseval (th. 2.2) becomes a definition. (The proof — Bochner's theorem — goes beyond the scope of the course; the intuition: a WSS process is an incoherent superposition of sinusoids, each carrying its share of the power.)
  • White noise: decorrelated to all orders, \(R = \sigma^2\delta\) ⇒ \(S = \sigma^2\) constant — "all frequencies on equal footing". A band-limited white noise (physically realizable): \(S = \sigma^2\mathbf{1}_{|\nu| \leq B}\) ⇒ \(R(\tau) = 2B\sigma^2\,\mathrm{sinc}(2B\tau)\): decorrelation at the scale \(1/B\) (direct computation of the FT of the rectangle, cf. th. 4.1).
  • Filtered noise: \(Y = h * X\) ⇒ \(S_Y = |H|^2\,S_X\) (colored noise): the filter shapes the spectrum squared.
  • AR(1) (auto-regressive model \(X_n = \rho X_{n-1} + W_n\), \(|\rho| < 1\)): \(R[\tau] = \frac{\sigma_W^2}{1-\rho^2}\,\rho^{|\tau|}\) and \(S(\nu) = \frac{\sigma_W^2}{|1 - \rho e^{-i2\pi\nu}|^2}\) — the simplest model of colored noise, ubiquitous in finance and sensors.
Definition 7.4 — The periodogram "Natural" estimator of \(S_X\) from \(N\) samples: $$\hat S_N(\nu) = \frac{1}{N}\Big|\sum_{n<N} X_n\, e^{-i2\pi\nu n}\Big|^2 \qquad (\text{on the DFT grid: } |X_k|^2/N).$$
Theorem 7.5 — Bias and variance of the periodogram
  • Bias (leakage): \(\mathbb{E}[\hat S_N] = S * W_N\) where \(W_N\) is the Dirichlet kernel (prop. 0.2): the expectation "smears" over ±1 bin — a strong line masks a weak line (hence the windows, prop. 5.4). The bias tends to 0 as \(N \to \infty\) (\(W_N \to \delta\)).
  • Variance: for a Gaussian process, \(\mathrm{Var}[\hat S_N(\nu)] \approx S_X(\nu)^2\) for every \(N\) — the relative error is 100% and does not decrease: the periodogram alone is a poor estimator, whatever the observation duration.
Sketch. Bias: \(\hat S_N = \widehat{z_N}\,\overline{\widehat{z_N}}\) with \(z_N = X\cdot w_N\) (truncation = rectangular window); the product in time becomes a convolution of the spectrum with \(\hat w_N = W_N\) (prop. 3.2), then one takes the expectation. Variance: on the DFT grid, the \(\hat S_N(\nu_k)\) are asymptotically decorrelated and, for a Gaussian, \(\hat S_N(\nu)\) approximately follows \(S(\nu)\cdot \chi^2_2/2\) (squared modulus of two independent Gaussians); now \(\mathrm{Var}[\chi^2_2/2] = 1\), hence a variance of order \(S^2\), independent of \(N\).∎
Bartlett and Welch methods — reducing the variance by averaging
  • Bartlett: split the \(N\) points into \(L\) disjoint segments of \(M = N/L\) points and average the periodograms: \(\mathrm{Var} \approx S^2/L\)… at the price of an increased bias (windows \(M\) times shorter ⇒ leakage multiplied) — the central bias/variance trade-off of spectral estimation.
  • Welch: overlapping segments (50%) and Hann windows: the same variance gain (Hann segments overlap "for free"), leakage controlled at −31 dB; this is the default method of instrumentation.
from scipy.signal import welch
n  = 20000; fs = 500.0
t  = np.arange(n)/fs
x  = np.sin(2*np.pi*50*t) + 0.02*np.sin(2*np.pi*80*t) + 0.5*rng.standard_normal(n)

f, S = welch(x, fs=fs, nperseg=1024, window='hann', noverlap=512)
# L = 2*20000/1024 - 1 segments: the line at 80 Hz (-34 dB below the line at 50 Hz)
# emerges from the noise, whereas the raw periodogram drowns it (th. 7.5)
Complement to the official syllabus: Wiener filtering The detailed syllabus of the course unit explicitly mentions "filtering and prediction of stationary random signals" and "Wiener filtering": here is the object. We observe \(Y = X + W\) (signal \(X\) WSS, noise \(W\) WSS, with spectra \(S_X, S_W\), assumed uncorrelated) and we seek the LTI filter \(h\) that minimizes the mean squared error \(\mathbb{E}|X - h * Y|^2\). The (non-causal) solution is the Wiener filter: $$H(\nu) = \frac{S_X(\nu)}{S_X(\nu) + S_W(\nu)}.$$ Reading: at frequencies where the signal dominates (\(S_X \gg S_W\)) the filter transmits (\(H \approx 1\)); where the noise dominates, it cancels out — the spectrum decides, optimally in the \(L^2\) sense. This is exactly the "filtered noise" of Theorem 7.3 (\(S_Y = |H|^2 S_X\)) inverted: instead of computing the effect of a filter on a spectrum, we design the filter that best separates signal and noise, from their estimated spectral densities (Definition 7.4 and Theorem 7.5 — estimated in practice by Bartlett/Welch). The causal filter is built by spectral factorization (via the transfer function \(H(z)\) of ch. 5); the linear prediction of an AR process (the \(X_n = \rho X_{n-1} + W_n\) of Theorem 7.3) is its "zero noise" case: predicting is filtering toward the future. The Kalman filter is its recursive state-space variant, seen in the downstream modules of the program.
