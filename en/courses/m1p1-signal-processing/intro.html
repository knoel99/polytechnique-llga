Introduction and overview

ModuleSignal Processing
CodeAPM_51055_EP
Team and workloadInstructor: Rémi Flamary (École Polytechnique, co-director of the master's program) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · signals, Fourier, sampling, filtering, time-frequency analysis, spectral estimation — up to the spectrograms consumed by ML and the spectral distances of optimal transport (the same instructor's M1P2 module).
Status in the programRecommended — LLGA track

The signal is the raw material of modern AI: voice, audio, images, time series are signals that Fourier, Shannon and filtering allow to represent before any learning. This course connects the classical signal processing chain to its current machine learning uses — the spectrograms consumed by speech models, the spectral losses of optimal transport, the sampling of sensors. Taught by Rémi Flamary, co-director of the master's program, it directly prepares for the Period 2 and M2 modules that handle real signals.

What you will be able to do

  • Characterize a signal and a linear time-invariant system (impulse, frequency response, stability, causality).
  • Move from time to frequency with confidence: Fourier series, Fourier transform, and a reasoned choice of representation.
  • Apply the Shannon theorem and understand what "sampling well" requires — and what aliasing is.
  • Implement a DFT/FFT and design digital low-pass, high-pass, band-pass filters.
  • Analyze a non-stationary signal through time-frequency analysis and wavelets rather than the global transform alone.
  • Estimate the spectrum of a random signal (periodogram, averaging) and interpret its noise.
  • Connect spectral representation and learning: why a spectrogram is a good input for a neural network.

Where this course fits in the program

Recommended option of the LLGA track in Year 1 · Period 1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS). It builds on the two refresher modules: Refresher in Statistics for the probability theory useful for random signals, and Refresher in Computer Science for NumPy and the implementation of the labs.

Downstream, three modules directly consume this course. Speech Technology for Conversational AI (M2) starts from the speech signal representations introduced here; Multimodal Generative AI leverages spectrograms as the interface between audio and models; Computational Optimal Transport for ML and Fairness, a module by the same instructor, uses the spectral distances (sinkhorn on spectrograms) whose foundations are laid in the chapter "The signal for modern ML". The course also sheds light on the filtering and convolution that Image Analysis and Computer Vision mobilizes from its first chapters.

Course outline

  • Review: Fourier series in the L² sense, Fubini: the functional analysis tools from which the whole course follows.
  • Signals and linear systems: impulse response, convolution, stability — the common framework.
  • The Fourier series: decomposing a periodic signal, in the L² sense and beyond intuition.
  • The Fourier transform: the universal time-frequency change of variable and its properties.
  • Sampling: the Shannon theorem: from the continuous to the discrete without losing information — or knowing that you are.
  • DFT, FFT and digital filtering: the discrete transform, its fast algorithm, and filter design.
  • Time-frequency analysis and wavelets: representing a signal whose content evolves — the short-time Fourier window and beyond.
  • Random signals and spectral estimation: noise, periodogram, estimating the spectrum of a process.
  • The signal for modern ML: spectrograms as network inputs, spectral losses — the explicit bridge to learning.
  • The teacher's research: from signals to optimal transport: Rémi Flamary's research, from signal matching to regularized optimal transport.

How to study this module

  • Work through the review chapter even if you come from an applied background: the Fourier series in the L² sense is presented there with exactly the level required afterwards.
  • Listen to your transforms: every lab can be validated by ear (filtered sound, sampled sound) — perceptual checking is an excellent error detector.
  • Redo in NumPy an FFT and a filtering on a real signal (audio, sensor) before using the ready-made functions.
  • Memorize the properties of the Fourier transform (duality, convolution-product): they save half the computations of the exercises.
  • Spend time on random signals, often new to students: they are the key to spectral estimation and to the M2 audio modules.
  • Finish with the chapter "The signal for modern ML" then with the teacher's research: this is the best preparation for Computational Optimal Transport and Speech Technology.
