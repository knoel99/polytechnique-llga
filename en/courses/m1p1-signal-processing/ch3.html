3. The Fourier transform

Definition 3.1 — The Fourier transform For \(x \in L^1(\mathbb{R})\) (then \(L^2\) by density): $$\hat x(\nu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi \nu t}\, dt, \qquad x(t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \hat x(\nu)\, e^{i 2\pi \nu t}\, d\nu.$$ \(\nu\) in Hz (or \(\omega\) in rad/s with a factor \(2\pi\)); the "continuous" transform generalizes the Fourier coefficients to non- periodic signals: a signal of infinite duration has a continuous spectrum.

Convolution (via Fubini). \(\widehat{x*h}(\nu) = \iint x(\tau)h(t-\tau)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt\,d\tau\). The modulus of the integrand is \(|x|\,|h| \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^2)\) (a product of integrable functions), so Fubini allows the exchange; set \(u = t - \tau\): \(\int x(\tau)e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau}d\tau \cdot \int h(u)e^{-i2\pi\nu u}du = \hat x\,\hat h\). Differentiation. \(\widehat{x'}(\nu) = [x e^{-i2\pi\nu t}] + i2\pi\nu\int x e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\) by parts; the boundary terms vanish for \(x\) the derivative of an \(L^1\) function. Duality. \(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}x)(t) = \int \hat x(\nu) e^{-i2\pi\nu t}d\nu = x(-t)\) (inversion formula evaluated at \(-t\)): Fourier² reverses time — hence product ↔ convolution by the previous property applied twice. Dilation. \(u = at\): \(\hat{}(at) = \int x(u)e^{-i2\pi\nu u/a}du/|a|\).∎
Proposition 3.3 — The Gaussian is its own transform (computed) $$\mathcal{F}\big(e^{-\pi t^2}\big)(\nu) = e^{-\pi \nu^2}.$$
Set \(g(t) = e^{-\pi t^2}\) and consider \(\hat g\). We exploit the differential equation \(g' = -2\pi t\,g\). Integrating by parts, \(\int t\,g(t)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\int g'(t)\,e^{-i2\pi\nu t}dt = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\,i2\pi\nu\,\hat g = -i\nu\,\hat g\). Hence \(\hat g'(\nu) = -i2\pi \int t g e^{-i2\pi\nu t} = -i2\pi(-i\nu)\hat g = -2\pi\nu\,\hat g\) — the same differential equation. Solution: \(\hat g(\nu) = \hat g(0)\,e^{-\pi\nu^2}\), and \(\hat g(0) = \int g = 1\) (Gaussian integral).∎
The key duo: convolution ↔ multiplication Filtering = convolving in time = multiplying in frequency: \(y = x*h \iff \hat y = \hat x\,H\), where \(H(\nu) = \hat h(\nu)\) is the frequency response (prop. 1.4). Low-pass: \(H \approx 1\) for \(|\nu| < \nu_c\), \(\approx 0\) beyond. This is also why the FFT speeds up the convolutions of CNNs: complexity \(\mathcal{O}(n \log n)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) (see the Deep Learning module). With proposition 3.3, a "narrow" Gaussian in time is "wide" in frequency — a foretaste of the next theorem.
Theorem 3.4 — Heisenberg-Gabor uncertainty (proved) For \(x\) of energy 1, with standard deviations \(\sigma_t\) (time, around \(t_0\)) and \(\sigma_\nu\) (frequency): $$\sigma_t \, \sigma_\nu \ \geq\ \frac{1}{4\pi},$$ with equality if and only if \(x\) is a Gaussian (modulated and translated).
Time translations and frequency modulations do not change the product \(\sigma_t\sigma_\nu\) (delay = pure phase; modulation = a translation of the spectrum): one assumes \(t_0 = \nu_0 = 0\), \(\|x\|_2 = 1\). Let us derive the key identity by parts (vanishing boundary terms): \(\int t\,\frac{d}{dt}|x|^2 dt = -\int |x|^2 = -1\), and \(\frac{d}{dt}|x|^2 = 2\mathrm{Re}(x\,\overline{x'})\), hence \(2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle t\,x, x'\rangle = 1\), i.e. \(|\langle tx, x'\rangle| \geq 1/2\). Cauchy-Schwarz: \(\tfrac12 \leq \|t x\|_2\,\|x'\|_2 = \sigma_t\,\|x'\|_2\). Finally \(\|x'\|_2^2 = \|\widehat{x'}\|_2^2 = \|i2\pi\nu\,\hat x\|_2^2 = (2\pi\sigma_\nu)^2\) (Parseval + differentiation). Hence \(\sigma_t\sigma_\nu \geq 1/(4\pi)\). Equality: Cauchy-Schwarz must be saturated, \(x' = \lambda t x\): the solutions are the Gaussians \(e^{-\pi t^2/\sigma^2}\) (up to a linear phase and a translation).∎
Definition 3.5 — Useful distributions: Dirac and comb Without formalism: the Dirac mass \(\delta\) is "the function" of mass 1 concentrated at 0, defined by \(\int f\,\delta = f(0)\) (a distribution, i.e. a continuous linear form on test functions). We admit: \(\hat\delta = 1\), \(\hat 1 = \delta\) (duality), and the Dirac comb \(\,\Sha_{T_e}(t) = \sum_n \delta(t - nT_e)\), whose transform is a comb: $$\widehat{\Sha_{T_e}}(\nu) = \frac{1}{T_e}\,\Sha_{1/T_e}(\nu).$$ These objects make rigorous: the spectrum of a sinusoid (two \(\delta\) lines), sampling (a product with a comb, ch. 4), white noise (ch. 7).
