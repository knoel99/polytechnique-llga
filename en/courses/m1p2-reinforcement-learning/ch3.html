3. Learning: TD, SARSA, Q-learning (unknown model)

In practice we know neither \(P\) nor \(r\): we interact. Temporal-difference learning learns by correcting each prediction toward the observation:

Algorithm 3.1 — TD(0), SARSA, Q-learning
  • TD(0) (evaluation): \(V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t)\big]\); the bracket is the TD error \(\delta_t\).
  • SARSA (on-policy): \(Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t,A_t)\big]\).
  • Q-learning (off-policy): $$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\Big[R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(S_{t+1}, a') - Q(S_t, A_t)\Big].$$
Theorem 3.2 — Convergence of TD(0) and Q-learning (sketch via Robbins-Monro) Under sufficient exploration (every pair \((s,a)\) visited infinitely often) and steps \(\alpha_t(s,a) \in (0,1)\) with \(\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty\), \(\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty\): \(V_t \to V^\pi\) (TD(0), Tsitsiklis 1994) and \(Q_t \to Q^\star\) (Q-learning, Watkins & Dayan 1992) almost surely.
TD(0) is a Robbins-Monro recursion. The update writes \(V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,\delta_t\) with \(\delta_t = r + \gamma V(S') - V(s)\). With the state \((S_t, S_{t+1})\) fixed, the expectation of \(\delta_t\) given \(V\) equals \(r(S_t) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P^\pi(s'|S_t) V(s') - V(S_t) = (T_\pi V - V)(S_t)\) — the TD error is unbiased noise around the Bellman residual. The target equation is therefore \(h(V) = T_\pi V - V = 0\), whose unique zero is \(V^\pi\) (Theorem 1.5). The remainder satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 0.5: the contraction of \(T_\pi\) (modulus \(\gamma\)) provides the monotonicity \((V - V^\pi)^\top (T_\pi V - V) \leq -(1-\gamma)\|V - V^\pi\|^2_2\)... we normalize by the visit frequency of \(s\), and Theorem 0.4 (Robbins-Siegmund) concludes. Q-learning: same scheme with two extra ingredients. (i) the operator \(\mathcal{T}\) (with max) remains a contraction of modulus \(\gamma\) (Theorem 1.6); (ii) the max introduces a bias conditional on the sample, but not asymptotically: in the limit, the argmax is stable and the iteration converges to the unique fixed point \(Q^\star\). Hence the result: Q-learning converges whatever the exploration policy (as long as it visits everything) — this is the off-policy property. ∎
Definition 3.3 — n-step returns and eligibility traces The \(n\)-step return mixes bootstrapping and Monte-Carlo: $$G_t^{(n)} = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1} R_{t+n} + \gamma^{n} V(S_{t+n}).$$ TD(\(\lambda\)), "forward" view: geometrically average the n-step returns: $$G_t^\lambda = (1-\lambda) \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \lambda^{n-1} G_t^{(n)}.$$ "Backward" view, implementable online with one eligibility trace per state: $$e_t(s) = \gamma \lambda\, e_{t-1}(s) + \mathbb{1}[S_t = s], \qquad V_{t+1}(s) = V_t(s) + \alpha\, \delta_t\, e_t(s) \quad \forall s.$$ The trace accumulates the states "responsible" for the current reward and fades as \((\gamma\lambda)^k\). Equivalence theorem (Sutton & Barto, ch. 12): for tabular evaluation with fixed steps, the two views realize the same cumulative update over an episode — equivalence stated here without proof.
Proposition 3.4 — GAE: derivation of the generalized advantage estimator Set the TD error for the critic \(V\): \(\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t)\). Then:
Step 1 (telescoping). By immediate recursion, $$G_t^{(n)} - V(S_t) = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \gamma^l\, \delta_{t+l} \qquad (\text{n-step advantage, } A_t^{(n)}).$$ Step 2 (geometric mixing). The GAE estimator is the combination \((1-\lambda)\sum_n \lambda^{n-1} A_t^{(n)}\) — the same as TD(\(\lambda\)) — and the telescoping propagates to the sum: $$\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}.$$ Bias-variance reading. \(\lambda = 0\) gives \(\hat A_t = \delta_t\): one bootstrap step, strong bias (if \(V\) is poor), low variance. \(\lambda = 1\) gives \(\hat A_t = G_t - V(S_t)\): Monte-Carlo, no bootstrap bias, maximal variance. GAE allows positioning oneself between the two — it is the standard estimator of the algorithms of chapters 4 and 5 (Schulman et al., 2016). ∎
Theorem 3.5 — Q-learning overestimation: the bias of the max, demonstrated For all real random variables \(X, Y\) with finite expectations: $$\mathbb{E}[\max(X, Y)] \;\geq\; \max\big(\mathbb{E}[X], \mathbb{E}[Y]\big),$$ with strict inequality as soon as \(X \neq Y\) with positive probability.
We write \(\max(x,y) = \tfrac12\big(x + y + |x - y|\big)\). The function \((x,y) \mapsto |x - y|\) is convex, so by Jensen \(\mathbb{E}|X - Y| \geq |\mathbb{E}X - \mathbb{E}Y|\). Hence: $$\mathbb{E}[\max(X,Y)] = \tfrac12\big(\mathbb{E}X + \mathbb{E}Y + \mathbb{E}|X-Y|\big) \geq \tfrac12\big(\mathbb{E}X + \mathbb{E}Y + |\mathbb{E}X - \mathbb{E}Y|\big) = \max(\mathbb{E}X, \mathbb{E}Y),$$ strict if \(\mathbb{E}|X-Y| > |\mathbb{E}X - \mathbb{E}Y|\), i.e. if \(X - Y\) is not a.s. constant. Moreover \(\mathbb{E}[\max(X,Y)] \leq \max(\mathbb{E}X, \mathbb{E}Y) + \tfrac12\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(X - Y)^2]}\): the bias grows with the noise. ∎
Application: the Q-learning target, \(y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')\), takes the expectation of a max of noisy Q-values (network, sampling): the max preferentially selects overestimates, and the bootstrap propagates the bias — hence systematically optimistic values and overexcited policies.
Algorithm 3.6 — Double Q-learning: the correction Maintain two independent estimators \(Q_A, Q_B\) (each updated on half of the transitions, or randomly swapped), and decouple selection and evaluation: $$a^\star = \arg\max_a Q_A(s', a), \qquad y = r + \gamma\, Q_B(s', a^\star), \qquad Q_A(s,a) \leftarrow Q_A(s,a) + \alpha\big[y - Q_A(s,a)\big]$$ (and symmetrically by swapping \(A \leftrightarrow B\)). Since \(\mathbb{E}[Q_B(s', a^\star)]\) is taken without a max over the noise of \(Q_B\), the positive bias of Theorem 3.5 disappears (the argmax is still computed on \(Q_A\), but the evaluation no longer "picks the noise that suits it"). This is the tabular version of van Hasselt (2010); the deep version (Double DQN, chapter 5) uses the current network and the target network as the two estimators.
Exploration: \(\varepsilon\)-greedy policy With probability \(\varepsilon\): random action; otherwise \(\arg\max_a Q(s, a)\). Without exploration, the learning signal dies out; decreasing \(\varepsilon\) is the standard setting — chapter 6 systematizes this choice.
The deadly triad Function approximation (networks) + bootstrapping (TD targets) + off-policy (Q-learning): combining the three can make the iteration diverge (Baird's counterexample, 1995). The convergence proof of Theorem 3.2 collapses as soon as the operator is no longer a contraction within the approximation class. The whole of chapter 5 is an art of living with this triad: replay, target networks, clipping, double Q.
Q-learning ≠ SARSA in one sentence Q-learning learns the optimal policy whatever the exploration policy (off-policy); SARSA evaluates the policy actually followed (including exploration) — more cautious near cliffs! (Classic lab: Cliff Walking, exercise 4.)
