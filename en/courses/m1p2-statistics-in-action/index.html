How to run a statistical study end to end on real data: plan the trial, randomize, compute the required power, hunt biases, correct multiple tests, bootstrap, then communicate honest results? This module teaches the statistician's craft rather than the theory of tests: every section starts from a concrete situation, from the agricultural trial to the A/B test. This methodological rigor is a valuable differentiator in a machine learning master's program, where the data collection plan often makes the difference between a result and an illusion.

What you will be able to do

  • Run the complete cycle of a statistical study, from the initial question to communicable conclusions.
  • Design an experimental plan: randomization, blocks, and a power computation suited to the objective.
  • Identify the biases of observational studies and the classic pitfalls of inference.
  • Apply the basics of causal inference: potential outcomes and difference-in-differences (DiD).
  • Practice regression for real: diagnostics, transformations, reasoned decisions.
  • Correct multiple tests (Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg) according to the goal.
  • Build intervals and tests by bootstrap on arbitrary data.
  • Run a modern A/B test: sequential, targeted effects — and report the results honestly.

0. Review: the statistician's survival kit

L3 background used (cf. Refresher Statistics module)
  • Convergence and limit theorems (ch. 4): law of large numbers, CLT \(\sqrt{n}(\bar X_n - \mu) \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), delta method and bootstrap — the engine of almost every approximation in this module;
  • Parametric estimation (ch. 5): bias, variance, quadratic error, maximum likelihood estimators and their asymptotic properties;
  • Confidence intervals and tests (ch. 6): level, power, p-value, type I and II errors, two-sample Student test, chi-square test;
  • Linear regression (ch. 7): ordinary least squares, Gauss-Markov, tests on the coefficients, \(R^2\) — chapter 5 of this module revisits this foundation "on misbehaving data";
  • Monte Carlo simulation: generating laws, checking a method by simulation — the permanent working tool of the applied statistician (and of all the labs in this course).
Definition 0.1 — Statistical model and data A statistical model for data \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) is a family \((P_\theta)_{\theta \in \Theta}\) of candidate laws. In practice, the question is not "is the model true?" (it never is) but rather: what question are we asking the data, and which quantity \(\psi(\theta)\) — mean effect, difference of rates, quantile, coefficient — answers that question? The whole module is organized around this triptych: question → collection plan → estimation + uncertainty. The formula \(\bar x \pm 1.96\, s/\sqrt{n}\) from Refresher Statistics is legitimate only if the collection plan is: this module deals with what happens before and around the formula.
Proposition 0.2 — The bias-variance identity and what it implies For every square-integrable estimator \(\hat\theta\) of \(\theta\): $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}\hat\theta - \theta\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)}_{\text{variance}}.$$
Set \(\mu = \mathbb{E}\hat\theta\) and expand \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta-\mu) + (\mu - \theta)]^2 = \mathbb{E}(\hat\theta-\mu)^2 + 2(\mu-\theta)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}(\hat\theta - \mu)}_{=0} + (\mu-\theta)^2\). The cross term is zero because \(\mu\) is the expectation of \(\hat\theta\). ∎
In practice, the bias mostly comes from the plan (selection, confounding) and the variance from the sample size: increasing \(n\) never corrects a selection bias — this is the central message of chapters 2 and 3.
Position in the master's program This module is the "craft" complement of Refresher Statistics: less new theory, more decisions. It also prepares the M2 module "Privacy and Uncertainty Quantification", which pushes the uncertainty question as far as the calibration of predictive models; the statistics–ML bridge is provided by the Machine Learning module (ch. 1 and 7), and the processing of textual data by the NLP module.

1. The life cycle of a statistical study

A real statistical study does not begin with a dataset: it begins with a question. The diagram below structures the whole module; each chapter tackles a link where studies typically fail.

Definition 1.1 — Population, sample, statistical unit, indicator The population is the set of units the question concerns; the sample is the observed subset; the statistical unit is the grain at which we measure (a patient, a field, a user, a query); the indicator (or outcome, OEC for "Overall Evaluation Criterion" in online experimentation) is the quantity measured. A study is externally valid if the sample allows an answer for the target population; internally valid if the comparisons made within the sample are reliable. One can be internally valid and externally wrong (a trial on healthy volunteers).
Reproducibility: HARKing and the garden of forking paths Two documented pathologies: HARKing (Kerr, 1998 — hypotheses formulated after seeing the results, presented as a priori) and the "garden of forking paths" (Gelman & Loken, 2013): without any fraud, the mere freedom to choose the specification after the fact (control variables, coding, exclusion of points, subgroups) multiplies the tests actually performed far beyond those reported. Practical answers: pre-registration of the analysis plan, reproducible scripts, reports of every look taken at the data — exactly the disciplines of ch. 8 for A/B testing.

2. Experimental designs: randomization, blocks, power

Definition 2.1 — Randomized experiment (Fisher, 1935) \(n\) experimental units receive \(K\) treatments; the assignment is drawn at random among the possible allocations (with possible balance constraints). Randomization plays three roles: (i) on average, it balances all factors, measured or not; (ii) it grounds an exact test of the null effect (prop. 2.2); (iii) it legitimizes the causal inference of ch. 4. Historical note: the "lady tasting tea" experiment (Fisher, 1935) — 8 cups, 4 "milk first", 4 "tea first", randomized order.
Proposition 2.2 — Exact randomization test, computed by hand Six plots with yields 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 (q/ha; teaching data) receive A or B, three each. Observed: A = {6, 7, 8}, B = {3, 4, 5}, difference of means \(\hat d = 7 - 4 = 3\). Under the null hypothesis "no effect", \(\hat d\) takes each of the \(\binom{6}{3} = 20\) values \(\bar x_A - \bar x_B\) with probability \(1/20\). The largest possible value of \(|\hat d|\) is 3, reached only for the splits {6,7,8} and {3,4,5} (the split {5,7,8} against {3,4,6} gives \(\hat d = 6.67 - 4.33 = 2.33 < 3\)). Therefore $$p \;=\; \mathbb{P}\big(|\hat d| \geq 3 \,\big|\, H_0\big) \;=\; \frac{2}{20} \;=\; 0.10 \quad\text{(exact, without any distributional assumption).}$$
The only thing used: under \(H_0\), the treatment does not influence the yield, so the observed value of \(\hat d\) is piecewise constant and only the assignment is random; the assignment being uniform over the 20 balanced allocations, the p-value is the proportion of allocations at least as extreme. Enumerating the 20 splits (or a symmetry argument on A/B) gives exactly 2 splits with \(|\bar x_A - \bar x_B| = 3\) and all others \(\leq 2.33\). ∎
Definition 2.3 — Randomized complete block design (RCBD) When the units are heterogeneous but can be grouped into homogeneous blocks (neighboring plots, production days, hospitals), one draws independently within each block a permutation of the \(K\) treatments. Analysis model: $$y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \ \text{i.i.d.},$$ with \(\tau_i\) the effect of treatment \(i\), \(\beta_j\) the effect of block \(j\). Blocks turn the variability between units into an explicit factor: ch. 9 shows, on numerical data, a variance gain by a factor of 30 and more.
Theorem 2.4 — Sample size for a target power (two-sample Gaussian test) To test \(H_0 : \mu_A = \mu_B\) against \(\mu_B - \mu_A = \delta\) at level \(\alpha\) (two-sided) with common variance \(\sigma^2\), \(n\) measurements per group and the normal approximation, the power is $$\pi(\delta) \;=\; \Phi\!\Big( \frac{\delta}{\sigma}\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{2}} - z_{1-\alpha/2} \Big),$$ and power \(1 - \beta\) is reached for $$n \;=\; \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{(\delta/\sigma)^2} \quad \text{measurements per group.}$$
The test rejects when \(|\hat d| > z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma\sqrt{2/n}\) with \(\hat d = \bar y_B - \bar y_A \sim \mathcal{N}(\delta, 2\sigma^2/n)\). Under the alternative, \(\mathbb{P}(\hat d < -s) \approx 0\) (with \(s = z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma\sqrt{2/n}\) and \(\delta > 0\)), so \(\pi = \mathbb{P}(\hat d > s) = \Phi\big(\tfrac{\delta - s}{\sigma\sqrt{2/n}}\big) = \Phi\big(\tfrac{\delta}{\sigma}\sqrt{\tfrac n2} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\big)\). Writing \(\pi = 1 - \beta\) amounts to \(\tfrac{\delta}{\sigma}\sqrt{\tfrac n2} - z_{1-\alpha/2} = z_{1-\beta}\), hence the formula by isolating \(n\). ∎
Example 2.5 — Power computed, and a sizing table We want to detect \(\delta = 0.3\,\sigma\) (a small but economically relevant effect) with \(\alpha = 5\,\%\) and 80 % power: \(z_{0.975} = 1.960\), \(z_{0.8} = 0.842\), $$n = \frac{2\,(1.960 + 0.842)^2}{0.3^2} = \frac{2 \times 7.85}{0.09} = 174.4 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{175 \text{ units per group}}.$$ Complete table (values rounded up):
\(\delta/\sigma\)Power 80 %Power 90 %Reading
0.2393526very small effect: costly
0.3175234our case
0.56385medium effect
0.82533large effect: a pilot suffices
Professional moral: half the power is paid in the square root of n, doubling the precision costs ×4 the sample, and nobody "finds" an effect of 0.2σ with 30 observations. In practice one replaces \(\sigma^2\) with an estimate from a pilot or the literature, and \(\delta\) with the smallest useful effect (the minimum clinically or economically relevant difference), never with the effect one hopes for.
Power a priori, never a posteriori Power is a design tool. The "observed power" computed after the fact with \(\hat\delta\) is a monotone transformation of the p-value: it brings no new information and muddles the report (Hoenig & Heisey, 2001). What should be reported: the confidence interval of the effect (ch. 10).

3. Observational versus experimental: biases

Definition 3.1 — Observational study, confounding factor A study is observational when the assignment of "treatments" (exposure, product, degree) is not controlled by the experimenter. A confounding factor \(Z\) is a variable that influences both the exposure and the response; it creates a "natural" difference of means between groups even without any effect. Canonical example: ice cream sales and drownings (confounded by weather). Adjustment (standardization, regression, matching) only corrects measured confounders; randomization also neutralizes the unmeasured ones — that is the whole difference between ch. 2 and the present chapter.
Theorem 3.2 — Simpson's paradox, real data: kidney stones (Charig et al., 1986) Historical comparison of two kidney stone treatments (B: minimally invasive surgery; A: open surgery), % success:
StratumTreatment ATreatment B
Small stones81/87 = 93 %234/270 = 87 %
Large stones192/263 = 73 %55/80 = 69 %
Overall273/350 = 78 %289/350 = 83 %
A is better in every stratum and worse in aggregate: B was preferentially given to small stones (easy cases). This is a pure confounding effect by severity, mechanically reversible by standardization.
Let \(p_{ks}\) be the success rate of treatment \(k\) in stratum \(s\) and \(w_s^{(k)}\) the proportion of patients of stratum \(s\) in group \(k\). The overall rate of group \(k\) is \(\sum_s w_s^{(k)}\, p_{ks}\): it mixes effectiveness (\(p\)) and case mix (\(w\)). B can win in aggregate with \(p_{Bs} \leq p_{As}\) for every \(s\) as soon as \(w^{(B)}\) loads the easy strata: here \(w^{(B)}_{\text{small}} = 270/350 = 0.77\) versus \(87/350 = 0.25\) for A. Standardizing on a common profile \(w\) (e.g. \(w^{(A)}\)) restores A's advantage in each stratum, hence on average. ∎
Definition 3.3 — Selection bias and survivorship bias Selection bias: the sample is not representative of the target population (volunteers, trade shows, survey respondents). Survivorship bias: the special case where the filter is survival / still being in activity; the departed carry their bad results away with them.
Example 3.4 — Two worked examples (a) Wald's airplanes (1943). Returning from missions, bullet holes concentrate on the wings and fuselage, almost none on the engines. Reinforcing the hit zones would be the survivor's error: planes hit in the engine do not come back. The armor goes where the survivors are untouched. (The episode, popularized by W. Allen, is reconstructed and discussed by Mangel & Samuels, 2002.)

(b) Mutual funds (teaching figures calibrated on Elton, Gruber & Blake, 1996). Of 200 funds existing in 2015, 100 closed before 2025. Advertising cites the average return of the 100 survivors: 8 %/year. The 100 vanished ones made −5 %/year before closing. The return actually experienced by a 2015 investor: $$\frac{100 \times 8 + 100 \times (-5)}{200} = 1.5\,\%/\text{year} \quad \text{versus} \quad 8\,\% \text{ displayed.}$$ Over 10 years, a compounded gap of 1 %/year ≈ 10 % of missing final wealth — enough to rank every product on the market differently.
Where do you meet these biases in ML? Everywhere: models trained on current customers (churners do not notify their departure); evaluations of a recruitment system on hires (non-hires have no observed performance — this is the crux of ch. 4); academic benchmarks filtered by the publications that use them (failures are not submitted). Reflex: always ask how the sample was constituted and who dropped out of it.

4. Causal inference: potential outcomes and DiD

Definition 4.1 — Potential outcomes, ATE (Neyman 1923; Rubin 1974) For each unit \(i\) and treatment \(w \in \{0, 1\}\), we posit two potential outcomes \(Y_i(0)\) and \(Y_i(1)\); the individual effect is \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) and the average effect \(\mathrm{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[\tau_i]\). We only ever observe one value \(Y_i = W_i Y_i(1) + (1 - W_i) Y_i(0)\): the fundamental causal problem is a missing data problem (cf. Refresher Statistics, ch. 9). Two a priori assumptions: consistency (the observed value equals the potential outcome of the received treatment) and no interference (the treatment of unit \(j\) does not affect \(i\)) — together SUTVA.
Theorem 4.2 — Randomization debiases the difference of means If \(W \perp (Y(0), Y(1))\) (unbiased randomization), then \(\hat\tau = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0\) is unbiased for the ATE, and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau) \approx \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_0^2}{n_0}\).
\(\mathbb{E}[\bar Y_1 \mid W] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid W = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\) by independence, and likewise for \(\bar Y_0\), hence \(\mathbb{E}\hat\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)] = \mathrm{ATE}\). The two means are over disjoint, hence independent, groups, which gives the sum of the empirical variances. ∎
In an observational setting, \(W\) is self-selected: \(\mathbb{E}[Y(1)\mid W{=}1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid W{=}0]\) mixes the causal effect with selection. The whole modern machinery (adjustment, matching, instrumental variables, DiD) aims at reconstructing a randomized comparison.
Definition 4.3 — Difference-in-differences, the sketch Two groups (treated, control), two dates (before, after), without randomization: $$\widehat{\mathrm{DiD}} \;=\; \big(\bar Y_{\text{after, treated}} - \bar Y_{\text{before, treated}}\big) - \big(\bar Y_{\text{after, control}} - \bar Y_{\text{before, control}}\big).$$ Central assumption: parallel trends — without treatment, the treated/control gap would have stayed constant; the control serves as a "trajectory counterfactual". This is the most used causal identification in applied economics since Card & Krueger (1994).
Example 4.4 — Minimum wage New Jersey / Pennsylvania (Card & Krueger, 1994) Rise of the NJ minimum wage (April 1992); neighboring Pennsylvania serves as the control. Average full-time employment (FTE) per restaurant:
BeforeAfterChange
New Jersey (treated)20.4421.03+0.59
Pennsylvania (control)23.3321.17−2.16
$$\widehat{\mathrm{DiD}} = 0.59 - (-2.16) = +2.75 \ \text{FTE} \quad (\approx +11\,\% \text{ vs before}).$$ The regional economy was depressing both states; the naive "NJ before/after" comparison (+0.59) would underestimate the effect, and the "NJ/PA after" comparison (−1.14 FTE) would outright flip its sign: only the double difference neutralizes the common shock. 5. Regression in practice: diagnostics and transformations

Reminders (cf. Refresher Statistics module, ch. 7)
  • OLS estimator \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\), residuals \(e = y - \hat y\), hat matrix \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\), \(\hat y = Hy\);
  • Gauss-Markov: BLUE under i.i.d. zero-mean errors; Student tests if moreover Gaussian;
  • The Machine Learning module (ch. 2) covers ridge/lasso regularization — here we stay in inferential mode: the coefficients are the object of interest.
Proposition 5.1 — Leverage and Cook's distance The leverage of observation \(i\) is \(h_{ii}\), the diagonal of \(H\); \(\sum_i h_{ii} = p\) (number of parameters), \(0 \leq h_{ii} \leq 1\). Cook's distance $$D_i = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,s^2}\cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}$$ measures the influence of \(i\) on the whole set of coefficients. Leverage point: \(h_{ii} > 2p/n\); influential point: \(D_i > 4/n\).
For the trace: \(H\) is an orthogonal projector (onto im \(X\)), and the trace of a projector equals its rank \(= \mathrm{rank}(X) = p\). Removing row \(i\) and re-estimating gives \(\hat\beta - \hat\beta_{(-i)} = \frac{(X^\top X)^{-1}x_i e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}\); Cook's distance standardizes this variation by the dispersion \(p s^2\) of the estimators — hence the formula: the residual alone (an outlier can be well predicted!) or the leverage alone (an extreme point can be perfectly aligned) do not suffice; it is their product that tips the line. ∎
Definition 5.2 — The four diagnostic plots (1) Residuals vs fitted values: curvature ⇒ non-linearity (add quadratic terms, splines); funnel ⇒ heteroscedasticity. (2) Q-Q of residuals: heavy tails ⇒ fragile intervals/Student tests at small \(n\). (3) Scale-location (\(\sqrt{|e|}\) vs \(\hat y\)): checks the constancy of \(s^2\). (4) Residuals vs leverage (Cook): hunts influential points. Professional reflex: these four plots before any interpretation, systematically.
Example 5.3 — Transformations: the log as a universal tool Prices of 30 goods (\(y\), euros) against surface area (\(x\), m²). Typical symptoms: funnel-shaped residuals, the line pulled by a few very high-leverage luxury goods. Remedy: regress \(\log y\) on \(\log x\): $$\log y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log x_i + \varepsilon_i \iff y_i = e^{\beta_0}\, x_i^{\beta_1}\, e^{\varepsilon_i}.$$ \(\beta_1\) becomes an elasticity: \(+1\,\%\) of surface ⇒ \(+\beta_1\,\%\) of price. If \(\hat\beta_1 = 1.08\), the interpretation "each extra square meter adds 8 %" replaces the fragile linear interpretation "each square meter adds €3,120" — wrong for a studio as for a mansion. The log transformation: (i) symmetrizes distributions and stabilizes variance when the effect is multiplicative; (ii) crushes extreme leverages; (iii) makes errors relative, which is often the right physical/economic model. General family: Box-Cox \(y^{(\lambda)} = (y^\lambda - 1)/\lambda\), with \(\lambda\) chosen by profile likelihood; special cases \(\lambda = 1\) (identity), \(\lambda = 0\) (log), \(\lambda = 1/2\) (square root, Poisson).
Heteroscedasticity: robust errors, no panic Heteroscedasticity does not bias \(\hat\beta\) (Gauss-Markov only needs homoscedasticity for efficiency, not consistency); it distorts the standard errors. Modern solution: sandwich estimators (White, HC1) — \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta) = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathrm{diag}(e_i^2) X (X^\top X)^{-1}\) — available everywhere (statsmodels, sandwich in R). With grouped or panel data, cluster by unit (cf. ch. 4, Bertrand et al. 2004).

6. Multiple testing: Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg

The modern statistician tests thousands of hypotheses at once (genomics, multi-metric A/B testing, subgroup analyses). Testing \(m\) true null hypotheses each at \(\alpha = 5\,\%\) produces on average \(\alpha m\) false positives — with \(m = 1000\): 50 "discoveries" in pure noise. One must choose what to control.

Definition 6.1 — FWER and FDR Among \(R\) rejected hypotheses of which \(V\) are false (false positives): $$\mathrm{FWER} = \mathbb{P}(V \geq 1) \qquad \text{(at least one error)}$$ $$\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{V}{R \vee 1} \right] \qquad \text{(expected proportion of errors).}$$ Controlling the FWER is required when a single false discovery is costly (pivotal clinical trial); controlling the FDR is enough for exploration (differential expression, product optimization) — we accept some noise among the discoveries.
Theorem 6.2 — Bonferroni controls the FWER Rejecting the \(p_i \leq \alpha/m\) gives \(\mathrm{FWER} \leq \alpha\).
Union bound (Boole): if an error is made, at least one p-value under a true null has crossed its threshold, so $$\mathrm{FWER} \leq \mathbb{P}\Big( \bigcup_{i \in I_0} \{p_i \leq \alpha/m\} \Big) \leq \sum_{i \in I_0} \frac{\alpha}{m} \leq \frac{m_0\,\alpha}{m} \leq \alpha,$$ where \(m_0 \leq m\) is the number of true nulls. No independence is required. ∎
Price to pay: as \(m\) grows, the threshold \(\alpha/m\) becomes untenable (\(m = 10^4\) ⇒ \(5 \times 10^{-6}\)) and power collapses; the Šidák variant (\(1 - (1-\alpha)^{1/m}\), exact under independence) does not change the order of magnitude.
Theorem 6.3 — Benjamini-Hochberg controls the FDR (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) Let \(p_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq p_{(m)}\) be the sorted p-values and $$k^* = \max\Big\{ k : p_{(k)} \leq \frac{k}{m}\,q \Big\}.$$ Rejecting the \(k^*\) smallest p-values (none if the set is empty) controls \(\mathrm{FDR} \leq \frac{m_0}{m} q \leq q\) when the p-values under true nulls are independent of each other and of the rest (extension: PRDS positive dependence, Benjamini & Yekutieli 2001; otherwise, replace \(q\) by \(q / \sum_{k=1}^m 1/k\)).
Case where all nulls are true (\(m_0 = m\)). Then \(V = R\) whenever \(R \geq 1\), so \(V/(R \vee 1) = \mathbf{1}\{R \geq 1\}\) and \(\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{P}(R \geq 1)\). Now \(R \geq 1\) requires \(p_{(1)} \leq q/m\), and \(p_{(1)} = \min_i p_i\) with i.i.d. uniform \(p_i\): $$\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{P}\Big(\min_i p_i \leq \frac{q}{m}\Big) = 1 - \Big(1 - \frac{q}{m}\Big)^m \;\leq\; q.$$ General case (sketch). Decompose \(\mathrm{FDR} = \sum_{i \in I_0} \mathbb{E}\big[ \tfrac{1}{R} \mathbf{1}\{p_i \leq p_{(k^*)}\}\big]\) over the rejection rank. The two ingredients of the complete proof (Benjamini & Hochberg, Thm 1): (i) for each null \(i\), the event "\(i\) rejected at rank \(r\)" requires \(p_i \leq qr/m\) and at least \(r - 1\) of the other p-values \(\leq qr/m\); (ii) by independence of \(p_i\) from the rest, the product of probabilities yields a factor \(qr/m\), which, killed by the \(1/r\) of the weighting, leaves the bound \(\frac{q}{m}\) — then the telescoping summation over ranks (Abel's transformation) bounds the total by \(\frac{m_0}{m} q\). The subtlety (and beauty) of the result: the data-dependent threshold \(q k^*/m\) "self-adjusts" to the number of apparent discoveries. ∎

7. The bootstrap in practice

Reminder (cf. Refresher Statistics module, ch. 4)
The nonparametric bootstrap resamples \(B\) times, with replacement, \(n\) observations, recomputes the statistic on each resample, and reads the variability off the bootstrap distribution. Here we push through to intervals, edge cases and code.
Definition 7.1 — The plug-in principle (Efron, 1979) The empirical law \(\hat P_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \delta_{x_i}\) is the natural nonparametric estimator of the unknown law \(P\). The bootstrap simulates the inference problem by replacing \(P\) with \(\hat P_n\): if \(T_n = t(x_1, \dots, x_n\,; P)\) is the statistic of interest, its bootstrap version \(T_n^* = t(x_1^*, \dots, x_n^* ; \hat P_n)\) is computed by Monte Carlo. Ideal: the law of \(T_n^* - t(\hat P_n)\) approximates that of \(T_n - t(P)\).
Theorem 7.2 — Consistency for the mean (and beyond, sketch) If \(x_i\) are i.i.d., \(\mathbb{E}X^2 < \infty\), the law of \(\sqrt n(\bar x^* - \bar x)\) conditional on the data converges to the same limit \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) as \(\sqrt n(\bar x_n - \mu)\).
Conditional on the data, the \(x_i^*\) are i.i.d. with law \(\hat P_n\), expectation \(\bar x\) and variance \(\hat\sigma_n^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2\). The CLT applied to this sequence (for almost every draw of the data) gives \(\sqrt n(\bar x^* - \bar x) \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \hat\sigma_n^2)\), and \(\hat\sigma_n^2 \to \sigma^2\) almost surely (LLN) — the conditional Slutsky lemma concludes. The same scheme holds for regular functionals of \(P\) (differentiable functionals — functional delta method); this is the subject of the empirical Donsker theorem and van der Vaart's theory (ch. 23). ∎
Example 7.3 — Three intervals for the median Eight response times (ms): 118, 132, 127, 141, 122, 135, 128, 240 (one heavy point). Observed median: \((128+132)/2 = 130\). A "hand-made" normal CI: delicate (no simple \(\sqrt n\) formula for the median). Bootstrap with \(B = 2000\): draw 2000 resamples, each yields a median \(m_b^*\); take the 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles; typical output: CI ≈ [124; 137]. The basic interval (reverse percentile) reverses: \([2\hat m - q_{97.5}^*,\ 2\hat m - q_{2.5}^*]\), which corrects a version bias; BCa additionally readjusts for bias and skewness via jackknife. Reading rule: if percentile and basic differ strongly, the bootstrap distribution is very asymmetric — a signal in itself. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

x = np.array([118, 132, 127, 141, 122, 135, 128, 240])
n, B = len(x), 5000
med_star = np.median(rng.choice(x, size=(B, n), replace=True), axis=1)
mhat = np.median(x)

# 1. percentile: quantiles of the bootstrap law
lo, hi = np.quantile(med_star, [0.025, 0.975])
# 2. basic (reverse percentile): symmetrization around the estimate
lo_b, hi_b = 2*mhat - hi, 2*mhat - lo
print(f"mediane = {mhat}, IC percentile [{lo:.0f}, {hi:.0f}], IC basic [{lo_b:.0f}, {hi_b:.0f}]")
# 3. bootstrap bias: E*[m*] - mhat
print(f"biais bootstrap estime : {med_star.mean() - mhat:+.2f} ms")
When the bootstrap fails Dependent data: the i.i.d. bootstrap breaks the structure — use the block bootstrap (weather, financial series, web streams); extremes and deep quantiles: the empirical law does not have a rich enough tail; small \(n\) (< 10): the empirical law is too coarse a scale (at most \(n^n\) distinct resamples, and often far fewer distinct values of the statistic); non-regular statistics (boundaries, number of components): the bootstrap can be inconsistent. Research bridge: Naulet, Roy, Sharma & Veitch use weighted bootstraps for the graphex tail index — the estimator of the "hidden mass" of large networks (ch. 11).

8. A/B testing: sequential and targeted effects

Definition 8.1 — A/B test and OEC An online randomized experiment: every unit (user, query, session — the randomization unit must be the analysis unit) receives variant A (control) or B (treatment). The OEC (Overall Evaluation Criterion) is the single decision metric, defined before the experiment; secondary metrics (guardrails: latency, error rate, churn) are tested with multiple-testing correction (ch. 6). Discipline: no OEC, no test.
Theorem 8.2 — The cost of "peeking" (looking before the end) A fixed-horizon test of level \(\alpha\), examined \(K\) times with stopping as soon as a p-value drops below \(\alpha\), has a false positive rate $$\mathbb{P}(\exists k \leq K : p_k \leq \alpha) \;\geq\; \alpha \quad \text{and} \quad \leq\; 1 - (1 - \alpha)^K \ \text{(if independent).}$$ For \(K = 5\) looks and \(\alpha = 5\,\%\), already \(1 - 0.95^5 = 22.6\,\%\); with daily continuous monitoring over weeks, Johari, Pekelis, Walsh and Ye (KDD 2017) measure rates reaching about three times the nominal level.
The lower bound is clear (the first look is a level-\(\alpha\) test). The upper bound: union bound over the \(K\) events \(\{p_k \leq \alpha\}\), each of probability \(\leq \alpha\) under \(H_0\) — with equality × independence when the looks are independent tests (a rough approximation: in practice successive p-values are highly correlated, which makes the true rate smaller than the bound but far above \(\alpha\)). Hence the simulation of exercise 7. ∎
Definition 8.3 — Always-valid p-values and mSPRT (Johari et al., 2017) A sequential p-value \(p_t\) is always valid if \(\mathbb{P}(\exists t : p_t \leq \alpha) \leq \alpha\) for every stopping time — one may then look as often as desired. Construction: a mixture of sequential probability ratio tests (mSPRT); the mirror Bayesian version: monitor the posterior of \(d = p_B - p_A\) and its credible interval at every instant (code below), with a non-informative Beta prior; the two families correspond asymptotically (same boundaries in \(t\), cf. the Reinforcement Learning module ch. 6 for bandits, the other answer to the problem). 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(52)

# ---- Bayesian A/B test: Beta posteriors, decision by expected loss ----
conv = np.array([1274, 1378])          # conversions
n    = np.array([12000, 12000])        # visits
a, b = 1.0, 1.0                        # Beta(1, 1) prior
post = [rng.beta(conv[i] + a, n[i] - conv[i] + b, size=200_000) for i in range(2)]

d = post[1] - post[0]                  # absolute uplift drawn a posteriori
loss_if_B = np.where(d < 0, -d, 0.0)   # loss if we deploy B and A was better

print(f"P(B > A)            : {(d > 0).mean():.3f}")
print(f"gain median         : {np.median(d):.5f}")
print(f"IC 95 % du gain     : {np.quantile(d, [0.025, 0.975])}")
print(f"E[perte | deploi B] : {loss_if_B.mean():.2e}")
# Typical outputs: P(B>A) = 0.984; median uplift = 0.0087;
# CI = [0.0007, 0.0166]; E[loss] = 2.2e-05.
# Decision: the cost of a wrong choice of B (2 conversions out of 10^5) is negligible
# compared with the expected gain (+0.87 point) -- we deploy B.
Definition 8.4 — Targeted (heterogeneous) effects and the associated errors The average effect hides heterogeneity: B wins on mobile, loses on desktop. Two symmetric pitfalls: (i) subgroup spinning: 20 segments ⇒ expect a "significant gain" in one of them by pure noise (ch. 6, Bonferroni/BH mandatory); (ii) ignored real interaction effects: the aggregated OEC can cancel out opposite effects (Simpson, ch. 3). A sound framework: specify segments a priori, test the segment × treatment interaction, correct for multiple testing. On the variance side: CUPED (Deng et al., 2013) reuses the metric measured before the experiment as a covariate — a typical "free" variance reduction of 30–50 %, statistically equivalent to the blocks of ch. 2.
Interference and validity Two-sided market (Uber: more drivers ⇒ wait time drops for everyone, including the control), CDN caches, social network effects: SUTVA (ch. 4) violated. Answers: randomize by clusters (cities, IP clusters — power loss via the design effect \(1 + (m-1)\rho\)), or isolate (switchback). See Kohavi, Tang & Xu (2020) for the complete catalog of production pitfalls; the Reinforcement Learning module (bandits, ch. 6) addresses the "exploit during the experiment" alternative.

10. Communicating: intervals, p-values, honest visualizations

Milestone 10.1 — The ASA's six principles on the p-value (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016)
  1. a p-value measures the incompatibility of the data with a specified statistical model;
  2. it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor that the result is due to chance;
  3. scientific conclusions and decisions should not pass only through a threshold (p < 0.05);
  4. full transparency: correct p-values only with the procedure and analysis reported in full;
  5. a p-value, or statistical significance, measures neither the size of an effect nor its importance;
  6. an isolated p-value proves nothing by itself — facts, context and replication make the proof.
Collective follow-up: Amrhein, Greenland & McShane (2019), "Scientists rise up against statistical significance" — more than 800 signatories: stop classifying results as "significant" / "not significant".
Example 10.2 — Same data, two stories Trial with \(n = 40\) per arm, \(\hat d = 3.1\) points, \(s = 15\), p = 0.041 (two-sided). Test story: "significant effect (p = 0.04)" — suggests clear evidence. Estimation story: 95 % CI of \(d\): \(3.1 \pm 1.96 \times 15\sqrt{2/40} = 3.1 \pm 6.6 = [-3.5; 9.7]\): the data are compatible with a null effect, a negative one, or one triple the estimate. The second story is the right one: same numbers, honest information. Corollary: "p = 0.20" does not mean "proven that the effect is null" — the CI might be [−1; +8]: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
Definition 10.3 — Honest visualizations: the rules of the craft Axes: never silently truncated on bar charts (a 2 % difference becomes a mountain); bars for counts start at zero, lines may be truncated if stated. Error bars: always say what they carry — SD (dispersion of the data), SE (precision of the mean) or a 95 % CI (inference); by default, show the CI. Raw data: overlay individual points (jitter, violin) on the summaries — 8 observations do not deserve a boxplot. Dual axis: almost always a lie. Effect size + uncertainty + n in every figure/caption; never show a percentage without a count. Synthesis reference: Cumming (2014), "The new statistics".
Example 10.3 — Arithmetic of the reproducibility crisis (Ioannidis, 2005) A field tests 1000 hypotheses, 100 of which are true. Power 80 %, \(\alpha = 5\,\%\): true positives \(= 80\), false positives \(= 0.05 \times 900 = 45\). Among the published discoveries (\(125\)), the proportion of real discoveries is $$\frac{80}{80 + 45} = 64\,\%.$$ Lower the power to 20 % (underpowered studies, cf. ch. 2): true positives 20, false 45 ⇒ 31 %. Add the biases of ch. 1 (analytical flexibility, selective publication): the PPV can plummet toward 0 — hence systematic replication, pre-registration, and the "announced n and analysis plan" standards of the industrial A/B testing world.
