1. From the neuron to the deep network

Definition 1.1 — Artificial neuron and MLP A neuron computes \(h = \sigma(\langle w, x\rangle + b)\) with \(\sigma\) a nonlinear activation function. A multilayer perceptron of depth \(L\) stacks: $$h^{(0)} = x, \qquad h^{(\ell)} = \sigma\big(W^{(\ell)} h^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}\big), \qquad f_\theta(x) = W^{(L)} h^{(L-1)} + b^{(L)}.$$ Without \(\sigma\), the network would reduce to a single affine map (exercise 2): nonlinearity is the price of expressivity. Parameter size: \(|\theta| = \sum_\ell (n_{\ell} n_{\ell-1} + n_\ell)\).

1.1. The universal approximation theorem

Theorem 1.2 — Universal approximation (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991) Let \(\sigma\) be continuous and discriminatory (it is not constant on any interval where a nontrivial signed measure vanishes — the sigmoid and \(\tanh\) qualify). Then for every \(f \in \mathcal{C}(K)\) on a compact \(K \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) and every \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exists \(g(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \alpha_j\, \sigma(w_j^\top x + b_j)\) (a single hidden layer) such that \(\sup_{x \in K} |f(x) - g(x)| \le \varepsilon\).
Guided sketch, in two parts — part (ii) is the technical core of Cybenko, part (i) gives its constructive intuition.

(i) Construction by sigmoidal "gates" (one-dimensional case). Let \(f\) be continuous on \([0,1]\). A steep sigmoid \(\sigma(k(x-a))\) tends toward the indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{x > a}\) as \(k \to \infty\); by taking differences, $$\pi_{k}(x) = \sigma(k(x - a)) - \sigma(k(x - b)), \qquad a < b,$$ tends toward \(\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(x)\) (a gate). Since \(f\) is uniformly continuous, the step function \(s_n = \sum_{i} f(t_i)\,\mathbf{1}_{[t_i, t_{i+1})}\) on a subdivision of step \(1/n\) satisfies \(\|f - s_n\|_\infty \le \omega_f(1/n) \to 0\). Replacing each indicator by a steep gate \(\pi_k\) with \(k\) large enough (the error is concentrated on two intervals of width \(\sim 1/k\) around \(a\) and \(b\), where it is bounded), one obtains \(\|f - g\|_\infty \le \varepsilon\) with \(g = \sum_i f(t_i)\,\pi_{k,i}\) — a finite linear combination of sigmoids.

(ii) Density by Hahn–Banach (the general argument). Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the vector space spanned by the \(x \mapsto \sigma(w^\top x + b)\). Suppose the closure of \(\mathcal{G}\) for \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) is not \(\mathcal{C}(K)\). By the Hahn–Banach theorem (separation of a missing dense subspace), there exists a nonzero finite signed measure \(\mu\) on \(K\) such that \(\int g \,d\mu = 0\) for every \(g \in \mathcal{G}\), hence in particular $$\int_K \sigma(w^\top x + b)\, d\mu(x) = 0 \qquad \forall\, w \in \mathbb{R}^d,\ b \in \mathbb{R}.$$ Cybenko's technical lemma then asserts that \(\mu = 0\) — a contradiction. The idea of the lemma: if \(\hat\mu(t) = \int e^{t^\top x} d\mu(x)\) denotes the Fourier–Laplace transform of \(\mu\), the vanishing above for a "rich enough" sigmoid forces \(\hat\mu \equiv 0\) on an open set, hence \(\mu = 0\) by injectivity of the Fourier transform on finite measures.

∎
What the theorem does not say It guarantees the existence of good weights, not that one can find them by optimization: the proof constructs sigmoids of slope \(k \to \infty\), a regime gradient descent does not explore; and the required number of neurons \(J\) can be exponential in \(d\). Depth partially corrects this last point.
Why depth? Some functions require an exponential number of neurons on one layer but a polynomial number on several (e.g. parity, products). Depth factorizes representations: edges → textures → objects (vision), characters → words → syntax (NLP), neighbors → communities → graphs (Graph ML).

1.2. Initialization: derivation of Xavier and He

A bad initialization produces activations that explode or vanish from the forward pass onward. Analyze a linear layer \(a = Wh\) with \(h \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\), \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}} \times n_{\mathrm{in}}}\), iid centered weights of variance \(v_W\), iid centered inputs of variance \(v_h\), independent of the weights.

Proposition 1.3 — Forward and backward variance $$\mathrm{Var}(a_i) = n_{\mathrm{in}}\, v_W\, v_h \quad\text{(forward pass)}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}\big[(\nabla_h)_j\big] = n_{\mathrm{out}}\, v_W\, v_\delta \quad\text{(backward pass, } \nabla_h = W^\top \delta\text{)}.$$ To preserve the variance in both directions, one needs \(v_W = 1/n_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(v_W = 1/n_{\mathrm{out}}\) simultaneously; the harmonic compromise of Glorot & Bengio (2010) is \(v_W = 2/(n_{\mathrm{in}} + n_{\mathrm{out}})\).
Forward pass: \(a_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}} W_{ij} h_j\). The terms are independent and centered, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances: \(\mathrm{Var}(a_i) = \sum_j \mathrm{Var}(W_{ij})\,\mathrm{Var}(h_j) = n_{\mathrm{in}} v_W v_h\) (\(\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}h_j] = \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}]\mathbb{E}[h_j] = 0\)). Backward pass: symmetric computation on the columns, \((\nabla_h)_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}} W_{ij} \delta_i\). Wanting \(n_{\mathrm{in}} v_W \approx 1\) and \(n_{\mathrm{out}} v_W \approx 1\), the harmonic mean \(2/(n_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{out}})\) approximates both constraints. ∎
He (2015) for ReLU ReLU zeroes half of its inputs: if \(h = \mathrm{ReLU}(a)\) with \(a\) centered, then \(\mathbb{E}[h^2] = \tfrac12 \mathrm{Var}(a)\). To compensate this \(\tfrac12\) factor, He et al. (2015) propose \(v_W = 2/n_{\mathrm{in}}\) (and similarly a \(2/(n_{\mathrm{in}} n_{\mathrm{prev}}^{2})\)-style term for the backward pass). In practice: nn.init.kaiming_normal_ for ReLU/GELU, xavier_normal_ for tanh. Finally, zero initialization breaks the symmetry forever: all the neurons of a layer would receive the same gradient and remain identical.
