0. Review: matrix differential calculus and numerical stability

Deep learning is, 90% of it, differential calculus applied to functions with billions of parameters. This chapter consolidates the L3 tools we will need every week: differentiation of matrix functions, the central limit theorem (to understand why SGD works) and a bit of numerical stability (to understand why naive implementations produce NaNs).

L3 review — the basic mechanics
  • Chain rule: \((g \circ f)'(x) = g'(f(x))\, f'(x)\); in dimension: \(\nabla (g \circ f)(x) = J_f(x)^\top \nabla g(f(x))\), where \(J_f\) is the Jacobian matrix \((J_f)_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial x_j\).
  • Gradient of quadratic forms: \(\nabla_x \tfrac12\|Ax - b\|^2 = A^\top(Ax - b)\) and \(\nabla_x x^\top A x = (A + A^\top)x\).
  • Derivatives of the usual activations: \(\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)\), \(\tanh' = 1 - \tanh^2\), \(\mathrm{ReLU}' = \mathbf{1}_{x>0}\), and for the softmax \(\hat y = \mathrm{softmax}(z)\): \(\partial \hat y_k / \partial z_j = \hat y_k(\delta_{kj} - \hat y_j)\).
  • CLT: the mean of \(m\) iid variables with variance \(\sigma^2\) fluctuates around its expectation with standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt m\) (cf. Refresher Statistics module).

0.1. Differentiation of matrix functions

A network's parameters are matrices; one must know how to differentiate with respect to a matrix. The most convenient convention: the gradient of a function \(f : \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathbb{R}\) is the matrix \(G\) such that \(f(X + H) = f(X) + \langle G, H\rangle + o(\|H\|)\) with the Frobenius inner product \(\langle G, H \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(G^\top H)\). Three identities suffice for this whole course.

Proposition 0.1 — Useful differential identities For \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), \(a \in \mathbb{R}^m\), \(b \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(A\) of compatible size: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\,\mathrm{tr}(A^\top X) = A, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\, a^\top X b = a\, b^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\, \tfrac12\|X A - B\|_F^2 = (XA - B)A^\top.$$
We prove the second (the others are identical). Write \(a^\top X b = \sum_{i,j} a_i X_{ij} b_j = \mathrm{tr}(b a^\top X)\). The differential is \(d(a^\top X b) = a^\top H b = \langle a b^\top, H\rangle\) for any perturbation \(H\), so the gradient (in the Frobenius sense) is \(ab^\top\). For the third: \(f(X) = \tfrac12\|XA - B\|_F^2\), \(df = \langle XA - B,\; HA\rangle = \langle (XA-B)A^\top,\; H\rangle\), using the cyclicity of the trace \(\mathrm{tr}(M^\top H A) = \mathrm{tr}((M A^\top)^\top H)\) with \(M = XA - B\). ∎
Practical rule In a network, "the gradient climbs back through the transposes": if the forward pass contains \(z = Wx\), the backward pass contains \(\nabla_x = W^\top \nabla_z\) and \(\nabla_W = \nabla_z\, x^\top\). Always check the dimensions: \(\nabla_W\) has the size of \(W\), \(\nabla_x\) that of \(x\). This is the most effective quality control against backprop bugs.

0.2. CLT and mini-batch noise

Why optimize on mini-batches rather than on the whole dataset? Let \(\mathcal{D} = (\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N)\) be iid and the empirical loss \(L(\theta) = \frac1N \sum_i \ell(\theta; \xi_i)\). The mini-batch gradient \(B = (\xi_{i_1}, \dots, \xi_{i_m})\) is \(\hat g(\theta) = \frac1m \sum_{j=1}^m \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta; \xi_{i_j})\).

Proposition 0.2 — The stochastic gradient is an unbiased estimator with \(1/\sqrt m\) noise \(\mathbb{E}[\hat g] = \nabla L\) and, if the individual gradients have finite covariance \(\Sigma = \mathrm{Cov}(\nabla \ell(\theta; \xi))\), then $$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat g) = \frac{1}{m}\,\Sigma.$$
Linearity of expectation and uniform sampling: \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla \ell(\theta;\xi_i)] = \nabla L\). For the covariance, the draws are independent: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\tfrac1m\sum_j G_j) = \tfrac{1}{m^2}\sum_j \mathrm{Cov}(G_j) = \tfrac1m \Sigma\), where \(G_j = \nabla \ell(\theta; \xi_{i_j})\). The standard deviation of the estimation therefore decreases as \(\|\Sigma\|^{1/2}/\sqrt m\): multiplying the batch size by 100 divides the noise by only 10 — an economic argument for small batches, completed in chapter 3 (the noise also has a beneficial exploration effect). ∎

0.3. Numerical stability: softmax and the log-sum-exp trick

The softmax \(\hat y_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}\) appears in all classification losses and in attention. In floating-point precision (float32, max \(\approx 3{,}4 \times 10^{38}\)), the naive implementation overflows: \(e^{1000} = \infty\), and \(\infty/\infty = \mathrm{NaN}\). The universal trick is to subtract the maximum before exponentiating.

Proposition 0.3 — log-sum-exp identity and stable softmax Set \(\mathrm{LSE}(z) = \log \sum_{j=1}^n e^{z_j}\) and \(m = \max_j z_j\). Then: $$\mathrm{LSE}(z) = m + \log \sum_{j=1}^n e^{z_j - m}, \qquad m \;\le\; \mathrm{LSE}(z) \;\le\; m + \log n,$$ $$\mathrm{softmax}(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{z_j - m}},$$ and this last form never overflows.
Factorization: \(\sum_j e^{z_j} = e^{m} \sum_j e^{z_j - m}\), hence \(\mathrm{LSE}(z) = m + \log \sum_j e^{z_j - m}\). Since \(0 < e^{z_j - m} \le e^0 = 1\) and at least one term equals exactly 1 (that of the maximum), the sum lives in \([1, n]\), giving the bracket. For the softmax, dividing numerator and denominator by \(e^m > 0\) does not change the value. Stability: all the exponents \(z_i - m \le 0\), so each \(e^{z_i - m} \in (0, 1]\): no overflow, and the dominant term of the denominator equals exactly 1, so the result stays accurate even when the other terms underflow toward 0 (which is harmless: the softmax simply tends toward a one-hot). ∎ 
import torch

z = torch.tensor([1000., 1001., 999.])
# print(torch.softmax(z, 0))          # NaN with a naive implementation
print(torch.logsumexp(z, 0))          # 1001.4076: stable (PyTorch always applies it)
print(torch.softmax(z - z.max(), 0))  # stable form: [0.2447, 0.6652, 0.0900]
Numerical culture This section also prepares chapter 10: most NaNs during training come from an unprotected softmax/exponent, from the log of a zero probability (one minimizes \(-\log p\) via log-softmax, never \(-\log(\mathrm{softmax})\)), or from a too-large learning rate. The rule: when a NaN appears, walk back up the forward pass layer by layer until you find the first infinite activation.
