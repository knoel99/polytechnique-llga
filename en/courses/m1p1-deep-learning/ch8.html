8. Transfer learning and fine-tuning

Training "from scratch" has become the exception: the dominant practice consists in starting from a model pre-trained on immense corpora (ImageNet for vision, terabytes of text for LLMs) and adapting it. This chapter gives the framework and the vocabulary; the LLMs module (M2) applies it at scale.

8.1. The three adaptation regimes

  • Frozen features / linear probing: freeze the whole backbone, train a linear classifier (or a small head) on the pre-trained representations. A valuable diagnostic: feature quality is measured by the performance of a simple softmax on top (see exercise 12).
  • Full fine-tuning: retrain all the weights with a smaller step (typically 10 to 100× smaller than pre-training) — the risk of catastrophic forgetting (loss of pre-trained knowledge) is managed by a decaying step and few epochs.
  • Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT): train only a small fraction of new parameters, the rest staying frozen — all the more relevant as models grow.
Why it works The low layers learn quasi-universal features (chapter 4: edges, textures; chapter 9: graph morphology); task specificity lives in the high layers. The performance versus number of labeled domain examples curve shifts massively to the left — this is the most measurable practical benefit of modern deep learning.

8.2. Domain adaptation, in a nutshell

When the target has no labels (labeled source domain → unlabeled target domain), domain adaptation aligns the distributions: adversarial (a discriminator tries to guess the domain from the features: they must become non-discriminative), or via distribution distances (MMD, Wasserstein distances — see the Optimal Transport module). The guiding idea: learn representations invariant to the domain but discriminative for the task.

8.3. LoRA: a mathematical sketch

Definition 8.1 — LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) For a pre-trained matrix \(W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}\), one freezes \(W_0\) and learns a low-rank update: $$W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times r},\ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\ r \ll \min(d_{\mathrm{in}}, d_{\mathrm{out}}),$$ with \(B\) initialized to zero and \(A\) Gaussian: \(\Delta W = 0\) at the start, training departs exactly from the pre-trained model. \(\alpha/r\) sets the effective scale of the update.
Why a low rank suffices The working hypothesis, backed by Aghajanyan et al. (2020), is that weight updates during adaptation have a low "intrinsic rank": \(\Delta W\) is well approximated by a product \(BA\) of small rank \(r\) (4 to 64 in practice). Let us count: adapting a \(d \times d\) layer with \(r = 8\) costs \(2dr = 16d\) parameters instead of \(d^2\) — for \(d = 4096\), a factor of 1000. Other benefits: a single \(W_0\) kept in memory + per-task interchangeable \((A, B)\) pairs (instant swap); and for inference latency, one can merge \(W = W_0 + BA\) at no extra cost. The generalizations (QLoRA: quantized backbone + LoRA) are covered in the LLMs module (M2). 
import torch, torch.nn as nn

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r=8, alpha=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base                       # W0 frozen
        for p in self.base.parameters(): p.requires_grad = False
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, base.in_features) * 0.01)
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(base.out_features, r))
        self.scale = alpha / r
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.base(x) + self.scale * (x @ self.A.T @ self.B.T)
# trainable params: r*(in+out) instead of in*out
