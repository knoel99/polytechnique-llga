0. Review: vector spaces and probabilities

L3 background used
  • Vector spaces: basis, dimension, projection; cosine similarity \(\cos(u, v) = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}\) — invariant under dilation (\(\cos(u,v)=\cos(\lambda u, v)\) for \(\lambda > 0\), which "cancels out" document lengths); the \(\ell_2\) norm will be used to normalize TF-IDF vectors;
  • Linear algebra: singular value decomposition \(\mathbf{X} \approx \mathbf{U}_k \Sigma_k \mathbf{V}_k^\top\) — the engine of LSA (ch. 4) and the direct cousin of PCA (Machine Learning module);
  • Conditional probabilities and Bayes: \(\mathbb{P}(c \mid d) \propto \mathbb{P}(d \mid c)\,\mathbb{P}(c)\) — the Naive Bayes classifier (ch. 5) follows directly from it; the Dirichlet distribution \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\), conjugate of the multinomial — the foundation of LDA (ch. 4);
  • Markov chains and dynamic programming (Refresher CS): forward/backward and Viterbi (ch. 6) are Bellman recursions on a chain; the TextRank (ch. 8) is a Markov chain raised to a power;
  • Likelihood, MLE and EM: word2vec (ch. 3), LDA (ch. 4), Naive Bayes (ch. 5) and the Unigram tokenizer (ch. 1) are all likelihood maximizations — by SGD, Gibbs, direct counting or EM;
  • Entropy and perplexity: for a language model, \(\mathrm{PPL} = \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{t} \log p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\big)\) — the universal quality measure of a generative model of text (M2 LLMs, ch. 3).
How to read this course Each chapter follows the same triptych: (i) a representation of text (counts, weights, vectors, distributions, latent topics), (ii) a learning algorithm with its statistical justification, (iii) a quantified evaluation. The thm/prop boxes contain the proven results; the proofs come at the end of each box. References of the form "module X, chapter N" point to the other courses of the master's program; the natural extension of this module is the M2 "Large Language Models" course, which picks up tokenization (ch. 1), BM25 (ch. 2), sentence embeddings (ch. 7) and transformers where we leave them.

1. The text processing pipeline

Definition 1.1 — Standard pipeline Raw text → cleaning (HTML, case, accents, Unicode) → sentence segmentation → tokenization (splitting into units: words, subwords, characters) → lexical normalization (stemming raw root / lemmatization canonical form; stopwords) → representation (ch. 2-4) → model (ch. 5-7) → evaluation. Each stage destroys information; the engineer's craft is deciding which information is expendable for the task at hand.

1.1. Unicode normalization and "clean" text

Definition 1.2 — Unicode normal forms The same glyph admits several encodings: "é" = one codepoint U+00E9 in NFC (canonical composition), but two (e + combining accent U+0301) in NFD. NFKC goes further: ligature "ﬁ" → "fi", digit "²" → "2", full-width letters → ASCII. Two strings that are visually identical but distinct in bytes break counting, deduplication and search; so we normalize before anything else (in practice NFKC, or NFC if typography and units must be preserved).
  • Case: lower() merges "Polytechnique" and "polytechnique" but also the acronym and the common word; in French, keep the accents in lowercase ("élève" ≠ "eleve");
  • Sentence segmentation: rules (punctuation + capital letter) or learning (Punkt, Kiss & Strunk 2006); real pitfalls: "M.", "etc.", German abbreviations ("z. B."), period before a closing quote. Bad segmentation wrecks ch. 8 (extractive summarization: the sentence is the unit being selected);
  • Stemming vs lemmatization: the stemmer (Porter, Snowball) cuts off suffixes by rules — fast, crude, suited to information retrieval; the lemmatizer uses a dictionary and morphology ("sont" → "être") — slower, better for fine-grained analysis. In industry: stemmer for search, lemmatizer for analysis.

1.2. Subword tokenization: BPE, WordPiece, Unigram, SentencePiece

Why subwords Splitting on spaces works poorly: German compounds ("Donaudampfschifffahrt"), French elisions ("l'arbre": 1 or 2 tokens?), some Asian languages write without spaces, and any closed vocabulary runs into unknown words (typos, neologisms, product references). Subword tokenizers learn, on the corpus, a fixed-size vocabulary (~32k-128k) whose concatenation reconstructs any string: frequent words = 1 token, rare words = a sequence of pieces.
Algorithm 1.3 — BPE (Byte Pair Encoding, Sennrich et al. 2016) Initial vocabulary: characters (or bytes). Iteratively: (1) count all pairs of adjacent symbols in the corpus (weighted by word frequencies); (2) merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; (3) repeat until reaching \(|V|\). The result is an ordered set of merge rules; tokenizing a new text = applying the merges in the learned order.
Example 1.4 — Step-by-step BPE training Mini-corpus (words, frequencies): base×5, bases×4, basse×3, basique×2. Initial alphabet: b, a, s, e, i, q, u. We write each word as a sequence of symbols and count adjacent pairs:

1(b,a)=14, (a,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, (s,i)=(i,q)=(q,u)=(u,e)=2ba (14; tie with (a,s), arbitrary choice)
2(ba,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, …bas (14)
3(bas,e)=9, (s,e)=7, (bas,s)=3, (e,s)=3, …base (9)

In the end, base, bases, basse and basique are whole tokens; an unexpected word like "basiste" breaks down cleanly into bas+iste-or-characters: never an unknown, at worst an overhead in length. This is exactly the mechanism, pushed to bytes and 128k tokens, that equips GPT and Llama — developed in M2 LLMs, ch. 1.
Variants 1.5 — WordPiece (BERT), Unigram (T5), SentencePiece
  • WordPiece: the same merge loop, but a pair's score is \(\frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)\,f(y)}\) — we merge the pair whose relative frequency increases the most, a proxy for the likelihood gain of a unigram model (justification: merging \(x,y\) replaces two symbols with one; the gain in bits is all the greater as the collocation is over-represented relative to independence);
  • Unigram (Kudo, 2018): a generative model — a candidate vocabulary (characters + frequent substrings), each segment \(s\) has a probability \(p(s)\), a word is segmented by Viterbi by maximizing \(\sum_j \log p(s_j)\). Training by EM: E — for each word, the posterior distribution over its segmentations (∝ product of the \(p(s_j)\), computable by a forward dynamic program); M — \(p(s)\) = normalized expected counts; then pruning of the segments that degrade the likelihood the least, until \(|V|\). Unlike BPE, the segmentation is probabilistic and admits several analyses (subword regularization);
  • SentencePiece: a language-independent layer that treats the sentence as a sequence of bytes, the space becoming a "▁" symbol; no pre-tokenization on spaces — essential for languages without spaces and for multilingual settings (it is the foundation of Llama and T5).
import re
from collections import Counter

# --- Toy BPE: learning merges on the mini-corpus of example 1.4 ---
words = {"b a s e": 5, "b a s e s": 4, "b a s s e": 3, "b a s i q u e": 2}
merges = []
for _ in range(8):
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, f in words.items():
        sym = w.split()
        for pair in zip(sym, sym[1:]):
            pairs[pair] += f
    if not pairs:
        break
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)          # most frequent adjacent pair
    merges.append(best)
    big = " ".join(best)
    words = {re.sub(r"(?<!\S)" + re.escape(big) + r"(?!\S)", "".join(best), w): f
             for w, f in words.items()}
print("fusions :", merges[:6])
# tokenize a new word = replay the merges in the learned order
def encode(word, merges):
    sym = list(word)
    for a, b in merges:
        i, out = 0, []
        while i < len(sym):
            if i + 1 < len(sym) and (sym[i], sym[i+1]) == (a, b):
                out.append(a + b); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        sym = out
    return sym
print(encode("basiste", merges))

1.3. Stopwords, n-grams, collocations

  • Stopwords: to be removed for classical text mining (they saturate the BoW); to be kept for transformers — negation ("ne…pas") and syntax matter. The decision must be justified by the task, not by habit;
  • N-grams: sequences of \(n\) tokens; "New York" (bigram) ≠ "New" + "York"; in classification, moving from unigrams to bigrams captures compositional polarity ("pas cher"); cost: the vocabulary explodes (potentially \(|V|^n\)) — hence ngram_range=(1,2) and min_df in the code of ch. 5;
  • Collocations: bigrams whose frequency exceeds what independence predicts, measured by mutual information \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}\) — a criterion reused as-is in ch. 2 to justify IDF, and in ch. 4 for topic coherence.

2. Bag of words, TF-IDF and BM25

Definition 2.1 — Bag of words (BoW) A document = a vector of counts over the vocabulary \(V\) (order lost): \(d \in \mathbb{N}^{|V|}\). Documents × terms matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{N}^{n \times |V|}\) — extremely sparse (a few hundred non-zeros per document across tens of thousands of columns; sparse representation, cf. Refresher CS).
Definition 2.2 — TF-IDF $$\mathrm{tfidf}(t, d) = \mathrm{tf}(t, d) \times \underbrace{\log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)}}_{\mathrm{idf}(t)},$$ \(\mathrm{tf}\) = frequency of the term in the document, \(\mathrm{df}\) = number of documents containing the term, \(n\) = number of documents. It penalizes omnipresent terms and rewards discriminative ones. L2 weighting: we post-normalize \(\tilde d = d / \|d\|_2\), so that the dot product is directly the cosine similarity — this is what TfidfVectorizer does by default.
Proposition 2.3 — IDF = mutual information Let us choose a document \(D\) uniformly among the \(n\), and let \(T\) denote the event "\(D\) contains the term \(t\)". Then for any document \(d\) containing \(t\): $$\mathrm{PMI}(T\,;D=d) \;=\; \log\frac{\mathbb{P}(T,\,D=d)}{\mathbb{P}(T)\,\mathbb{P}(D=d)} \;=\; \log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)} \;=\; \mathrm{idf}(t).$$ In other words: IDF is exactly the mutual information between "the event \(t\) occurs" and the choice of this particular document. A term present everywhere identifies no document (zero PMI); a rare term strongly identifies the documents that contain it.
\(\mathbb{P}(D=d)=1/n\) and \(\mathbb{P}(T)=\mathrm{df}(t)/n\), so \(\mathbb{P}(T,D=d)=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]\). Substituting: \(\mathrm{PMI} = \log\frac{\mathbf{1}[t\in d]/n}{(\mathrm{df}/n)(1/n)} = \log\frac{n\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]}{\mathrm{df}}\), which equals \(\mathrm{idf}(t)\) if \(t \in d\) (and \(-\infty\) otherwise). ∎
Common variants (all useful in practice)
  • Sublinear TF: \(1+\log \mathrm{tf}\) — "3 occurrences do not signal the topic 3× more than a single one";
  • Boolean TF: \(\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{tf}>0]\) — robust to highly repetitive documents (spam, bots);
  • Smoothed IDF: \(\log\frac{1+n}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1\) (the scikit-learn variant) — avoids blow-ups for \(\mathrm{df}=n\);
  • Composite schemes (SMART nomenclature): different weighting on the document side (e.g. L2 of log-tf × idf) and on the query side (e.g. plain cosine); the performance of a search engine is often tuned here, before any model.
Model 2.4 — BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza) and its probabilistic reading A direct heir of probabilistic information retrieval (the Robertson–Sparck Jones 2-Poisson framework): a document is a mixture of "informative / non-informative" for the query; the number of occurrences \(x\) of the term follows two Poisson distributions with intensities \(\mu_1 \gg \mu_0\) depending on the class; the log-likelihood ratio \(\;x\log\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_0}-(\mu_1-\mu_0)\;\) is increasing then saturating in \(x\). BM25 approximates its shape: $$\mathrm{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)\cdot \frac{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)+k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)},\qquad \mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)=\log\frac{n-\mathrm{df}(t)+0{,}5}{\mathrm{df}(t)+0{,}5}.$$
Two limits to know by heart. Saturation: as \(\mathrm{tf}\to\infty\), the factor tends to \(k_1+1\) (bounded); at \(\mathrm{tf}=0\) it equals \(0\); it reaches half of its maximum when \(\mathrm{tf}=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl})\) — \(k_1\) tunes the saturation speed, \(b\) the length penalty (\(b=0\): no normalization; \(b=1\): proportional). Hence the flaws of raw TF-IDF: unbounded linear growth on the tf side, and no handling of document length. ∎

Proposition 2.5 — Hashing trick for giant vocabularies For huge or a priori unbounded \(|V|\) (tweet streams, logs): hash each term \(h(t) \in \{1,\dots,D\}\) with a random sign \(s(t)\in\{\pm1\}\), and set \(\tilde x_i = \sum_{t:\,h(t)=i} s(t)\,x_t\). Then \(\mathbb{E}\,\langle \tilde x,\tilde y\rangle = \langle x,y\rangle\) — collisions cancel out in expectation.
\(\tilde x^\top\tilde y = \sum_{t,t'} s(t)s(t')\,x_t y_{t'}\,\mathbf{1}[h(t)=h(t')]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[s(t)s(t')]=\mathbf{1}[t=t']\) (independent signs); what remains is \(\sum_t x_t y_t\). The variance of the collisions decreases like \(1/\sqrt{D}\): with \(D\sim 2^{20}\), the approximation is excellent for classification. ∎
Geometry of documents Each document becomes a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\); we measure similarities (cosine), classify (Naive Bayes, SVM), cluster (k-means). The limitation of BoW: bag = no order and no semantics; "the cat eats the mouse" ≈ "the mouse eats the cat". Embeddings (ch. 3) and transformers (ch. 7) answer this. BM25 remains the baseline of any retrieval system — including RAG (M2 LLMs, ch. 7, where it serves as a reference point for dense retrievers).

3. Word embeddings

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013): distributional hypothesis "A word is characterized by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957). Skip-gram: for each position \(t\), maximize the log-likelihood of the context words \(c\) within a window: $$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t}\sum_{c\in \mathrm{window}(t)} \log p(w_c\mid w_t), \qquad p(w_c\mid w_t)=\frac{\exp(z_c^\top z_t)}{\sum_{v\in V}\exp(z_v^\top z_t)}$$ — a softmax over \(|V|\) words at each position: \(\mathcal{O}(|V|)\) per example, prohibitive. CBOW: symmetric (predict the word from the average of its context).
Theorem 3.1 — Negative sampling: the derived loss We draw \(k\) "negatives" \(w_1,\dots,w_k \sim p_n(w)\propto \mathrm{freq}(w)^{3/4}\) (the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4) and replace the softmax with a series of binary classifications "is this (target, word) pair a true context pair?": $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\log\sigma(z_c^\top z_t) - \sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{w_i\sim p_n}\big[\log\sigma(-z_{w_i}^\top z_t)\big],$$ at a cost of \(\mathcal{O}(k)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(|V|)\). The gradient in \(z_t\): $$\nabla_{z_t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\big(1-\sigma(z_c^\top z_t)\big)z_c \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(z_{w_i}^\top z_t)\,z_{w_i}.$$ Reading: the positive \(c\) is attracted with a force that vanishes once it is already well predicted (\(1-\sigma\)); each negative is repelled proportionally to its current score \(\sigma\) — a negative that is "too well classified" (often a semantically close word!) receives the maximal correction. Theoretical interpretation: negative sampling is a form of NCE (noise contrastive estimation, Gutmann & Hyvärinen 2010; cf. Goldberg & Levy 2014).
Two settings that matter (and why)
  • Frequency subsampling: very frequent words ("the", "of") drown the signal; they are discarded with probability \(p_{\mathrm{discard}}(w)=1-\sqrt{t/f(w)}\) (threshold \(t\sim10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-3}\)) — the signal per pair stays intact and the computation is sped up;
  • The 3/4 exponent: flattening the frequencies boosts rare negatives, which are the most informative ones (frequent negatives are trivial to repel). Same trick as for the \(\mathrm{freq}^{3/4}\) word weighting: an empirical compromise between signal and coverage.
GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014): least squares on cooccurrences Let \(X_{ij}\) be the number of cooccurrences (words \(i,j\)) in the corpus. GloVe factorizes \(\log X_{ij}\) with a weighted bilinear model: $$J=\sum_{i,j} f\!\left(X_{ij}\right)\big(z_i^\top \tilde z_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2,\qquad f(x)=\min\!\Big[\big(x/x_{\max}\big)^{3/4},\,1\Big],$$ where \(f\) cuts the weight of rare cooccurrences (noisy, possibly zero; one cannot take \(\log 0\)) and saturates for frequent ones. The biases \(b_i,\tilde b_j\) absorb the marginal frequencies. Deep connection: skip-gram with a weighted window is an implicit factorization of a shifted PMI matrix (Levy & Goldberg, 2014) — GloVe does it explicitly, and globally (the whole matrix at once) rather than locally (sampled windows).
fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017): subwords A word = the sum of the vectors of its character \(n\)-grams (3 to 6): \(z_w = \sum_{g\in G_w} z_g\). Consequences: an out-of-vocabulary word still receives a vector (the sum of its \(n\)-grams), and typos and rich morphology (German: compounds; French: conjugations) are absorbed. The same team shows that "fastText + bigrams" is a formidable classification baseline (cf. ch. 5).

3.1. Evaluating embeddings

  • Lexical similarity: rate word pairs (SimLex-999, WordSim-353), correlate with the cosine — metric: Spearman correlation;
  • Analogies: precision@1 on \(z_b - z_a + z_c \approx z_d\) excluding \(\{a,b,c\}\) from the search (the Mikolov protocol): "king − man + woman ≈ queen". Caution: analogy accuracy is sensitive to word frequency;
  • Downstream: the real evaluation is the final task (ch. 5).
Proposition 3.2 — SIF sentence embeddings (Arora et al., TACL 2017) Sentence = weighted average with \(a(t)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(t)}\) (an anti-frequency weight; \(\alpha\sim10^{-3}\)), then removal of the first principal component of the corpus: $$\bar v(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{w\in S}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(w)}\,z_w,\qquad v(S) = \bar v(S) - u_1 u_1^\top \bar v(S).$$
Why remove \(u_1\): in the generative model of Arora et al., each sentence is emitted around a "discourse vector" \(c_S\), but a common vector \(c_0\) (syntax, function words) biases all the sentences of the corpus — that is exactly the direction of greatest variance, hence \(u_1\). The orthogonal projection removes this shared nuisance term; on Semantic Textual Similarity (STS), this single removal gains several correlation points and makes this baseline "simple but hard to beat" without a trained encoder. It is the direct ancestor of SBERT embeddings (ch. 7). ∎
Bias in embeddings — the WEAT test (Caliskan et al., 2017) Two sets of target words \(X,Y\) (e.g. professions) and two attributes \(A,B\) (e.g. male/female first names). Association score \(s(w)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\cos(z_w,z_a)-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}\cos(z_w,z_b)\); test statistic $$t = \frac{\mathrm{mean}_{x\in X}\,s(x)-\mathrm{mean}_{y\in Y}\,s(y)} {\mathrm{sd}_{w\in X\cup Y}\,s(w)},$$ significance by permutations of the targets. On GloVe/word2vec: scientific professions associate with the male attribute, artistic ones with the female — the corpus contains these stereotypes, and the geometry encodes them. Mitigation (Bolukbasi et al., 2016): estimate the bias direction (PCA of the "he/she" pairs…), neutralize neutral words (remove their projection onto this direction) and equalize the pairs — we will see the "representation → bias → correction" pattern again in M2 (LLM safety, ch. 9 of the LLMs module).
Text ↔ graphs, the other LLGA bridge word2vec on random walks yields DeepWalk/node2vec (Graph ML module, ch. 3); conversely, a Graph of Words (nodes = words, edges = cooccurrence within a window) enables text mining with graph tools — this is a research axis of the course's professor, detailed in ch. 9: the words ↔ graphs loop is a signature of the master's program.

4. Topic models

Model 4.1 — LDA (Blei, Ng & Jordan, 2003): the full generative model For each document \(d\) and each position \(i\) of the corpus: $$\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha),\quad z_{d,i}\mid\theta_d \sim \theta_d,\quad \varphi_k\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\beta),\quad w_{d,i}\mid z_{d,i},\varphi \sim \varphi_{z_{d,i}}.$$ A document is a mixture of \(K\) topics; each word is drawn from the topic of its position. The likelihood \(p(\mathbf{w})\) sums over all assignments \(z\) — intractable (\(K^{\#\mathrm{words}}\) terms): hence the variational Bayes inference (original paper) or Gibbs sampling (practical usage). Connected reminders: EM from the ML module (variational inference is its Bayesian version); Markov chains (L3 refresher — the sampler is a chain; geometric convergence under conditions).
Theorem 4.2 — Collapsed Gibbs sampling: the update equation By integrating out \(\theta,\varphi\) (Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy), the law of an assignment \(z_i\) given everything else depends only on counts: $$\mathbb{P}(z_i = k \mid z_{-i},\, w) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{k,w_i}^{-i}+\beta}{n_k^{-i}+|V|\beta}}_{\text{does topic \(k\) like word \(w_i\)?}}\;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{d,k}^{-i}+\alpha}{n_d^{-i}+K\alpha}}_{\text{does document \(d\) like topic \(k\)?}}$$ where \(n_{k,w}\) = number of times topic \(k\) has emitted \(w\), \(n_{d,k}\) = topic \(k\) in \(d\), exponent \(-i\) = "without the current position". Each \(z_i\) is sampled sequentially, over thousands of passes; the topics emerge by reinforcement (the rich-get-richer property of Dirichlets).
By conjugacy, the marginal of the corpus factorizes into multivariate beta functions: \(p(w,z\mid\alpha,\beta)=\prod_k \frac{B(n_{k,\cdot}+\beta)}{B(\beta)} \prod_d \frac{B(n_{d,\cdot}+\alpha)}{B(\alpha)}\). The conditional \(\mathbb{P}(z_i=k\mid\cdot)\) is the ratio \(p(w,z_i=k,z_{-i})/p(z_{-i},w_{-i})\): all the factors not touching position \(i\) cancel out (a property of beta ratios: \(\frac{B(u+e_k)}{B(u)}=\frac{u_k}{\sum_j u_j}\)), leaving the product of the two predictive factors above. ∎
Choosing the hyperparameters and K Small \(\alpha\) = pure documents (few topics per document); small \(\beta\) = pure topics (distinctive words). The asymmetric version (non-uniform \(\alpha_k\)) lets a "noise" topic emerge. \(K\) is chosen by validation (held-out perplexity) or by coherence — see below; the two criteria do not rank models in the same order, an important result of text mining.
Evaluation 4.3 — Perplexity vs coherence Perplexity (held-out): \(\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum \log p(w_{\mathrm{test}})\big)\) with \(p(w\mid d)=\sum_k \theta_{d,k}\varphi_{k,w}\) — but it correlates poorly with human judgment (Chang et al., 2009: models with lower perplexity may produce less interpretable topics). NPMI coherence: for the \(M\) most probable words of each topic, average $$\mathrm{NPMI}(u,v)=\frac{\log\dfrac{P(u,v)}{P(u)P(v)}}{-\log P(u,v)} \in [-1,1]$$ over all pairs (probabilities estimated from document cooccurrences): it measures the actual collocation of the topic's words — well correlated with humans. The engineer's rule: perplexity for predictive capacity, NPMI/\(C_v\) for interpretability.
Proposition 4.4 — LSA: truncated SVD as the PCA of text On the TF-IDF matrix \(\mathbf{X}\): \(\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\mathbf{V}_k^\top\); the columns of \(\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\) = the documents' coordinates in the latent space (dimensions = directions of maximal variance, uncorrelated); \(\mathbf{V}_k\) gives the topics (word distributions once renormalized). Key differences: LSA is linear, deterministic, fast; LDA is generative, models the counts, yields interpretable mixtures — but LSA captures weighted cooccurrence and is often sufficient for document similarity. Link with PCA: PCA = SVD of the centered-scaled matrix (ML module); LSA = SVD of the TF-IDF matrix (no centering: the "mean" of text makes no sense).
  • After LDA: BERTopic (embeddings + clustering + c-TF-IDF) for topics on contemporary corpora — consistent with the "embeddings, ch. 3" pipeline; for the industrial use of topics: monitoring, compliance, e-discovery, detection of emerging themes;
  • Word Sense Induction: applying clustering to all the occurrences of a single word (BERT contexts) reveals its senses — the joint Vazirgiannis–Buscaldi paper of ch. 9 does exactly that, with mutual information maximization;
  • Topic models on graphs (TopicSBM etc.): the idea extended to networks (see the Graph ML module).

5. Text classification

Theorem 5.1 — Multinomial Naive Bayes, derived With the (false!) assumption of word independence given the class, \(\mathbb{P}(d\mid c)=\prod_{t\in d}\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)^{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)}\), and Bayes: $$\hat c = \arg\max_c \Big[\log \mathbb{P}(c) + \sum_{t\in d}\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\log \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)\Big].$$ MLE of the parameters: \(\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}}{n_c}\) (counts), \(\mathbb{P}(c)=\frac{n_c}{n}\). Laplace smoothing: $$\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|V|}.$$
(i) The MLE comes from maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood under the constraint \(\sum_t \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=1\) (Lagrange multipliers — relative counts). (ii) Problem: a word seen at test time but never in class \(c\) gives \(\log 0 = -\infty\) — a single rarity wipes out the whole class; in French ("compte" etc.) this is systematic on small corpora. (iii) Laplace smoothing = the MAP under a uniform Dirichlet\((1,\dots,1)\) prior: the posterior density \(\propto \prod_t \pi_t^{n_{t,c}}\cdot\prod_t\pi_t^{1-1}\) is maximized at \(\frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|V|}\) — the exact Bayesian link of ch. 0. ∎
Why NB works despite an absurd assumption Conditional independence is massively violated ("New" predicts "York"). But the decision (argmax) is often correct even when the probabilities are badly calibrated: it is enough for the right candidate to win, not for its score to be right (the theory of Domingos & Pazzani). Hence NB's status: bad probabilities, good baseline — always the first one in a text project, because it trains in seconds and sets the performance floor.
Sparse logistic regression: why it works in high dimension On TF-IDF, \(\#\text{features} \gg \#\text{docs}\) (10⁵ features for 10³ documents): the risk of overfitting seems maximal, yet the regularized linear model (L2, or L1 to select) generalizes very well. Three reasons: (1) in high dimension the vectors are almost orthogonal — examples are naturally separable, and regularization suffices to pick a "sober" separator; (2) IDF weighting has already done a large share of the statistical work (prop. 2.3); (3) discriminative unigrams/bigrams are exactly the features a linear model weights well. State of the art of the "classical" era until 2018 (Joulin et al., fastText); still formidable below 1,000 labeled examples.
  • Calibration: when the probability is consumed (business thresholds, ticket routing), recalibrate — Platt (logistic regression on the scores) or isotonic; diagnostic: reliability curve (predicted vs observed reliability);
  • Imbalanced classes: class weighting (class_weight="balanced"), optimal threshold on the PR curve (not ROC); metric: macro-F1 (per-class average — every class counts equally) rather than accuracy (which flatters the majority); per-intent confusion matrix for chatbots;
  • Networks: sentence CNNs (Kim, 2014), BiLSTMs; then transformer fine-tuning (ch. 7). Empirical learning curves: linear+TF-IDF wins below ~10²-10³ examples, the transformer above;
  • Zero-shot / few-shot with an LLM: zero labeled examples, k examples in the prompt (M2 LLMs, ch. 4): an excellent exploratory starting point and assisted labeling (active learning), rarely the final solution in production (cost, latency, confidentiality) — the trade-off is detailed in M2.

6. Sequences: NER, HMM, CRF

Definition 6.1 — Sequence labeling Assign a label to each token: POS (grammatical category) or NER (named entities: PER, ORG, LOC…) in BIO ("Begin/Inside/Outside"): "Kim" = B-PER, "Noel" = I-PER. NER is the gateway to building knowledge graphs (entity + relation extraction).
HMM: forward, backward, Viterbi — the recurrences derived HMM: hidden states \(z_t\) (labels), emissions \(\mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z_t)\), transitions \(\mathbb{P}(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\). Notation: \(\alpha_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_1,\dots,w_t,\,z_t=z)\) (forward) and \(\beta_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_{t+1},\dots,w_T\mid z_t=z)\) (backward): $$\alpha_t(z)=\mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z)\sum_{z'}\alpha_{t-1}(z')\,\mathbb{P}(z\mid z'), \qquad \beta_t(z)=\sum_{z'}\mathbb{P}(z'\mid z)\,\mathbb{P}(w_{t+1}\mid z')\,\beta_{t+1}(z').$$
Forward: condition on \(z_{t-1}\). \(\alpha_t(z)=\sum_{z'}\mathbb{P}(w_{1:t},z_{t-1}=z',z_t=z) =\sum_{z'}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(w_{1:t-1},z_{t-1}=z')}_{\alpha_{t-1}(z')} \,\mathbb{P}(z\mid z')\,\mathbb{P}(w_t\mid z)\) — Markovian: the past depends only on \(z_t\). Backward: symmetric, conditioning on \(z_{t+1}\). Uses: likelihood \(\mathbb{P}(w_{1:T})=\sum_z \alpha_T(z)\); marginal posteriors \(\mathbb{P}(z_t\mid w)=\alpha_t(z)\beta_t(z)/\mathbb{P}(w)\) — this is the E-step of an EM to train the HMM (ML module). All in \(\mathcal{O}(T K^2)\). ∎
Algorithm 6.2 — Viterbi: optimality by substructure The most likely label path satisfies the recursion $$\delta_t(z) = \max_{z'} \big[\delta_{t-1}(z')\, \mathbb{P}(z \mid z')\big]\ \mathbb{P}(w_t \mid z),\qquad \delta_0(z)=\mathbb{P}(z)\mathbb{P}(w_1\mid z),$$ and the optimal labeling is read off by backtracking the argmaxes — \(\mathcal{O}(T K^2)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(K^T)\).
Optimality: any optimal path arriving at \((t,z)\) has an optimal prefix up to \((t-1,z')\) — otherwise replacing the prefix with a better one increases the total probability (exchange argument: the future factors depend only on \(z\), Markovian). So it suffices to follow the best predecessor: this is Bellman (RL module, ch. 2) on a Markov chain, with max instead of expectation. ∎
CRF (Lafferty et al., 2001): the discriminative approach An HMM models \(\mathbb{P}(w,z)\) jointly (generative); a CRF models \(\mathbb{P}(z\mid w)\) directly: $$\mathbb{P}(z\mid w)=\frac{\exp\big(\sum_{t}\theta^\top f(z_{t-1},z_t,w,t)\big)}{Z(w)}, \qquad \nabla_\theta \log\mathbb{P} = \mathbb{E}_{p(z\mid w)}[f]-f(z^\star)$$ — arbitrary features over the whole sequence: word identity, suffixes, capitalization, shape of the neighbor, hyphen, position…, plus transition features \(z_{t-1}\to z_t\) (which forbid O→I-PER: an entity start must be B). Inference: forward-backward for \(Z(w)\) and the feature expectation; Viterbi for prediction. The gain over HMM: no "waste" in modeling the words, rich features, arbitrary interactions — long the state of the art for NER (CoNLL-2003).
BIO decoding and entity-level NER evaluation Per-token predictions must be converted into spans: an entity = a B-X (I-X)* sequence. Entity evaluation (not token): an entity is correct iff both type and exact boundaries match; $$\mathrm{P}=\frac{\#\text{correct}}{\#\text{predicted}},\qquad \mathrm{R}=\frac{\#\text{correct}}{\#\text{gold}},\qquad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.$$ Token-level F1 (lenient about boundaries) always overestimates entity-level F1 — always report entity-level F1 (the conlleval standard). Industrial reflex: audit boundary errors ("Banque de France" truncated) separately from type errors (PER vs ORG).
Today Transformers label token by token (per-token softmax + BIO decoding; the head is often a linear-chain CRF or a simple softmax with BIO constraints in post-processing); the spaCy and transformers libraries (the ner pipeline) deliver the state of the art in a few lines; the lab builds an entity-relation extractor that feeds the mini-GraphRAG of the Graph ML module — ch. 8 of this course for the full pipeline.

7. Transformers for NLP

  • Architecture: carried over from the Deep Learning module ch. 6 (multi-head attention \(\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V\), residuals, LayerNorm, encoder/decoder); here we apply it to text — the M2 LLMs course (ch. 2) will revisit the architecture in depth (RoPE, GQA, MoE…);
BERT (Devlin et al., 2019): MLM + NSP in detail Bidirectional encoder, pretrained on two tasks: (1) Masked LM: 15 % of tokens drawn; among them 80 % replaced by [MASK], 10 % by a random word, 10 % unchanged; the original token is predicted (output: per-position classification). The mixing avoids a pretraining/fine-tuning mismatch (the model will never see [MASK] downstream again) and forces a robust representation. (2) Next Sentence Prediction: classify whether two sentences follow each other (the pair [CLS] A [SEP] B) — a weak task, abandoned by RoBERTa in favor of more data. Result: [CLS] = sentence representation; one model, dozens of tasks via a simple head layer.
GPT (Radford et al.) and T5 (Raffel et al., 2019) GPT: causal decoder — \(\mathcal{L}=-\sum_t\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\), pretraining = predicting the next token; generative by construction, zero task-specific architecture (this is the family extended in M2). T5: everything in text-to-text — span corruption (masking continuous fragments and regenerating them), every task (classification, translation, QA…) formulated as textual input→output; it unifies MLM and CLM.
Protocol 7.1 — Full fine-tuning (the stable recipe)
  1. Head: a linear layer \(\mathrm{Linear}(d, K)\) on [CLS] (classification) or per position (NER, POS);
  2. Optimizer: AdamW, learning rate \(\{2\times10^{-5}, 3\times10^{-5}, 5\times10^{-5}\}\) — a short grid, fine-tuning is sensitive but flat around the optimum; linear warmup for 10 % then linear decay;
  3. Epochs: 2 to 4 (beyond that: brutal overfitting on small corpora — watch the dev set); batch 16-32;
  4. Small corpora (<1k): freeze the lower layers, layer-wise decay (layer-wise lr decay), or adapters/LoRA (added parameters << 1 % — revisited in M2, ch. 6);
  5. Cost: distillation (DistilBERT ≈ −40 % params, −97 % of training time, ~97 % of the performance), int8 quantization — the performance/cost trade-off is an engineering criterion, deepened in M2 (LLMs ch. 6, ML Ops).
Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019): the triplet loss derived To encode sentences in a comparable space (search, clustering, RAG): siamese BERT + pooling (mean), trained with a triplet loss on (anchor \(a\), positive \(p\), negative \(n\)): $$\mathcal{L}=\max\big(0,\ d(a,p)-d(a,n)+\varepsilon\big).$$
If the margin is respected (\(d(a,p)+\varepsilon\le d(a,n)\)), the loss is zero: nothing to learn. Otherwise, the gradient \(\nabla = \nabla d(a,p)-\nabla d(a,n)\) pulls the anchor toward the positive and pushes it away from the negative, with a force proportional to the violation. Crucial point: the quality depends on the negatives (mini-batch: the other pairs provide hard negatives — this is what shapes the space). This is the kind of embeddings that indexes the corpora of RAG systems — detailed in M2 LLMs ch. 7 (dense retrieval, reranking). ∎
French: CamemBERT, FlauBERT, JuriBERT CamemBERT (Martin et al., 2020): RoBERTa retrained on 138 GB of French text — the reference French model; FlauBERT: an academic French BERT; JuriBERT (Douka et al., 2021 — research from this course, ch. 9): an adaptation to legal French, where the vocabulary (doctrine, grievances, appeal) confuses general models. Multilingual: mBERT, XLM-R (100+ languages, shared embeddings — at the price of capacity diluted per language).

8. Industrial text mining

This chapter assembles the building blocks of ch. 1-7 into products: summaries, keywords, deduplication, ticket classification — the real workloads of NLP in industry, together with their engineering pitfalls.

8.1. TextRank: extractive summarization by PageRank

TextRank (Mihalcea & Tarau, 2004) (1) Sentence → node; similarity between sentences \(S_i,S_j\) (overlap of filtered words): \(w_{ij}=\frac{|\{t\in S_i\}\cap\{t\in S_j\}|}{\log|S_i|+\log|S_j|}\); (2) symmetric weights → random walk; (3) score: $$\mathrm{TR}(u)=\frac{1-d}{N}+d\sum_{v\to u}\frac{w_{vu}}{\sum_{v'}w_{vv'}}\,\mathrm{TR}(v),$$ iterated from the uniform distribution (matrix power); geometric convergence to the unique stationary distribution (Perron–Frobenius: primitive stochastic matrix — a Markov chain reminder, ch. 0); (4) the k top-scoring sentences = the summary (reordered chronologically). The same algorithm on the graph of words (cooccurrence window) yields keyword extraction — "PageRank for text".
The recursion reads \(\pi^{(k+1)}=\big[(1-d)\tfrac{\mathbb{1}}{N} +d\,M\big]\pi^{(k)}\) with \(M\) the weighted transition matrix: the operator is contracting (factor \(d<1\)), hence geometric convergence at rate \(d\) — typically settling in ~20-30 iterations for \(d=0.85\). ∎

8.2. Keyword extraction: RAKE, YAKE, KeyBERT

  • RAKE (Rose et al., 2010): split on stopwords/punctuation → candidate phrases; a word's score = \(\deg(w)/\mathrm{freq}(w)\) (degree in the cooccurrence graph within the candidates) + bigram frequency; a phrase's score = the sum of its words. Fast, no external corpus, sensitive to stopwords;
  • YAKE (Campos et al., 2020): unsupervised statistical features per word — frequency, dispersion of positions (a keyword appears everywhere), case (a capital letter mid-sentence = signal), redundancy of variants (edit distance) — an aggregated score, with left/right windows for context; robust and multilingual;
  • KeyBERT (Grootendorst, 2020): extract candidates (n-grams), embed them (ch. 3), keep the ones closest to the document — and diversify with MMR: $$\mathrm{MMR}=\arg\max_{c\,\in\,C\setminus S}\ \Big[\lambda\cos(c,d)-(1-\lambda)\max_{s\in S}\cos(c,s)\Big],$$ which adds to the set \(S\) the candidate that is relevant and not very redundant; the same "embeddings + MMR" pattern returns in RAG (M2, ch. 7).
Extractive vs abstractive Extractive (TextRank; BERT: ranking sentences): attributable, hallucination-free, but redundant and choppy. Abstractive (generating): fluent, but requires a generative model and a faithfulness evaluation — this is the territory of M2 (LLMs ch. 7-8) and of the M2 instructor's research (meeting summarization, cf. ch. 9). In industry: extractive remains the trusted solution when every sentence must be traceable to its source.

8.3. Deduplication at scale: MinHash + LSH

Proposition 8.1 — MinHash estimates the Jaccard similarity For uniform \(h\), \(\;\mathbb{P}\big(\min_{x\in A} h(x)=\min_{y\in B} h(y)\big) = J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\). Stacking \(m\) hashings → a signature \(\mathrm{sig}(A)\in\{0,\dots,2^{64}\}^m\), and the collision frequency estimates \(J\).
Let \(z\) be the element of \(A\cup B\) minimizing \(h\) (a.s. unique for uniform \(h\)). \(\min h(A)=\min h(B)\) iff \(z\in A\cap B\). Now \(z\) is uniform over \(A\cup B\) (permutation invariance of hashings), hence the probability \(\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\). ∎
LSH (Locality-Sensitive Hashing): bands and rows The signature is split into \(b\) bands of \(r\) rows; two documents are candidates if an entire band coincides. Detection probability: $$\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{detect}}(J)=1-\big(1-J^{\,r}\big)^{b}$$ — a steep sigmoid: with \(r=5,\ b=20\): \(J=0.5\to 47\,\%\), \(J=0.8\to 99.96\,\%\). The accepted/rejected boundary is "chosen" through \((r,b)\) — this is the exact deduplication mechanism used on LLM pretraining corpora (M2 LLMs, ch. 3, where it is detailed with scale figures; lab implementation: datasketch).

9.1. Michalis Vazirgiannis: text mining through graphs

  • Graph-of-Word and TW-IDF — F. Rousseau & M. Vazirgiannis, Graph-of-word and TW-IDF: new approach to ad hoc IR, CIKM 2013 (doi); overview: Graph of Words: Boosting Text Mining Tasks with Graphs, WWW 2017 (doi). The idea: represent each document as a graph of words (nodes = terms, edges = cooccurrence within a window), and replace the tf(t,d) of TF-IDF (ch. 2) with the centrality of the term — the sum of the weights of its edges (weighted degree). The weight captures the local cohesion of the term in the discourse, not just its frequency; TW-IDF improves ad hoc retrieval on reference collections. Position: directly extends ch. 2 — "counting becomes a graph score"; it is also the gateway to the Graph ML module (motifs, communities, k-cores).

Exercises

  1. TF-IDF on paper. On 3 given documents, compute by hand the TF-IDF matrix (natural log) of the reduced vocabulary, then the cosine similarity of each pair; verify with TfidfVectorizer (watch out for L2 normalization).
    Solution (method): (1) count tf(t,d); (2) df(t) over the 3 documents, then \(\mathrm{idf}=\log(3/\mathrm{df})\) (0 for a term present everywhere — it disappears); (3) multiply; (4) normalize each row \(\tilde d=d/\|d\|_2\), so that \(\cos(d_i,d_j)=\tilde d_i^\top \tilde d_j\) — this is what sklearn returns; (5) classic pitfall: sklearn uses the smoothed IDF \(\log\frac{1+n}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1\); pass smooth_idf=False to recover the formula from the course. ∎
  2. IDF = PMI. Rederive proposition 2.3 in the cases \(\mathrm{df}(t)=n\) (term everywhere) and \(\mathrm{df}(t)=1\) (unique term); deduce why L2 weighting alone is not enough to squash omnipresent words.
    Solution: \(\mathrm{df}=n \Rightarrow \mathrm{idf}=\log 1=0\) — the term discriminates no document (zero PMI); \(\mathrm{df}=1 \Rightarrow \mathrm{idf}=\log n\) — the term perfectly identifies its document. The L2 norm rescales documents, not columns: a frequent word keeps a high tf in all documents — only IDF (a per-column weight) squashes it. ∎
  3. BPE. Redo example 1.4 with the corpus stage×3, stages×2, stand×4 and list the 5 first merges; what happens if we add stand×40?
    Solution (start): initial pairs (s,t)=9, (t,a)=9, (a,g)=5, (a,n)=4… — the initial (s,t)/(t,a) tie is broken arbitrarily; with stand×40, the pair (a,n) reaches 44 and stand becomes a token before the other roots — this is BPE's lesson: the vocabulary encodes the corpus frequencies, not linguistics. ∎
  4. BM25. Show that the tf factor of BM25 is increasing, bounded by \(k_1+1\), and reaches half its value at \(\mathrm{tf}=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl})\); comment on the effect of \(b=0.9\) on a corpus of documents with widely varying lengths.
    Solution: set \(g(x)=\frac{x(k_1+1)}{x+c}\) with \(c=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl})>0\) — derivative \(g'(x)=\frac{c(k_1+1)}{(x+c)^2}>0\), \(g\to k_1+1\), and \(g(x)=\frac{k_1+1}{2} \Leftrightarrow x=c\). High \(b\): long documents see their \(c\) increase → slower saturation and a stronger penalty — suited when length signals richness; dangerous on heterogeneous mixtures of short/long documents. ∎
  5. Naive Bayes. Derive the MLE estimator of \(\mathbb{P}(t \mid c)\), show the problem of zero probabilities and justify Laplace smoothing as the MAP under Dirichlet(1,…,1) (theorem 5.1); implement and compare against MultinomialNB on 20 Newsgroups; test the effect of α on calibration.
    Solution (core): Lagrangians → relative counts; a test word absent from the class → \(\log 0\): the class is eliminated; the uniform prior adds a "pseudo-count" of 1 to every term → the smoothed formula; α>1 squashes differences (less overconfident), α<1 sharpens them (more discriminative, less calibrated). ∎
  6. Skip-gram. Write the negative sampling loss \(\log \sigma(z_c^\top z_t) + \sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{w_k \sim p_n}\log \sigma(-z_{w_k}^\top z_t)\); compute the gradient in \(z_t\) and interpret it (who is attracted, who is repelled, with what force); implement on a small corpus and check that words with similar contexts have close embeddings.
    Solution: gradient = \(-\,(1-\sigma_c)\,z_c+\sum_k\sigma_k\,z_{w_k}\) (cf. thm 3.1); the positive is attracted with weight \((1-\sigma)\), the negatives repelled with weight \(\sigma\); check: cosine neighborhood of "graphe" ≈ {réseau, nœud, arête}. ∎
  7. Analogies and bias. With pretrained embeddings (GloVe fr), test 10 analogies and measure the success rate; compute the WEAT statistic on two sets of professions × two lists of first names; implement neutralization (projection removal) and measure the effect on both scores.
  8. LDA. Write the joint law of model 4.1, integrate out \(\theta_d\) and \(\varphi_k\) and recover the Gibbs equation of theorem 4.2 in the case \(K=2\), \(\alpha=\beta=1\) (the multivariate betas reduce to generalized factorials); verify numerically the "rich-get-richer" reinforcement on 200 words.
  9. Viterbi. Implement Viterbi for a toy POS tagger (2 labels, 5-word vocabulary); verify optimality against exhaustive enumeration; complexity \(\mathcal{O}(TK^2)\); modify the proof of thm 6.2 for the case of paths with zero weights.
  10. MinHash/LSH. For \(r=5, b=20\), plot \(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{detect}}(J)=1-(1-J^r)^b\) and locate the "threshold"; which pair \((r,b)\) at \(m=256\) gives a threshold close to \(J=0.7\)?
    Solution: the sigmoid goes from ~0.1 to ~0.9 between \(J\approx0.45\) and \(J\approx0.65\) — threshold \(\approx(1/b)^{1/r}\approx 0.55\); to target 0.7 with \(m=256\): try \(r=7\) (0.7⁷≈0.082) and \(b=256/7\approx36\); keep the rule of thumb: threshold \(\approx (1/b)^{1/r}\), quality increases with \(m\). ∎
