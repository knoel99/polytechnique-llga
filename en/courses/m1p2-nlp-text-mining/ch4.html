4. Topic models

Model 4.1 — LDA (Blei, Ng & Jordan, 2003): the full generative model For each document \(d\) and each position \(i\) of the corpus: $$\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha),\quad z_{d,i}\mid\theta_d \sim \theta_d,\quad \varphi_k\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\beta),\quad w_{d,i}\mid z_{d,i},\varphi \sim \varphi_{z_{d,i}}.$$ A document is a mixture of \(K\) topics; each word is drawn from the topic of its position. The likelihood \(p(\mathbf{w})\) sums over all assignments \(z\) — intractable (\(K^{\#\mathrm{words}}\) terms): hence the variational Bayes inference (original paper) or Gibbs sampling (practical usage). Connected reminders: EM from the ML module (variational inference is its Bayesian version); Markov chains (L3 refresher — the sampler is a chain; geometric convergence under conditions).
Theorem 4.2 — Collapsed Gibbs sampling: the update equation By integrating out \(\theta,\varphi\) (Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy), the law of an assignment \(z_i\) given everything else depends only on counts: $$\mathbb{P}(z_i = k \mid z_{-i},\, w) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{k,w_i}^{-i}+\beta}{n_k^{-i}+|V|\beta}}_{\text{does topic \(k\) like word \(w_i\)?}}\;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{d,k}^{-i}+\alpha}{n_d^{-i}+K\alpha}}_{\text{does document \(d\) like topic \(k\)?}}$$ where \(n_{k,w}\) = number of times topic \(k\) has emitted \(w\), \(n_{d,k}\) = topic \(k\) in \(d\), exponent \(-i\) = "without the current position". Each \(z_i\) is sampled sequentially, over thousands of passes; the topics emerge by reinforcement (the rich-get-richer property of Dirichlets).
By conjugacy, the marginal of the corpus factorizes into multivariate beta functions: \(p(w,z\mid\alpha,\beta)=\prod_k \frac{B(n_{k,\cdot}+\beta)}{B(\beta)} \prod_d \frac{B(n_{d,\cdot}+\alpha)}{B(\alpha)}\). The conditional \(\mathbb{P}(z_i=k\mid\cdot)\) is the ratio \(p(w,z_i=k,z_{-i})/p(z_{-i},w_{-i})\): all the factors not touching position \(i\) cancel out (a property of beta ratios: \(\frac{B(u+e_k)}{B(u)}=\frac{u_k}{\sum_j u_j}\)), leaving the product of the two predictive factors above. ∎
Choosing the hyperparameters and K Small \(\alpha\) = pure documents (few topics per document); small \(\beta\) = pure topics (distinctive words). The asymmetric version (non-uniform \(\alpha_k\)) lets a "noise" topic emerge. \(K\) is chosen by validation (held-out perplexity) or by coherence — see below; the two criteria do not rank models in the same order, an important result of text mining.
Evaluation 4.3 — Perplexity vs coherence Perplexity (held-out): \(\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum \log p(w_{\mathrm{test}})\big)\) with \(p(w\mid d)=\sum_k \theta_{d,k}\varphi_{k,w}\) — but it correlates poorly with human judgment (Chang et al., 2009: models with lower perplexity may produce less interpretable topics). NPMI coherence: for the \(M\) most probable words of each topic, average $$\mathrm{NPMI}(u,v)=\frac{\log\dfrac{P(u,v)}{P(u)P(v)}}{-\log P(u,v)} \in [-1,1]$$ over all pairs (probabilities estimated from document cooccurrences): it measures the actual collocation of the topic's words — well correlated with humans. The engineer's rule: perplexity for predictive capacity, NPMI/\(C_v\) for interpretability.
Proposition 4.4 — LSA: truncated SVD as the PCA of text On the TF-IDF matrix \(\mathbf{X}\): \(\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\mathbf{V}_k^\top\); the columns of \(\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\) = the documents' coordinates in the latent space (dimensions = directions of maximal variance, uncorrelated); \(\mathbf{V}_k\) gives the topics (word distributions once renormalized). Key differences: LSA is linear, deterministic, fast; LDA is generative, models the counts, yields interpretable mixtures — but LSA captures weighted cooccurrence and is often sufficient for document similarity. Link with PCA: PCA = SVD of the centered-scaled matrix (ML module); LSA = SVD of the TF-IDF matrix (no centering: the "mean" of text makes no sense).
  • After LDA: BERTopic (embeddings + clustering + c-TF-IDF) for topics on contemporary corpora — consistent with the "embeddings, ch. 3" pipeline; for the industrial use of topics: monitoring, compliance, e-discovery, detection of emerging themes;
  • Word Sense Induction: applying clustering to all the occurrences of a single word (BERT contexts) reveals its senses — the joint Vazirgiannis–Buscaldi paper of ch. 9 does exactly that, with mutual information maximization;
  • Topic models on graphs (TopicSBM etc.): the idea extended to networks (see the Graph ML module).
