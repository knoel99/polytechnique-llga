3. Word embeddings

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013): distributional hypothesis "A word is characterized by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957). Skip-gram: for each position \(t\), maximize the log-likelihood of the context words \(c\) within a window: $$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t}\sum_{c\in \mathrm{window}(t)} \log p(w_c\mid w_t), \qquad p(w_c\mid w_t)=\frac{\exp(z_c^\top z_t)}{\sum_{v\in V}\exp(z_v^\top z_t)}$$ — a softmax over \(|V|\) words at each position: \(\mathcal{O}(|V|)\) per example, prohibitive. CBOW: symmetric (predict the word from the average of its context).
Theorem 3.1 — Negative sampling: the derived loss We draw \(k\) "negatives" \(w_1,\dots,w_k \sim p_n(w)\propto \mathrm{freq}(w)^{3/4}\) (the unigram distribution raised to the power 3/4) and replace the softmax with a series of binary classifications "is this (target, word) pair a true context pair?": $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\log\sigma(z_c^\top z_t) - \sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{w_i\sim p_n}\big[\log\sigma(-z_{w_i}^\top z_t)\big],$$ at a cost of \(\mathcal{O}(k)\) instead of \(\mathcal{O}(|V|)\). The gradient in \(z_t\): $$\nabla_{z_t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{neg}} = -\big(1-\sigma(z_c^\top z_t)\big)z_c \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma(z_{w_i}^\top z_t)\,z_{w_i}.$$ Reading: the positive \(c\) is attracted with a force that vanishes once it is already well predicted (\(1-\sigma\)); each negative is repelled proportionally to its current score \(\sigma\) — a negative that is "too well classified" (often a semantically close word!) receives the maximal correction. Theoretical interpretation: negative sampling is a form of NCE (noise contrastive estimation, Gutmann & Hyvärinen 2010; cf. Goldberg & Levy 2014).
Two settings that matter (and why)
  • Frequency subsampling: very frequent words ("the", "of") drown the signal; they are discarded with probability \(p_{\mathrm{discard}}(w)=1-\sqrt{t/f(w)}\) (threshold \(t\sim10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-3}\)) — the signal per pair stays intact and the computation is sped up;
  • The 3/4 exponent: flattening the frequencies boosts rare negatives, which are the most informative ones (frequent negatives are trivial to repel). Same trick as for the \(\mathrm{freq}^{3/4}\) word weighting: an empirical compromise between signal and coverage.
GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014): least squares on cooccurrences Let \(X_{ij}\) be the number of cooccurrences (words \(i,j\)) in the corpus. GloVe factorizes \(\log X_{ij}\) with a weighted bilinear model: $$J=\sum_{i,j} f\!\left(X_{ij}\right)\big(z_i^\top \tilde z_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2,\qquad f(x)=\min\!\Big[\big(x/x_{\max}\big)^{3/4},\,1\Big],$$ where \(f\) cuts the weight of rare cooccurrences (noisy, possibly zero; one cannot take \(\log 0\)) and saturates for frequent ones. The biases \(b_i,\tilde b_j\) absorb the marginal frequencies. Deep connection: skip-gram with a weighted window is an implicit factorization of a shifted PMI matrix (Levy & Goldberg, 2014) — GloVe does it explicitly, and globally (the whole matrix at once) rather than locally (sampled windows).
fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017): subwords A word = the sum of the vectors of its character \(n\)-grams (3 to 6): \(z_w = \sum_{g\in G_w} z_g\). Consequences: an out-of-vocabulary word still receives a vector (the sum of its \(n\)-grams), and typos and rich morphology (German: compounds; French: conjugations) are absorbed. The same team shows that "fastText + bigrams" is a formidable classification baseline (cf. ch. 5).

3.1. Evaluating embeddings

  • Lexical similarity: rate word pairs (SimLex-999, WordSim-353), correlate with the cosine — metric: Spearman correlation;
  • Analogies: precision@1 on \(z_b - z_a + z_c \approx z_d\) excluding \(\{a,b,c\}\) from the search (the Mikolov protocol): "king − man + woman ≈ queen". Caution: analogy accuracy is sensitive to word frequency;
  • Downstream: the real evaluation is the final task (ch. 5).
Proposition 3.2 — SIF sentence embeddings (Arora et al., TACL 2017) Sentence = weighted average with \(a(t)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(t)}\) (an anti-frequency weight; \(\alpha\sim10^{-3}\)), then removal of the first principal component of the corpus: $$\bar v(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{w\in S}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+p(w)}\,z_w,\qquad v(S) = \bar v(S) - u_1 u_1^\top \bar v(S).$$
Why remove \(u_1\): in the generative model of Arora et al., each sentence is emitted around a "discourse vector" \(c_S\), but a common vector \(c_0\) (syntax, function words) biases all the sentences of the corpus — that is exactly the direction of greatest variance, hence \(u_1\). The orthogonal projection removes this shared nuisance term; on Semantic Textual Similarity (STS), this single removal gains several correlation points and makes this baseline "simple but hard to beat" without a trained encoder. It is the direct ancestor of SBERT embeddings (ch. 7). ∎
Bias in embeddings — the WEAT test (Caliskan et al., 2017) Two sets of target words \(X,Y\) (e.g. professions) and two attributes \(A,B\) (e.g. male/female first names). Association score \(s(w)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\cos(z_w,z_a)-\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}\cos(z_w,z_b)\); test statistic $$t = \frac{\mathrm{mean}_{x\in X}\,s(x)-\mathrm{mean}_{y\in Y}\,s(y)} {\mathrm{sd}_{w\in X\cup Y}\,s(w)},$$ significance by permutations of the targets. On GloVe/word2vec: scientific professions associate with the male attribute, artistic ones with the female — the corpus contains these stereotypes, and the geometry encodes them. Mitigation (Bolukbasi et al., 2016): estimate the bias direction (PCA of the "he/she" pairs…), neutralize neutral words (remove their projection onto this direction) and equalize the pairs — we will see the "representation → bias → correction" pattern again in M2 (LLM safety, ch. 9 of the LLMs module).
Text ↔ graphs, the other LLGA bridge word2vec on random walks yields DeepWalk/node2vec (Graph ML module, ch. 3); conversely, a Graph of Words (nodes = words, edges = cooccurrence within a window) enables text mining with graph tools — this is a research axis of the course's professor, detailed in ch. 9: the words ↔ graphs loop is a signature of the master's program. 
import gensim.downloader as api
wv = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-100")        # pretrained embeddings

def analogy(a, b, c):                            # a - b + c ≈ ?
    v = wv[a] - wv[b] + wv[c]
    return wv.most_similar(v, topn=3)
print(analogy("king", "man", "woman"))

# quick evaluation: do the neighbors share the context?
for w in ["graph", "court", "ticket"]:
    print(w, "->", [x for x, _ in wv.most_similar(w, topn=3)])
