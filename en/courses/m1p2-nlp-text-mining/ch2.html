2. Bag of words, TF-IDF and BM25

Definition 2.1 — Bag of words (BoW) A document = a vector of counts over the vocabulary \(V\) (order lost): \(d \in \mathbb{N}^{|V|}\). Documents × terms matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{N}^{n \times |V|}\) — extremely sparse (a few hundred non-zeros per document across tens of thousands of columns; sparse representation, cf. Refresher CS).
Definition 2.2 — TF-IDF $$\mathrm{tfidf}(t, d) = \mathrm{tf}(t, d) \times \underbrace{\log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)}}_{\mathrm{idf}(t)},$$ \(\mathrm{tf}\) = frequency of the term in the document, \(\mathrm{df}\) = number of documents containing the term, \(n\) = number of documents. It penalizes omnipresent terms and rewards discriminative ones. L2 weighting: we post-normalize \(\tilde d = d / \|d\|_2\), so that the dot product is directly the cosine similarity — this is what TfidfVectorizer does by default.
Proposition 2.3 — IDF = mutual information Let us choose a document \(D\) uniformly among the \(n\), and let \(T\) denote the event "\(D\) contains the term \(t\)". Then for any document \(d\) containing \(t\): $$\mathrm{PMI}(T\,;D=d) \;=\; \log\frac{\mathbb{P}(T,\,D=d)}{\mathbb{P}(T)\,\mathbb{P}(D=d)} \;=\; \log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(t)} \;=\; \mathrm{idf}(t).$$ In other words: IDF is exactly the mutual information between "the event \(t\) occurs" and the choice of this particular document. A term present everywhere identifies no document (zero PMI); a rare term strongly identifies the documents that contain it.
\(\mathbb{P}(D=d)=1/n\) and \(\mathbb{P}(T)=\mathrm{df}(t)/n\), so \(\mathbb{P}(T,D=d)=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]\). Substituting: \(\mathrm{PMI} = \log\frac{\mathbf{1}[t\in d]/n}{(\mathrm{df}/n)(1/n)} = \log\frac{n\,\mathbf{1}[t\in d]}{\mathrm{df}}\), which equals \(\mathrm{idf}(t)\) if \(t \in d\) (and \(-\infty\) otherwise). ∎
Common variants (all useful in practice)
  • Sublinear TF: \(1+\log \mathrm{tf}\) — "3 occurrences do not signal the topic 3× more than a single one";
  • Boolean TF: \(\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{tf}>0]\) — robust to highly repetitive documents (spam, bots);
  • Smoothed IDF: \(\log\frac{1+n}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1\) (the scikit-learn variant) — avoids blow-ups for \(\mathrm{df}=n\);
  • Composite schemes (SMART nomenclature): different weighting on the document side (e.g. L2 of log-tf × idf) and on the query side (e.g. plain cosine); the performance of a search engine is often tuned here, before any model.
Model 2.4 — BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza) and its probabilistic reading A direct heir of probabilistic information retrieval (the Robertson–Sparck Jones 2-Poisson framework): a document is a mixture of "informative / non-informative" for the query; the number of occurrences \(x\) of the term follows two Poisson distributions with intensities \(\mu_1 \gg \mu_0\) depending on the class; the log-likelihood ratio \(\;x\log\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_0}-(\mu_1-\mu_0)\;\) is increasing then saturating in \(x\). BM25 approximates its shape: $$\mathrm{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)\cdot \frac{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)+k_1\!\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)},\qquad \mathrm{idf}_{25}(t)=\log\frac{n-\mathrm{df}(t)+0{,}5}{\mathrm{df}(t)+0{,}5}.$$
Two limits to know by heart. Saturation: as \(\mathrm{tf}\to\infty\), the factor tends to \(k_1+1\) (bounded); at \(\mathrm{tf}=0\) it equals \(0\); it reaches half of its maximum when \(\mathrm{tf}=k_1(1-b+b|d|/\mathrm{avgdl})\) — \(k_1\) tunes the saturation speed, \(b\) the length penalty (\(b=0\): no normalization; \(b=1\): proportional). Hence the flaws of raw TF-IDF: unbounded linear growth on the tf side, and no handling of document length. ∎

Proposition 2.5 — Hashing trick for giant vocabularies For huge or a priori unbounded \(|V|\) (tweet streams, logs): hash each term \(h(t) \in \{1,\dots,D\}\) with a random sign \(s(t)\in\{\pm1\}\), and set \(\tilde x_i = \sum_{t:\,h(t)=i} s(t)\,x_t\). Then \(\mathbb{E}\,\langle \tilde x,\tilde y\rangle = \langle x,y\rangle\) — collisions cancel out in expectation.
\(\tilde x^\top\tilde y = \sum_{t,t'} s(t)s(t')\,x_t y_{t'}\,\mathbf{1}[h(t)=h(t')]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[s(t)s(t')]=\mathbf{1}[t=t']\) (independent signs); what remains is \(\sum_t x_t y_t\). The variance of the collisions decreases like \(1/\sqrt{D}\): with \(D\sim 2^{20}\), the approximation is excellent for classification. ∎
Geometry of documents Each document becomes a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\); we measure similarities (cosine), classify (Naive Bayes, SVM), cluster (k-means). The limitation of BoW: bag = no order and no semantics; "the cat eats the mouse" ≈ "the mouse eats the cat". Embeddings (ch. 3) and transformers (ch. 7) answer this. BM25 remains the baseline of any retrieval system — including RAG (M2 LLMs, ch. 7, where it serves as a reference point for dense retrievers). 
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity
from rank_bm25 import BM25Okapi

corpus = ["graphe de connaissances entreprise",
          "réseau de neurones profond",
          "graphe neuronal et réseau d'entreprise"]
X = TfidfVectorizer().fit_transform(corpus)      # sparse matrix (3, |V|), L2-normalized
print(cosine_similarity(X).round(2))             # docs 0 and 2 are the closest

toks = [d.split() for d in corpus]
bm25 = BM25Okapi(toks)
print(bm25.get_scores("réseau entreprise".split()))  # BM25 score per document
