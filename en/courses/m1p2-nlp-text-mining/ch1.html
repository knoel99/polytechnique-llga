1. The text processing pipeline

Definition 1.1 — Standard pipeline Raw text → cleaning (HTML, case, accents, Unicode) → sentence segmentation → tokenization (splitting into units: words, subwords, characters) → lexical normalization (stemming raw root / lemmatization canonical form; stopwords) → representation (ch. 2-4) → model (ch. 5-7) → evaluation. Each stage destroys information; the engineer's craft is deciding which information is expendable for the task at hand.

1.1. Unicode normalization and "clean" text

Definition 1.2 — Unicode normal forms The same glyph admits several encodings: "é" = one codepoint U+00E9 in NFC (canonical composition), but two (e + combining accent U+0301) in NFD. NFKC goes further: ligature "ﬁ" → "fi", digit "²" → "2", full-width letters → ASCII. Two strings that are visually identical but distinct in bytes break counting, deduplication and search; so we normalize before anything else (in practice NFKC, or NFC if typography and units must be preserved).
  • Case: lower() merges "Polytechnique" and "polytechnique" but also the acronym and the common word; in French, keep the accents in lowercase ("élève" ≠ "eleve");
  • Sentence segmentation: rules (punctuation + capital letter) or learning (Punkt, Kiss & Strunk 2006); real pitfalls: "M.", "etc.", German abbreviations ("z. B."), period before a closing quote. Bad segmentation wrecks ch. 8 (extractive summarization: the sentence is the unit being selected);
  • Stemming vs lemmatization: the stemmer (Porter, Snowball) cuts off suffixes by rules — fast, crude, suited to information retrieval; the lemmatizer uses a dictionary and morphology ("sont" → "être") — slower, better for fine-grained analysis. In industry: stemmer for search, lemmatizer for analysis.

1.2. Subword tokenization: BPE, WordPiece, Unigram, SentencePiece

Why subwords Splitting on spaces works poorly: German compounds ("Donaudampfschifffahrt"), French elisions ("l'arbre": 1 or 2 tokens?), some Asian languages write without spaces, and any closed vocabulary runs into unknown words (typos, neologisms, product references). Subword tokenizers learn, on the corpus, a fixed-size vocabulary (~32k-128k) whose concatenation reconstructs any string: frequent words = 1 token, rare words = a sequence of pieces.
Algorithm 1.3 — BPE (Byte Pair Encoding, Sennrich et al. 2016) Initial vocabulary: characters (or bytes). Iteratively: (1) count all pairs of adjacent symbols in the corpus (weighted by word frequencies); (2) merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; (3) repeat until reaching \(|V|\). The result is an ordered set of merge rules; tokenizing a new text = applying the merges in the learned order.
Example 1.4 — Step-by-step BPE training Mini-corpus (words, frequencies): base×5, bases×4, basse×3, basique×2. Initial alphabet: b, a, s, e, i, q, u. We write each word as a sequence of symbols and count adjacent pairs:
StepMost frequent pairsChosen merge
1(b,a)=14, (a,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, (s,i)=(i,q)=(q,u)=(u,e)=2ba (14; tie with (a,s), arbitrary choice)
2(ba,s)=14, (s,e)=12, (e,s)=4, (s,s)=3, …bas (14)
3(bas,e)=9, (s,e)=7, (bas,s)=3, (e,s)=3, …base (9)
4(base,s)=4, (bas,s)=3, (s,e)=3, …bases (4)
5(bas,s)=3, (s,e)=3, …bass (3)
6(bass,e)=3, …basse (3)
In the end, base, bases, basse and basique are whole tokens; an unexpected word like "basiste" breaks down cleanly into bas+iste-or-characters: never an unknown, at worst an overhead in length. This is exactly the mechanism, pushed to bytes and 128k tokens, that equips GPT and Llama — developed in M2 LLMs, ch. 1.
Variants 1.5 — WordPiece (BERT), Unigram (T5), SentencePiece
  • WordPiece: the same merge loop, but a pair's score is \(\frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)\,f(y)}\) — we merge the pair whose relative frequency increases the most, a proxy for the likelihood gain of a unigram model (justification: merging \(x,y\) replaces two symbols with one; the gain in bits is all the greater as the collocation is over-represented relative to independence);
  • Unigram (Kudo, 2018): a generative model — a candidate vocabulary (characters + frequent substrings), each segment \(s\) has a probability \(p(s)\), a word is segmented by Viterbi by maximizing \(\sum_j \log p(s_j)\). Training by EM: E — for each word, the posterior distribution over its segmentations (∝ product of the \(p(s_j)\), computable by a forward dynamic program); M — \(p(s)\) = normalized expected counts; then pruning of the segments that degrade the likelihood the least, until \(|V|\). Unlike BPE, the segmentation is probabilistic and admits several analyses (subword regularization);
  • SentencePiece: a language-independent layer that treats the sentence as a sequence of bytes, the space becoming a "▁" symbol; no pre-tokenization on spaces — essential for languages without spaces and for multilingual settings (it is the foundation of Llama and T5).
import re
from collections import Counter

# --- Toy BPE: learning merges on the mini-corpus of example 1.4 ---
words = {"b a s e": 5, "b a s e s": 4, "b a s s e": 3, "b a s i q u e": 2}
merges = []
for _ in range(8):
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, f in words.items():
        sym = w.split()
        for pair in zip(sym, sym[1:]):
            pairs[pair] += f
    if not pairs:
        break
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)          # most frequent adjacent pair
    merges.append(best)
    big = " ".join(best)
    words = {re.sub(r"(?<!\S)" + re.escape(big) + r"(?!\S)", "".join(best), w): f
             for w, f in words.items()}
print("fusions :", merges[:6])
# tokenize a new word = replay the merges in the learned order
def encode(word, merges):
    sym = list(word)
    for a, b in merges:
        i, out = 0, []
        while i < len(sym):
            if i + 1 < len(sym) and (sym[i], sym[i+1]) == (a, b):
                out.append(a + b); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        sym = out
    return sym
print(encode("basiste", merges))

1.3. Stopwords, n-grams, collocations

  • Stopwords: to be removed for classical text mining (they saturate the BoW); to be kept for transformers — negation ("ne…pas") and syntax matter. The decision must be justified by the task, not by habit;
  • N-grams: sequences of \(n\) tokens; "New York" (bigram) ≠ "New" + "York"; in classification, moving from unigrams to bigrams captures compositional polarity ("pas cher"); cost: the vocabulary explodes (potentially \(|V|^n\)) — hence ngram_range=(1,2) and min_df in the code of ch. 5;
  • Collocations: bigrams whose frequency exceeds what independence predicts, measured by mutual information \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}\) — a criterion reused as-is in ch. 2 to justify IDF, and in ch. 4 for topic coherence.
