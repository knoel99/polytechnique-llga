5. Text classification

Theorem 5.1 — Multinomial Naive Bayes, derived With the (false!) assumption of word independence given the class, \(\mathbb{P}(d\mid c)=\prod_{t\in d}\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)^{\mathrm{tf}(t,d)}\), and Bayes: $$\hat c = \arg\max_c \Big[\log \mathbb{P}(c) + \sum_{t\in d}\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\log \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)\Big].$$ MLE of the parameters: \(\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}}{n_c}\) (counts), \(\mathbb{P}(c)=\frac{n_c}{n}\). Laplace smoothing: $$\mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=\frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|V|}.$$
(i) The MLE comes from maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood under the constraint \(\sum_t \mathbb{P}(t\mid c)=1\) (Lagrange multipliers — relative counts). (ii) Problem: a word seen at test time but never in class \(c\) gives \(\log 0 = -\infty\) — a single rarity wipes out the whole class; in French ("compte" etc.) this is systematic on small corpora. (iii) Laplace smoothing = the MAP under a uniform Dirichlet\((1,\dots,1)\) prior: the posterior density \(\propto \prod_t \pi_t^{n_{t,c}}\cdot\prod_t\pi_t^{1-1}\) is maximized at \(\frac{n_{t,c}+1}{n_c+|V|}\) — the exact Bayesian link of ch. 0. ∎
Why NB works despite an absurd assumption Conditional independence is massively violated ("New" predicts "York"). But the decision (argmax) is often correct even when the probabilities are badly calibrated: it is enough for the right candidate to win, not for its score to be right (the theory of Domingos & Pazzani). Hence NB's status: bad probabilities, good baseline — always the first one in a text project, because it trains in seconds and sets the performance floor.
Sparse logistic regression: why it works in high dimension On TF-IDF, \(\#\text{features} \gg \#\text{docs}\) (10⁵ features for 10³ documents): the risk of overfitting seems maximal, yet the regularized linear model (L2, or L1 to select) generalizes very well. Three reasons: (1) in high dimension the vectors are almost orthogonal — examples are naturally separable, and regularization suffices to pick a "sober" separator; (2) IDF weighting has already done a large share of the statistical work (prop. 2.3); (3) discriminative unigrams/bigrams are exactly the features a linear model weights well. State of the art of the "classical" era until 2018 (Joulin et al., fastText); still formidable below 1,000 labeled examples.
  • Calibration: when the probability is consumed (business thresholds, ticket routing), recalibrate — Platt (logistic regression on the scores) or isotonic; diagnostic: reliability curve (predicted vs observed reliability);
  • Imbalanced classes: class weighting (class_weight="balanced"), optimal threshold on the PR curve (not ROC); metric: macro-F1 (per-class average — every class counts equally) rather than accuracy (which flatters the majority); per-intent confusion matrix for chatbots;
  • Networks: sentence CNNs (Kim, 2014), BiLSTMs; then transformer fine-tuning (ch. 7). Empirical learning curves: linear+TF-IDF wins below ~10²-10³ examples, the transformer above;
  • Zero-shot / few-shot with an LLM: zero labeled examples, k examples in the prompt (M2 LLMs, ch. 4): an excellent exploratory starting point and assisted labeling (active learning), rarely the final solution in production (cost, latency, confidentiality) — the trade-off is detailed in M2.
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_20newsgroups
from sklearn.metrics import f1_score, classification_report

train = fetch_20newsgroups(subset="train", remove=("headers","footers","quotes"))
test  = fetch_20newsgroups(subset="test",  remove=("headers","footers","quotes"))

pipe = make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(min_df=3, ngram_range=(1,2), sublinear_tf=True),
                     LogisticRegression(C=10, max_iter=2000, class_weight="balanced"))
pipe.fit(train.data, train.target)
pred = pipe.predict(test.data)
print("macro-F1:", f1_score(test.target, pred, average="macro"))
print(classification_report(test.target, pred, target_names=test.target_names[:3]))
