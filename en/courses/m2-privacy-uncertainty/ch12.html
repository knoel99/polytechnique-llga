12. Applications: deciding under uncertainty

Example 12.1 — Medicine: calibration as a condition of care A risk model announcing "30% complications" triggers a costly protocol: if the 30% announcers actually operate at 45% (underconfidence), we overtreat; at 15% (overconfidence), we undertreat — both kill. Van Calster et al. (2019) document the chronic miscalibration of published clinical models and call it the "Achilles heel of predictive analytics"; remedy: mandatory reliability diagrams (ch. 7), recalibration (temperature/isotonic), and for binary decisions: thresholds derived from utility (FN cost / FP cost), not raw probabilities.
Definition 12.2 — Expected-utility decision, abstention, risk control With costs \(c_{\text{FP}}, c_{\text{FN}}\), the optimal classifier will predict 1 only if \(\hat p \geq c_{\text{FP}} / (c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})\) — an exact formula if \(\hat p\) is calibrated (ch. 6); hence the sequence of the module: calibrate (7), aggregate the sources (8-10), then decide. Selective abstention: the model may answer "I don't know" when the conformal set has > k elements or the CQR band is wider than a business threshold (delegation to a human); the risk-coverage curve is plotted in the lab. Risk control (Angelopoulos, Bates, Candès, Jordan, Lei, 2021): choose the threshold itself on a calibration set to control an expectation of arbitrary risk (error rate > budget; false negatives < budget); it is the same empirical-quantile equation as ch. 9, applied to a decision threshold — the final building block of the "guarantee" chain.
