1. Differential privacy: the foundation

The historical motivation (reconstructed by Dinur-Nissim, 2003): publishing "anonymized" statistics about a database is often enough to reconstruct the individual data by cross-referencing enough queries — naive anonymization (removing name/SSN) does not resist. The answer of Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith (2006): define protection as a property of the mechanism (the randomized algorithm), not of the data.

Definition 1.1 — Neighboring and differential privacy Two databases \(x, x'\) are neighboring if they differ in the data of a single individual (one row added/removed, or modified: one of the two conventions will be specified). A randomized mechanism \(M\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP if for every pair of neighboring databases and every subset \(S\) of outputs: $$\mathbb{P}(M(x) \in S) \;\leq\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \mathbb{P}(M(x') \in S).$$ \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) version: the inequality may be violated with probability \(\leq \delta\) (a figure to be read as "cryptographically improbable", \(\delta \ll 1/n\)). Reading: changing the data of one person changes the distribution of the published conclusions by at most (multiplicatively) \(e^\varepsilon\) — so these conclusions reveal little about that person, whatever the adversary's side knowledge.
Proposition 1.2 — Three structural properties (i) Post-processing: if \(M\) is \(\varepsilon\)-DP, any \(g \circ M\) (subsequent analysis, training, selective publication) still is — privacy does not degrade through processing of the outputs. (ii) Group privacy: if \(x, x'\) differ on \(k\) individuals, \(\mathbb{P}(M(x) \in S) \leq e^{k\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(M(x') \in S)\). (iii) Convexity: a mixture of \(\varepsilon\)-DP mechanisms is \(\varepsilon\)-DP.
(i) For every section \(A\), \(\mathbb{P}(g(M(x)) \in S) = \mathbb{P}(M(x) \in g^{-1}(S)) \leq e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(M(x') \in g^{-1}(S)) = e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(g(M(x')) \in S)\) — the direct image of a dominated measure preserves the Radon-Nikodym ratio. (ii) A chain of \(k\) intermediate neighboring databases and multiplication of the \(e^\varepsilon\). (iii) For a mixture with weights \(\lambda_j\) over \(M_j\) all \(\varepsilon\)-DP: \(\sum_j \lambda_j \mathbb{P}(M_j(x) \in S) \leq \sum_j \lambda_j e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(M_j(x') \in S) = e^\varepsilon \mathbb{P}(\text{mixture}(x') \in S)\). ∎
Definition 1.3 — Sensitivity The \(\ell_1\) (resp. \(\ell_2\)) sensitivity of a query \(f\) on neighboring databases: $$\Delta_1 f = \max_{x \sim x'} \|f(x) - f(x')\|_1, \qquad \Delta_2 f = \max_{x \sim x'} \|f(x) - f(x')\|_2.$$ Computed examples: counting (how many individuals satisfy \(Q\)?) \(\Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1\) (one row changes the count by at most 1); mean over \(n\) rows in \([0, 1]\) \(\Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1/n\) (adding one row shifts the mean by at most \(1/n\)); histogram with \(k\) bins \(\Rightarrow \Delta_1 = 1\) (one row falls in one bin — under the add/remove convention); maximum \(\Rightarrow \Delta_1\) unbounded (hence the systematic clipping in practice, ch. 4). Sensitivity measures "how much one individual can weigh" — it is what will set the noise.
Choosing \(\varepsilon\) — the embarrassing question No universal rule, but industry benchmarks: 2020 US Census: a global \(\varepsilon\) on the order of twenty in total for the person file (Abowd, 2018); Apple (keyboard telemetry): \(\varepsilon\) on the order of 4–8 per day; Google (RAPPOR): \(\varepsilon \approx \ln 3\) per report, cumulative budgets; academic literature: \(\varepsilon \leq 1\) "strict", 1–10 "reasonable". The essential point: \(\varepsilon\) is a budget that one spends (ch. 3), not a moral grade.
