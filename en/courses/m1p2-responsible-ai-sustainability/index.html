This module confronts AI with a double constraint: several simultaneous objectives — performance, energy, cost, impact — and finite resources. From the Pareto front to carbon-aware scheduling, from linear programming to honest measurement of emissions, it treats optimization as a lever for sustainability, with constant vigilance against greenwashing. It brings to the master's program its responsible compass: models and decisions one can justify with figures in hand.

  • Model sustainable decision support in linear and integer linear programming.
  • Quantify the emissions of an AI system with a defensible methodology.
  • Analyze real case studies: data centers, electric vehicles.

0. Refresher: optimization, duality, complexity

L3 reminders — the foundation (see Machine Learning module, ch. 0; Optimal Transport module, ch. 0; Optimization for AI module, ch. 0)
  • Linear program (LP): \(\min_x c^\top x\) subject to \(Ax \leq b\), \(x \geq 0\) — the feasible set is a polyhedron; the optimum (if it exists) is attained at a vertex (extreme point); solved in polynomial time (interior points, simplex in practice).
  • Duality: the dual of an LP is an LP; \(d^\star \leq p^\star\) always (weak duality), equality under conditions (Slater); the dual variables \(\lambda \geq 0\) are interpreted as marginal prices — how much an extra unit of each resource is worth. This "economic" reading will be our central tool: the price of carbon, the price of a MWh, the price of a GPU hour.
  • Integer variables (ILP): as soon as one variable is integral (choose / do not choose), the problem becomes NP-hard in general (see Refresher CS module, ch. 2); it is handled by continuous relaxation + branch and bound (ch. 4).
  • Partial derivatives, convexity: convexity distinguishes "easy" (a local minimum is global) from "hard" — see the Optimization for AI module, ch. 1.
Proposition 0.1 — The lemma that serves the whole module (proved) For an LP \(\min\{c^\top x : Ax = b,\ x \geq 0\}\) and any feasible dual solution \((\lambda, \mu \geq 0)\) (i.e. \(A^\top \lambda \leq c\) in the appropriate sense): \(b^\top \lambda \leq c^\top x\) for every feasible \(x\). If equality is attained, both solutions are optimal.
\(c^\top x \geq (A^\top\lambda)^\top x = \lambda^\top A x = \lambda^\top b\), using dual feasibility first, then \(Ax = b\). If \(c^\top x = b^\top\lambda\), then \(x\) attains a dual lower bound: \(x\) and \(\lambda\) are optimal (and the gap \(c^\top x - b^\top\lambda\), zero, decomposes as \(\mu^\top x\), whence the complementary slackness \(\mu_i x_i = 0\)). ∎
Position of the module — the "decision" point of view The other modules of the master's program optimize models (loss, generalization). Here we optimize decisions: when to launch which training run, on which data center, with what numerical precision, which performance/energy trade-off to accept. Three new ingredients: several incommensurable objectives (ch. 1), constrained physical resources (ch. 2), and the requirement to measure what we claim to optimize (ch. 6–7) — for a mis-counted kg CO2 optimizes a fable, not the climate.

1. Multi-objective optimization and the Pareto front

"Maximize accuracy", "minimize energy", "minimize latency": three objectives with no common unit. Adding them naively — a "magic loss" — hides the trade-offs; the Pareto formalism makes them explicit and negotiable.

Definition 1.1 — Dominance and Pareto optimality Let there be \(q\) objectives \(F = (F_1, \dots, F_q)\), all to be minimized over a feasible set \(\mathcal{X}\). A solution \(x\) dominates \(x'\) if \(F_i(x) \leq F_i(x')\) for all \(i\) and \(F_j(x) < F_j(x')\) for at least one \(j\). A solution is Pareto-optimal if no solution dominates it; the image of the set of these solutions is the Pareto front \(\mathcal{P} = F(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Par}}) \subset \mathbb{R}^q\). In the plane, it is the "southwest frontier" of the set of attainable values.
Theorem 1.2 — Weighted scalarization produces Pareto points (proved) Let \(w \in \mathbb{R}^q_+\), \(w \neq 0\), and \(x_w\) a minimizer of the scalar function \(F_w(x) = \sum_i w_i F_i(x)\) over \(\mathcal{X}\). Then:
  • if \(w \geq 0\) (positive or zero weights): \(x_w\) is weakly Pareto-optimal;
  • if \(w > 0\) (all weights strictly positive): \(x_w\) is Pareto-optimal.
Suppose \(x_w\) is dominated by some \(x' \in \mathcal{X}\): \(F_i(x') \leq F_i(x_w)\) for all \(i\), with a strict inequality for one index \(j\). Summing against \(w\): \(F_w(x') = \sum_i w_i F_i(x') \leq \sum_i w_i F_i(x_w) = F_w(x_w)\), and if \(w_j > 0\) the inequality becomes strict: \(F_w(x') < F_w(x_w)\) contradicts the minimality of \(x_w\). Hence (b); (a) follows by noting that with a zero weight on the strictly better component, only the weak inequality remains (weak optimality). ∎
Proposition 1.3 — The converse is false: Pareto points that no weighting finds (worked example) Objectives \(F_1(x) = x\) and \(F_2(x) = 1 - x^2\) over \(x \in [0, 1]\).
\(F_1\) is strictly increasing, \(F_2\) strictly decreasing: every point of \([0,1]\) is Pareto-optimal, and the front is the arc \(\{(t,\, 1-t^2) : t \in [0,1]\}\) — a concave arc (it sags above the chord \((0,1)\)–\((1,0)\): in the middle, \((0{,}5\,;\,0{,}75)\) against \((0{,}5\,;\,0{,}5)\) for the chord), typical of "hollowed" fronts. Yet, for every weighting \(w > 0\), the function \(F_w(x) = w_1 x + w_2(1 - x^2)\) is concave in \(x\) (second derivative \(-2w_2 < 0\)): its minimum over \([0,1]\) is attained at the endpoints, never inside. Weighted scalarizations thus produce only the two endpoints \(x=0\) and \(x=1\): the whole middle of the front — for example \(x = 0{,}5\) — is Pareto-optimal but invisible to any weighting. Geometry: a weighting traces a line of negative slope in the \((F_1,F_2)\) plane and picks the lowest point; on a hollowed front, the line can rest only at the endpoints. In practice: training networks (non-convex landscapes, often hollowed fronts) and then sweeping only the weights visits only part of the trade-off — other tools are needed (\(\varepsilon\)-constraints, Chebyshev method — def. 1.4; multi-objective evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II). ∎
Definition 1.4 — Two scalarizations that see the whole front
  • \(\varepsilon\)-constraints: minimize \(F_1(x)\) subject to \(F_i(x) \leq \varepsilon_i\) (\(i \geq 2\)). By sweeping the \(\varepsilon\), one visits the whole front — including the non-convex part. It is also the most dialogue-friendly formulation: "I impose at most \(k\) kWh on myself, what is the best accuracy?"
  • Chebyshev: \(\min_x \max_i\, w_i\,(F_i(x) - z_i^\star)\) where \(z^\star\) is the ideal point (the minimum of each objective separately). Every Pareto-optimal solution minimizes such a function for some \(w > 0\) (under a standard assumption); the resulting \(w\) reads as an exchange rate.
Example 1.5 — Accuracy vs training energy: the module's most cited front On a given task (say image classification), for every energy budget \(E\) (kWh measured at the wall, ch. 6), the best attainable accuracy \(\mathrm{acc}(E)\) defines a point of the front. Typically: rapid growth (the under-trained model gains a lot for little energy), then an elbow, then a plateau (scaling law — see the Deep Learning P2 module, ch. 1). Typical Pareto-rational decisions: stop at the elbow, where \(-d\,\mathrm{acc}/dE\) drops; the points beyond the elbow are dominated in the "carbon-cost" sense as soon as the kWh is valued at its carbon price (ch. 2). 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# example: two models (growing size), fictitious but realistic measurement
E = np.array([0.4, 1.1, 2.6, 5.0, 9.5, 18.0])      # kWh (measured at the wall)
A = np.array([71.2, 78.9, 84.1, 87.0, 88.3, 89.0]) # accuracy % on a validation set

# Pareto front (minimize E, maximize A): keep the non-dominated points
keep = [True]*len(E)
for i in range(len(E)):
    for j in range(len(E)):
        if E[j] <= E[i] and A[j] >= A[i] and (E[j], A[j]) != (E[i], A[i]):
            keep[i] = False
print("front points:", list(zip(E[keep], A[keep])))
# marginal gain of acc per kWh -- the "elbow" of the front
print("d.acc/dE:", np.diff(A)/np.diff(E))
plt.loglog(E, 100 - A, "o-"); plt.xlabel("E (kWh)"); plt.ylabel("error (%)")
plt.show()

2. Optimizing under resource constraints

Resources: GPU hours, watts, megabytes of memory, and — the module's signature angle — carbon, which depends on when and where one computes. The electric grid is not uniformly green: its carbon intensity varies by a factor of 1 to 10 depending on the hour and the region. A tremendous margin of maneuver for a scheduler.

Definition 2.1 — Carbon intensity and emissions of a computation The carbon intensity \(c_t\) (gCO2eq/kWh) is the amount of CO2 emitted per kWh consumed on the grid, at hour \(t\), in region \(r\) — public hourly data (grid operators, providers such as Electricity Maps). For a power \(P_t\) (kW) consumed during hour \(t\): $$\text{emissions (gCO}_2\text{eq)} \;=\; \sum_t c_t \cdot P_t \cdot \Delta t,$$ to be multiplied by the PUE (ch. 6) if one counts the whole data center. Unlike the kWh, this criterion is non-uniform in time: this is precisely what makes scheduling profitable.
Theorem 2.2 — Temporal shifting gains (at most) the dispersion of \(c_t\) (proved) Let there be a deferrable job of duration \(T\) hours (at constant power \(P\)), executable within a window \([0, H]\). Let \(\bar c\) be the mean and \(c_{(1)} \leq \dots \leq c_{(H)}\) the sorted intensities of the window's hours. Then the minimal emission is attained by executing the job during the \(T\) cleanest hours, and equals \(P\,\Delta t \sum_{s=1}^{T/\Delta t} c_{(s)}\) — in particular the maximal relative gain compared to an average immediate launch is \(\frac{\bar c - \text{mean of the } T \text{ smallest}}{\bar c} \leq \frac{\max c - \min c}{\max c}\).
The decision variable \(0 \leq x_t \leq 1\) (fraction of the job done at hour \(t\)) gives the LP \(\min \sum_t c_t x_t\) subject to \(\sum_t x_t = T/\Delta t\) — a continuous "knapsack": the optimal solution saturates the \(x_t\) of the smallest \(c_t\) (monotone permutation, exchange argument: if \(x_t < 1\), \(x_{t'} > 0\) with \(c_{t'} > c_t\), transferring \(\varepsilon\) from \(t'\) to \(t\) reduces the cost by \(\varepsilon(c_{t'} - c_t) > 0\)). The gain bound follows by comparing with \(\bar c\), itself between \(c_{(1)}\) and \(c_{(H)}\). ∎
What the theorem does not say: the real constraints A training run is not a "knapsack": it has a minimal duration, checkpoints that are costly to move, a deadline (the TME submission!), sometimes dependencies (pipeline, ch. 5 of the Deep Learning P2 module). The true formulation is an ILP: variables \(x_{j,t} \in \{0,1\}\) "task \(j\) runs at hour \(t\)", \(\sum_t x_{j,t} = d_j\) (duration), precedence constraints \(s_j + d_j \leq s_k\), windows, machine capacity \(\sum_j P_j x_{j,t} \leq C\), objective \(\min \sum_{j,t} c_t P_j x_{j,t}\). This is exactly the ground of ch. 4 (branch and bound) — and the heart of the "carbon-aware computing" paper by Radovanovic et al. (Google), deployed in production to shift deferrable tasks toward the cleanest windows and regions (references); the Emerging ML module (ch. 2) covers the systems side (federation, placement).
Example 2.3 — One night of training, 24 choices A 6-hour training run (1 GPU, 0.4 kW, PUE 1.1) can start at any time within the next 48 hours. Hourly intensities (fictitious data but of realistic shape: solar in the middle of the day, wind at night, gas at the evening peak): 
import numpy as np
c = np.array([ 85, 72, 60, 55, 58,110,220,310,380,340,260,160,
              95, 70, 55, 45, 38, 42,120,280,420,470,390,240])  # gCO2/kWh, 0h..23h
c = np.concatenate([c, c])            # two identical days (simplification)
T, P, pue = 6, 0.4, 1.1

best = min(range(48 - T), key=lambda s: c[s:s+T].sum())
worst = max(range(48 - T), key=lambda s: c[s:s+T].sum())
emi = lambda s: P*pue*c[s:s+T].sum() / 1000               # kg CO2eq
print(f"best slot {best}h-{best+T}h: {emi(best):.2f} kg")
print(f"worst slot {worst}h-{worst+T}h: {emi(worst):.2f} kg")
print(f"relative gain: {1 - emi(best)/emi(worst):.0%}")
# expected: ~2x difference between the afternoon solar slot and the evening peak
Dual interpretation: if carbon is penalized at a constant price \(\pi\) (€/tCO2), the "carbon-aware" scheduler and the "cost" scheduler coincide — otherwise, the dual of the LP (ch. 4) says exactly at what carbon price each task switches slots. This is a shadow price: ch. 0 kept its promise.

3. Model frugality: distillation, quantization, pruning

The cheapest kg CO2 is the one not emitted: reducing the model's cost at equal quality is the first lever of sustainable AI. Three families, complementary, all "Pareto-active" (ch. 1).

Definition 3.1 — Distillation (Hinton, Vinyals, Dean, 2015) Train a small student to reproduce the soft outputs of a large teacher: $$\mathcal{L} = \alpha\, \tau^2\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z_T/\tau)\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(z_S/\tau)\big) + (1-\alpha)\,\ell(y, z_S),$$ where \(\tau\) is the temperature: at high temperature, the teacher's logits deliver similarities between classes (dark knowledge) — far more information per example than the one-hot label alone. Reference: Hinton et al. 2015; for the LLM case (logit distillation), see the Deep Learning P2 module, ch. 6.
Proposition 3.2 — Uniform quantization error (proved) Uniform quantization \(q(x) = \mathrm{clip}\big(\mathrm{round}(x/s) + z,\, 0, 2^b-1\big)\) with \(s = \frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{2^b - 1}\) (step \(\Delta = s\)) satisfies, over \([x_{\min}, x_{\max}]\): $$|q(x)\cdot s - x| \;\leq\; \frac{\Delta}{2}.$$ For a network with \(L\)-Lipschitz layers of constants \(\mathrm{Lip}_1, \dots, \mathrm{Lip}_L\) and quantizations of steps \(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_L\): $$\|\hat f(x) - f(x)\| \;\leq\; \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \Big(\Delta_\ell/2\Big) \prod_{m > \ell} \mathrm{Lip}_m.$$
First inequality: nearest-neighbor rounding of a value inside a cell of width \(\Delta\) moves it by at most \(\Delta/2\); clipping only moves values closer to the bounds. Second: by induction on depth. Let \(\varepsilon_\ell\) be the perturbation at the output of layer \(\ell\), and assume (standard assumption) that quantizing the weights of layer \(\ell\) shifts its output by at most \(\Delta_\ell/2\). Layer \(\ell+1\), Lipschitz, propagates \(\varepsilon_\ell\) to \(\mathrm{Lip}_{\ell+1}\,\varepsilon_\ell\), then its own quantization adds at most \(\Delta_{\ell+1}/2\): \(\varepsilon_{\ell+1} \leq \mathrm{Lip}_{\ell+1}\,\varepsilon_\ell + \Delta_{\ell+1}/2\). Unrolling from \(\varepsilon_1 = \Delta_1/2\), each \(\Delta_\ell/2\) is multiplied by the Lipschitz constants of the subsequent layers, giving the announced sum (partial products \(\prod_{m>\ell}\mathrm{Lip}_m\)). ∎

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def quantize_uniform(x, bits=8):
    xmin, xmax = x.min(), x.max()
    s = (xmax - xmin) / (2**bits - 1)
    q = np.clip(np.round((x - xmin)/s), 0, 2**bits - 1).astype(np.int8)
    return q, q*s + xmin                       # codes + dequantize

W = rng.normal(size=(64, 128)) * 0.03          # a trained layer (fictitious)
for bits in (16, 8, 4, 2):
    _, Wq = quantize_uniform(W, bits)
    err = np.abs(Wq - W).max()
    print(f"{bits:2d} bits: max error {err:.2e} (step/2 = "
          f"{((W.max()-W.min())/(2**bits-1))/2:.2e})")
# the error exactly follows the step/2 of proposition 3.2; at 4 bits it becomes
# perceptible -> that is where QAT (simulated fine-tuning) takes over.

4. Sustainable decision support: linear and mixed integer programming

The core business of operations research: turning a complex decision into a mathematical model that a solver drives to the optimum — not "nearly". This is the toolbox of the module's instructor (ch. 8) and the language of the case studies (ch. 5).

Definition 4.1 — Standard form and modeling (recipe) General ILP: $$\min_{x}\; c^\top x \quad \text{subject to}\quad Ax \leq b,\; x_i \in \mathbb{Z} \;\;(i \in I),\; x \geq 0.$$ Modeling recipe (to know by heart): (1) index: what do we decide on? (task × hour, site × capacity); (2) variables: continuous (flows, powers) or binary (yes/no decisions, activation); (3) constraints: physical (capacity, conservation), business (deadline, regulatory); (4) objective: cost, carbon, or both (bi-objective, ch. 1). Three writing reflexes: "at least one of" = \(\sum x_j \geq 1\); "if-then" = big-\(M\); "fixed activation cost" = \(z \in \{0,1\}\), \(x \leq M z\).
Proposition 4.2 — Continuous relaxation: lower bound and integrality gap (proved on an example) The relaxation (which drops \(x_i \in \mathbb{Z}\)) is an LP: its optimum \(\rho^\star\) satisfies \(\rho^\star \leq v^\star\) (every integer solution is feasible for the relaxation — immediate proof by inclusion of sets). The gap \(v^\star - \rho^\star\) is the integrality gap. Example (fractional vs integer knapsack): capacity 10, items \((v_i, w_i) \in \{(10,7), (6,5), (5,4)\}\):
Relaxation. By decreasing value/weight ratio: \(\frac{10}{7} > \frac{5}{4} > \frac{6}{5}\). Take item 1 whole (7 units), leaving 3 units of capacity for item 3 (next ratio): \(\rho^\star = 10 + \frac{3}{4}\cdot 5 = 13{,}75\). ILP. The integer solutions: \(\{1\}\) (10, weight 7), \(\{2,3\}\) (11, weight 9), \(\{1\}\) + nothing else fits — \(v^\star = 11\). Integrality gap \(13{,}75 - 11 = 2{,}75\), i.e. 25%: branch and bound (def. 4.3) will have to close it while proving that no integer choice exceeds 11. ∎
Definition/Theorem 4.3 — Branch and bound: correctness (proved) Algorithm. (i) Solve the relaxation of the current problem; (ii) if the solution is integral, it is a candidate (upper bound); (iii) otherwise choose a fractional variable \(x_i\) and create two subproblems \(x_i \leq \lfloor \bar x_i\rfloor\), \(x_i \geq \lceil \bar x_i\rceil\) (branch); (iv) prune a subproblem if its relaxation is infeasible, already solved, or has bound \(\rho^\star \geq\) the current upper bound (bound). Correctness.
Each branching partitions the set of integer solutions (the two children cover everything, without overlap); subproblems pruned "by bound" cannot contain an optimum: their relaxation lower-bounds all their integer solutions (prop. 4.2), so none improves the current upper bound; the other cases prune empty sets. At termination, the tree has covered the whole space except regions proven non-optimal: the best integer candidate found is the global optimum. (Termination is guaranteed in a finite number of branchings, the variables being bounded by the constraints.) ∎
In practice: solvers, formulations, difficulty The quality of an ILP formulation is judged by the quality of its relaxation: two formulations exactly equivalent in integer solutions can differ by a factor of 1000 in solving time (this is the modeler's whole art — see Wolsey, references). Open-source solvers: CBC, HiGHS; commercial: Gurobi, CPLEX. The module does not ask you to run them by hand: exercise 6 walks through a branch and bound on a 4-item knapsack, then has it checked by PuLP: 
import pulp
# knapsack: maximize value under capacity, binary variables
v = {0: 6, 1: 5, 2: 5, 3: 1};  w = {0: 5, 1: 5, 2: 5, 3: 1};  C = 10
prob = pulp.LpProblem("knapsack", pulp.LpMaximize)
x = {i: pulp.LpVariable(f"x{i}", cat="Binary") for i in v}
prob += pulp.lpSum(v[i]*x[i] for i in v)
prob += pulp.lpSum(w[i]*x[i] for i in v) <= C
prob.solve(pulp.PULP_CBC_CMD(msg=0))
print([(i, x[i].value()) for i in v], "=", pulp.value(prob.objective))
# "sustainability" variant: two objectives (value, carbon) -> epsilon-constraint
# prob += carbon(x) <= budget; maximize value  (see exercise 8)

5. Case studies: data centers and electric vehicles

Case study 5.1 — Data center powered by renewable energies Data. A compute site: flexible load (deferrable jobs: training runs, indexing, simulations) and rigid load (interactive services). Local energies: solar \(s_t\) (daytime slots), wind \(w_t\), grid complement \(g_t \geq 0\) at intensity \(c_t\). Model (sliding-window LP). $$\min \sum_t \Big(\gamma\, g_t\, c_t\Big)\;\Delta t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \begin{cases} g_t + s_t + w_t \;\geq\; \mathrm{PUE}\cdot u_t & \text{(power balance)}\\ \sum_{t \in \text{day}} u_t \;\geq\; D & \text{(total deferred work)}\\ u_t \leq U^{\max}, \quad u_t = U^{\text{rigid}}_t + f_t,\; f_t \geq 0 &\end{cases}$$ where \(f_t\) is the power allocated to flexible work. Dual reading. The multiplier of the total work constraint equals (in €/carbon kWh) "how much it costs to delay a kWh of compute": when solar abounds it becomes zero (a period of carbon free-ness), when the evening peak arrives it explodes: this is the dynamic price signal derived from the model, and the same mechanism as the industrial deployment described by Radovanovic et al. (references): planning ahead with forecasts of \(s_t, w_t, c_t\) (forecasts to which the Machine Learning module, ch. 5–6, applies directly).
Case study 5.2 — Coordinated charging of an electric vehicle fleet Data. \(N\) vehicles, each plugged in from \(a_i\) to \(d_i\), need \(E_i\) (kWh), max power \(p_i\). Grid: hourly intensity \(c_t\), tariff \(p_t^\text{elec}\), local transformer capacity \(K\) (the real "bottleneck" of company parking lots: without coordination, everyone plugs in at 6 pm). Model. $$\min_{x_{i,t} \geq 0}\; \sum_{i,t}\; c_t\, x_{i,t} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \begin{cases} \sum_{t=a_i}^{d_i} x_{i,t}\,\Delta t \;\geq\; E_i & \forall i \;\;\text{(energy need)}\\ 0 \leq x_{i,t} \leq p_i & \forall (i,t) \\ \sum_i x_{i,t} \;\leq\; K & \forall t \;\;\text{(transformer capacity)}\end{cases}$$ Pure LP (continuous): solved to the optimum in seconds for thousands of vehicles. The dual delivers the usage prices of the transformer hour by hour — enough to price the incentive rather than forbid. ILP variant: fast charging available when needed (\(z_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}\), \(x \leq M z\), fixed cost \(\kappa z\)); stochastic variant: unexpected departures (scenarios, recourse); robust variant (ch. 8: uncertainty on \(c_t\)). 
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

# 3 vehicles, 8 hourly slots, transformer capacity 11 kW
T, K = 8, 11.0
c = np.array([ 60, 55, 52, 58,140,320,380,300])       # gCO2/kWh per slot
veh = [ (0, 4, 20.0, 7.0), (1, 5, 28.0, 7.0), (3, 8, 15.0, 3.5) ]  # (a, d, E, p)
n = len(veh) * T                                      # variable x[i, t] = idx i*T + t
cost = np.array([c[idx % T] for idx in range(n)])     # minimize sum c_t x_it

A_ub, b_ub = [], []                                   # capacity per slot
for t in range(T):
    row = [1.0 if idx % T == t else 0.0 for idx in range(n)]
    A_ub.append(row); b_ub.append(K)
A_eq, b_eq = [], []                                   # energy need
for i, (a, d, E, p) in enumerate(veh):
    row = [1.0 if (idx // T == i and a <= idx % T < d) else 0.0 for idx in range(n)]
    A_eq.append(row); b_eq.append(E)
bounds = [(0, p) for (a, d, E, p) in veh for _ in range(T)]
res = linprog(cost, A_ub=np.array(A_ub), b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=np.array(A_eq),
              b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
x = res.x.reshape(len(veh), T)
print("optimal emissions (kg CO2eq):", round(res.fun / 1000, 2))
print(np.round(x, 1))   # the cars wait for the low-carbon slots
What these cases teach (meta-lesson) (1) Structure matters more than size: these models with thousands of variables solve in seconds because they are linear; a single non-linear term can change everything. (2) The dual is a product: dynamic prices, incentive signals, marginal value of carbon. (3) The hard part is not the optimizer but the data (forecasts, measurements) and governance (who accepts that their task waits?) — optimization is the art of making these trade-offs explicit.

7. Limits, rebound effect and greenwashing

Honesty note — the five pitfalls of carbon accounting
  1. Average vs marginal. Adding up kWh at a country's average intensity underestimates the real effect: new consumption calls, at peak time, on marginal generation means (often gas: 400–600 g/kWh). The "average or marginal?" debate changes conclusions by a factor of 2 to 5 — honesty requires testing both.
  2. Chosen scope. Counting training but not inference (which, at the scale of millions of users, often dominates); forgetting hardware manufacturing (depreciation: up to a third of the footprint depending on the study); forgetting storage and network.
  3. Offsets and RECs. Buying green certificates does not change the physics of the grid: nighttime computing in a coal region emits coal, even covered with certificates. Claiming "net zero" on that basis is documented greenwashing.
  4. Rebound effect (Jevons). Making AI four times cheaper in energy often increases... total AI consumption: new uses (and training loops) absorb the gain. Efficiency is necessary, not sufficient: only a cap constraint (an absolute carbon budget, ch. 1: \(\varepsilon\)-constraints) turns it into a guarantee.
  5. False precision. "273.4 kg" when the actual hourly intensity is unknown to ±30%: publish ranges and assumptions, not magic numbers.
Proposition 7.1 — Audit grid for a "green AI" claim
Audit questionRed flag
What scope?"our model" without inference, without manufacturing, without data
What intensity, what source, what time?annual national average for a localized data center; "contractual" intensity

The final question — "does AI reduce more emissions than it causes?" — is legitimate, open, and beyond the reach of a simple measurement: it requires a full life-cycle model. The module provides the tools to pose it honestly, not to answer it with a slogan.
Professional positioning The "responsible AI" engineer is neither an activist nor an accountant: they are someone who knows how to (i) model a trade-off (ch. 1), (ii) optimize under resources (ch. 2, 4, 5), (iii) reduce the cost of models (ch. 3), (iv) measure with a protocol (ch. 6), (v) state the limits of their figures (ch. 7). Exercises

  1. Pareto front by hand. Objectives \(F_1(x)=(x-1)^2+1\), \(F_2(x)=(x+1)^2+1\), \(x \in [-3,3]\). (a) Plot the attainable set in the \((F_1,F_2)\) plane and the front. (b) Show that \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\) are dominated. (c) For \(w=(1,1)\), solve the scalarization and verify that it gives \(x=0\). (d) On the objectives \(F_1(x)=x\), \(F_2(x)=1-x^2\) of prop. 1.3, show that \(x=0{,}5\) is Pareto-optimal but is not the minimizer of any weighted scalarization \(w>0\). Solution: (c) \(F_w = 2x^2+4\) minimal at 0; (d) \(F_w\) is concave in \(x\), hence minimal at the endpoints, see prop. 1.3.
  2. Chebyshev. Still on \(F_1(x)=x\), \(F_2(x)=1-x^2\), \(x \in [0,1]\) (ideal point \(z^\star=(0,0)\)). (a) Verify that every point is Pareto-optimal. (b) Show that \(g_w(x) = \max\{w_1 F_1(x),\, w_2 F_2(x)\}\) attains its minimum at the equilibrium \(w_1 x = w_2(1-x^2)\) (one grows, the other decreases). (c) Deduce that every point of the front is produced by some \(w>0\) — a complete contrast with prop. 1.3 — and compute \(w_1/w_2\) to obtain \(x = 0{,}5\). Solution: (c) at equilibrium \(w_1/w_2 = (1-x^2)/x = 3/2\) for \(x=0{,}5\).

def distill_loss(zs, zt, y, tau=4.0, alpha=0.7):
    kd = F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(zs/tau, 1), F.softmax(zt/tau, 1),
                  reduction="batchmean") * tau*tau
    return alpha*kd + (1-alpha)*F.cross_entropy(zs, y)
