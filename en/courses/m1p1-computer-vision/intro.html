Making a machine see: such is the program of computer vision, from the optical formation of images to detection CNNs, through filtering, multi-view geometry, and 3D point clouds. This course builds "classical" vision and learned vision as one and the same story — convolution connects the manual filtering of chapter 2 to the CNNs of chapter 9 — and leads to a concrete application field: urban LiDAR analyzed at IGN, the instructor's research domain. Vision skills are in demand from industry (perception, mapping, manufacturing) to the generative multimodal models of the master's program.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how an image forms — camera, projection, sampling — and deduce the limits of the system.
  • Design and apply filters and convolutions, and connect classical filtering with the first layers of CNNs.
  • Detect edges and interest points (Canny, Harris, SIFT) and know what they are used for in practice.
  • Handle projective geometry: homographies, calibration, image rectification.
  • State the stereo problem and use epipolar geometry to reconstruct in 3D.
  • Estimate motion by optical flow (Lucas-Kanade) and know its assumptions of validity.
  • Segment an image by k-means or by graph cuts — the latter framing vision as a graph problem.
  • Implement vision CNNs for detection and segmentation, and process 3D point clouds with ICP.

Official objectives and syllabus (2026-2027 catalog)

The official sheet (X3A ME1) sets the frame: "the objective of computer vision is to compute properties of the world from digital images", with problems such as 3D shape recovery, motion estimation, or object recognition, and an introduction that "covers themes such as feature detection, motion estimation, image mosaics, and 3D vision". The themes are discussed "in terms of algorithms and mathematical tools", and the applications can be developed "in Python or C++" — the algorithmic orientation of these materials. Official prerequisites: not communicated; assessment: 5 small classes + 1 project.

Where this course fits in the program

Elective of Year 1 · Period 1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS), outside the recommended track: the "vision" choice of the period. Official prerequisites: not stated on the sheet. These materials assume two internal prerequisites: Signal Processing, whose filtering, convolution, and sampling it consumes (the review chapter takes up linear algebra, convolution, and probability), and Deep Learning, required for the vision CNN chapter.

Downstream, three modules directly extend this one: Computer Vision: From Fundamentals to Applications (Period 2) is its declared sequel, from fundamentals to applications; Multimodal Generative AI mobilizes visual encoders and diffusion over images; Advanced Deep Learning deepens vision architectures among advanced models. Note: the graph-cut segmentation of chapter 8 also connects vision to the "graphs" side of the master's program, up to Graph ML.

Course outline

  • Review: linear algebra, convolution, probability: the three pillars taken up from Signal Processing and the refreshers, at the level required in vision.
  • Image formation: camera and sampling: from the 3D world to the pixel — the camera model and its artifacts.
  • Filtering and convolution: smoothing, detecting, combining — the founding operation of everything that follows.
  • Edges: gradient and Canny: extracting the significant boundaries of an image.
  • Interest points: Harris and SIFT: anchoring correspondences between images, before deep learning.
  • Projective geometry and homographies: computing across views — crop, rectify, stitch.
  • Stereo vision and epipolar geometry: two views, one constraint — reconstructing depth.
  • Optical flow: Lucas-Kanade: measuring apparent motion, with its assumptions and its failures.
  • Segmentation: k-means and graph cuts: partitioning the image — and framing vision as a graph problem.
  • CNNs for vision: detection and segmentation: the learned successor to manual filtering, up to dense tasks.
  • 3D point clouds: ICP and urban analysis: aligning and analyzing LiDAR clouds — vision beyond images.
  • Research of the instructor: urban LiDAR at IGN: Mathieu Brédif's research, from the 3D city to massive geospatial data.

How to study this module

  • Visualize everything: each chapter benefits from being validated on real images (display the gradients, the Harris maps, the segmentations) — a vision error can be seen.
  • Implement convolution, Canny, and Lucas-Kanade in NumPy/OpenCV on your own photos before moving to CNNs: the convolutional layers will be all the more readable.
  • Write the projective geometry matrices by hand on a small numerical example (homogeneous points, homography): the formalism only settles in by being practiced.
  • If convolution or sampling is fuzzy, go back to the Signal Processing module; if CNNs are, to the Deep Learning module.
  • Remember the guiding thread: the same operator — convolution — runs through classical filtering and the learned networks of chapter 9.
  • Finish with the 3D chapters: ICP and urban LiDAR are a direct gateway to geospatial internships and to Computer Vision: From Fundamentals to Applications in Period 2.
