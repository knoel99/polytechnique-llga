3. Edges: gradient and Canny

An edge is a rapid variation of intensity: the first order (gradient) locates it by its maxima, the second order (Laplacian) by its zero crossings. The Canny detector (1986) combines these ideas into a pipeline still present in industrial chains.

Definition 3.1 — Image gradient \(\nabla I = (I_x, I_y)^\top\); magnitude \(|\nabla I| = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2}\) (rate of variation), direction \(\theta = \mathrm{atan2}(I_y, I_x)\) — edges are perpendicular to \(\nabla I\). In the discrete setting, \(I_x, I_y\) are estimated by Sobel (Example 2.4) after Gaussian smoothing.
Example 3.2 — Gradient by hand Intensities of a neighborhood \(\begin{pmatrix} 52 & 54 & 55\\ 50 & 120 & 122 \\ 48 & 118 & 120\end{pmatrix}\) at the central point: with Sobel, \(I_x = \frac{(55 + 2\cdot122 + 120) - (52 + 2\cdot50 + 48)}{4} = \frac{419 - 200}{4} \approx 55\), \(I_y = \frac{(48 + 2\cdot118 + 120) - (52 + 2\cdot54 + 55)}{4} = \frac{404 - 215}{4} \approx 47\). Magnitude \(\sqrt{55^2+47^2} \approx 72\) levels/pixel, direction \(\theta \approx \mathrm{atan2}(47, 55) \approx 40.5^\circ\) — a strong, diagonal edge. A pixel from a homogeneous area would give \(\pm 5\) under the noise: magnitude thresholding separates signal from noise.
Algorithm 3.3 — The Canny detector
  1. Gaussian smoothing \(G_\sigma\) (\(\sigma\) sets the scale of the detected edges);
  2. gradient (Sobel) → magnitude and direction;
  3. non-maximum suppression: quantize \(\theta\) into 4 directions (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) and keep a pixel only if its magnitude exceeds that of its two neighbors in the direction of the gradient — an edge one pixel wide;
  4. hysteresis thresholding at two levels (\(s_{low} < s_{high}\)): a pixel ≥ \(s_{high}\) is a starting point; the edge is propagated to neighbors ≥ \(s_{low}\) — which reconnects edges interrupted by noise without multiplying the false positives a single low threshold would produce.
Two settings (\(\sigma\), thresholds) — and a remarkable property: good detection/localization trade-off, implemented in a handful of milliseconds (OpenCV: cv2.Canny(img, s_low, s_high)).
Note — Zero crossings of the Laplacian (LoG) The second order detects edges as the zeros of \(\Delta(G*I) = (\Delta G)*I\) — the "LoG" kernel, close to the "difference of Gaussians" of ch. 4. The zero crossing is finer than the gradient maximum but more sensitive to noise, and the sign on either side encodes the bright/dark transition. Marr and Hildreth made it a model of the first level of the visual cortex — the biological prehistory of the convolutional networks of ch. 9.
