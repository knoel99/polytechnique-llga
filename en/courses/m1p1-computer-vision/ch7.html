7. Optical flow: Lucas-Kanade

The apparent motion of luminances between two images — optical flow — is the ingredient of tracking, video compression, and navigation. Lucas-Kanade (1981) remains the reference formulation, and its mathematics is a condensed version of the course.

Definition 7.1 — Gradient constraint and the aperture problem Luminance conservation assumption: \(I(x + u, y + v, t + \Delta t) = I(x, y, t)\). First-order Taylor expansion: $$I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0 \qquad (\text{with } u = dx/dt,\; v = dy/dt,\; I_t = \partial I/\partial t),$$ a single equation for two unknowns \((u, v)\): the line of possible flows. Locally, an aperture (small window) does not suffice to determine the motion of an edge — this is the aperture problem, or the illusion of the wheel turning behind a slot.
Theorem 7.2 — Lucas-Kanade: least squares over a window Assuming the flow is constant in a window \(W\), each pixel provides a gradient constraint; the optimal flow minimizes $$J(u, v) = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} w(x,y)\,\big(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\big)^2.$$ Setting the gradient to zero (\(\partial J/\partial u = \partial J/\partial v = 0\)) yields the normal equations $$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sum w\,I_x^2 & \sum w\,I_xI_y\\ \sum w\,I_xI_y & \sum w\,I_y^2\end{pmatrix}}_{=\,M,\ \text{the structure tensor!}} \begin{pmatrix}u\\ v\end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix}\sum w\,I_xI_t\\ \sum w\,I_yI_t\end{pmatrix}.$$
Derivative with respect to \(u\): \(2\sum w\,I_x(I_xu + I_yv + I_t) = 0\) → first row; symmetrically for \(v\) → second row. The matrix \(M\) is exactly Harris's structure tensor (Def. 4.1): Lucas-Kanade is solvable precisely where Harris detects a corner — same large \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\). ∎
Domain of validity: small motions (Taylor), locally constant flow — hence the two remedies: (i) iterative resolution by successive warping; (ii) coarse-to-fine approach via a pyramid (Prop. 2.5): estimate large at the coarse scale, refine while going down.
Example 7.3 — A 2×2 system by hand Window of 4 pixels, gradients and time derivative \((I_x, I_y\,;\,I_t)\): (3, 1; 8), (5, 2; 10), (4, 2; 9), (6, 3; 12). Sums: \(M = \begin{pmatrix}9+25+16+36 & 3+10+8+18\\ \cdot & 1+4+4+9\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}86 & 39\\ 39 & 18\end{pmatrix}\), right-hand side \(-\big(\sum I_xI_t, \sum I_yI_t\big) = -(182, 82)\). Let us solve: \(\det M = 86\times18 - 39^2 = 1548 - 1521 = 27\) — nearly singular (eigenvalues ≈ 103.7 and 0.26: nearly aligned gradients). Cramer's formulas: \(u = \frac{-182\times18 + 82\times39}{27} = \frac{-78}{27} \approx -2.89\), \(v = \frac{86\times(-82) + 39\times182}{27} = \frac{46}{27} \approx 1.70\). The flow is computable but unreliable: \(\lambda_2 \ll \lambda_1\) signals an edge, not a corner — Example 4.2 gave the opposite diagnosis on another tensor. In practice: require \(\lambda_2 > \tau\) before trusting the flow (and weight by the distance to the centroid to smooth).
Note — Horn-Schunck, the global version Instead of a window, Horn-Schunck (1981) regularizes over the whole image: minimize \(\int (I_xu + I_yv + I_t)^2 + \lambda\,\big(\|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2\big)\): data attachment (gradient constraint) + a smooth flow field. The Euler-Lagrange equations are solved iteratively; \(\lambda\) tunes the data/regularity trade-off — the same energy structure as the graph cuts of ch. 8, and as many inverse problems of the Signal Processing module. Modern variant: learned networks supervised by these very energies.
