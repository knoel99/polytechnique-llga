2. Filtering and convolution

Filtering an image means replacing each pixel with a combination of its neighborhood: smoothing noise, sharpening details, detecting variations. The universal tool is convolution, already met in 1D in the Signal Processing module (ch. 2): everything that follows is its 2D translation.

Definition 2.1 — 2D convolution and correlation For an image \(I\) and a kernel \(K\) with finite support: $$(I * K)(x, y) = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} I(x-i,\,y-j)\,K(i, j).$$ Convolution flips the kernel; correlation \(\sum I(x+i, y+j) K(i,j)\) does not — for symmetric kernels (Gaussian, box), the two coincide. A convolutional filter is linear and translation-invariant: these are exactly the LTI systems of signal processing, and the convolution theorem applies: \(I * K \longleftrightarrow \hat I \cdot \hat K\) (2D Fourier transform).
Proposition 2.2 — Separability of the Gaussian The 2D Gaussian kernel factors: \(G_\sigma(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2\sigma^2} = g_\sigma(x)\,g_\sigma(y)\) with \(g_\sigma(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\). Convolving by \(G_\sigma\) therefore amounts to convolving first along rows then along columns.
The factorization is an algebraic identity: \(e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2\sigma^2} = e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\cdot e^{-y^2/2\sigma^2}\) and \(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\). The rest follows by associativity of convolution: \(I * G_\sigma = I * (g_\sigma^{col} * g_\sigma^{row}) = (I * g_\sigma^{col}) * g_\sigma^{row}\). ∎
Gain: a \(k\times k\) kernel costs \(k^2\) multiplications per pixel in 2D, \(2k\) in two passes; for \(k = 31\): 961 → 62, i.e. 15.5× fewer. In practice one samples \(g_\sigma\) over \([-3\sigma, 3\sigma]\) (99.7% of the mass) and normalizes the sum to 1.
Definition 2.3 — Derivatives and finite differences The discrete derivative is approximated by centered differences \(\frac{\partial I}{\partial x}(x, y) \approx \frac{I(x+1,y) - I(x-1,y)}{2}\), that is, a convolution by \(\frac12[-1, 0, 1]\). On a noisy image, differentiating amplifies the high-frequency noise; one smooths first: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(G_\sigma * I) = \Big(\frac{\partial G_\sigma}{\partial x}\Big) * I,$$ the equality coming from swapping derivative and convolution (Fubini; see the ch. 0 review and the Signal Processing module). Conclusion: differentiating a smoothed image = convolving by the derivative of a Gaussian — a single kernel, optimal in the sense of the chosen scale. This is the starting point of ch. 3.

Proposition 2.5 — Pyramids: subsampling without folding Halving the resolution without precaution folds the frequencies beyond the new Nyquist frequency (Def. 1.4). The Gaussian pyramid therefore applies \(G_{\sigma_0}\) (with \(\sigma_0 \geq \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) pixel) before keeping one pixel out of two; each level \(I^{(k+1)} = \downarrow_2 (G * I^{(k)})\) represents the image at scale \(2^k\). These pyramids speed up multi-scale search (ch. 4: SIFT, ch. 7: pyramidal Lucas-Kanade) and progressive rendering.
