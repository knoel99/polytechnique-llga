5. Rigid bodies and quaternions

Definition 5.1 — Rigid body A rigid body is a set of material points whose mutual distances are constant. Its configuration is an element of \(SE(3) = SO(3) \times \mathbb{R}^3\) (rotation + translation), its state \((\mathbf{c}, R, \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{L})\): center of mass, orientation, momentum \(\mathbf{P} = M\mathbf{c}\), angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\). The motion obeys $$\dot{\mathbf{P}} = \mathbf{F}_{ext}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{L}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ext} \ \ (\text{wrenches}), \qquad \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{V} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})$$ where \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) is the angular velocity and \(\mathbf{L} = I_c R\, \boldsymbol{\omega}\)... more precisely \(\mathbf{L} = R\, I_{body}\, R^\top \boldsymbol{\omega}\), with \(I_{body}\) the inertia matrix in the body frame (symmetric positive definite).
Proposition 5.2 — Why unit quaternions Parameterizing \(SO(3)\) by three Euler angles suffers from gimbal lock (loss of rank of the parameterization, e.g. at ±90° of pitch) and from poor-quality interpolations. Unit quaternions \(q = (w, \mathbf{u})\), \(w^2 + \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = 1\), form the group \(\mathrm{Sp}(1) \simeq SU(2)\), double cover of \(SO(3)\): the rotation of angle \(\theta\) around the unit axis \(\mathbf{n}\) is \(q = (\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\ \mathbf{n}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2})\), composition is the quaternion product, and \(q\) and \(-q\) give the same rotation (anticipation for the weighted summation of chapter 7). Integration uses \(\dot{q} = \tfrac12\, q \otimes (0, \boldsymbol{\omega})\), then renormalization (\(q \leftarrow q/\|q\|\)) — the symplectic approach applies here too.
Theorem 5.3 — Conservation of angular momentum For a system of points subjected to central internal forces (\(\mathbf{F}_{ij}\) collinear with \(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i\)) and with no external wrench, \(\mathbf{L}\) is constant: this is the "skater's physics".
\(\dot{\mathbf{L}} = \sum_i (\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{c}) \times \mathbf{F}_i\). Let us group the internal forces in pairs \((i, j)\), with \(\mathbf{F}_{ij} = -\mathbf{F}_{ji}\) collinear with \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i\) (action-reaction principle + central forces): \((\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{c}) \times \mathbf{F}_{ij} + (\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{c}) \times \mathbf{F}_{ji} = \big[(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{c}) - (\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{c})\big] \times \mathbf{F}_{ij} = -\mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{F}_{ij} = 0\) by collinearity. With no external wrench, \(\dot{\mathbf{L}} = 0\). ∎
Example 5.4 — Pirouette: the numbers A skater modeled as a cylinder: arms spread \(I_1 = 4.0\) kg·m², arms folded \(I_2 = 1.6\) kg·m². She starts at \(\omega_1 = 1\) turn/s: conservation of \(L\) ⟹ \(\omega_2 = (I_1/I_2)\,\omega_1 = 2.5\) turns/s. Kinetic energy \(E = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\) with \(L = I\omega\) ⟹ \(E = \tfrac{L^2}{2I}\): \(E_1 = L^2/8\), \(E_2 = L^2/3.2 = 2.5\,E_1\) — the energy increases: it is the muscular work of drawing the arms in (no contradiction with the conservation of \(L\)). For a physics engine: integrating \(L\) and then recovering \(\omega = (R I_b R^\top)^{-1} L\) avoids the rotational energy drifts of stacked rigid bodies.

Implementation detail: sleeping is stable Stacking boxes at rest is the nightmare of real-time engines: micro-bounces keep the system alive. Industrial standard: "sleeping" (freezing bodies whose average velocity remains below a threshold for \(n\) frames), contact margins (allowing slight interpenetration), and sub-stepping (several small steps per frame rather than one large one). The major architectures (PhysX, Jolt, Bullet) combine all of this; the algorithm remains that of chapter 6.
