4. Particles, forces, mass-spring systems

Definition 4.1 — Particle system A system of \(n\) particles is the state \((\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{v}_i)_{i=1}^n \in \mathbb{R}^{6n}\) endowed with a force field \(\mathbf{F}_i(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v})\). The dynamics \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_i = \mathbf{v}_i,\ m_i \dot{\mathbf{v}}_i = \mathbf{F}_i\) is integrated by one of the schemes of chapter 2. Usual forces: gravity \(m_i \mathbf{g}\); linear drag \(-c_d \mathbf{v}_i\) or quadratic \(-c_q \|\mathbf{v}_i\| \mathbf{v}_i\); spring \(\mathbf{F}_{i\leftarrow j} = k(\|\ell\| - r_{ij})\,\hat{\ell}\) with \(\ell = \mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i\), rest length \(r_{ij}\); axial damper \(-c\,(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i)\cdot \hat{\ell}\,\hat{\ell}\). This is the basic model of "magical" effects (fire, smoke, dust) and the forerunner of the SPH of chapter 11.
Proposition 4.2 — Eigenmodes of a mass-spring network For small displacements around rest, a network with masses \(M\) (diagonal) and stiffness matrix \(K\) (symmetric, positive definite if the network is anchored) linearizes to \(M\ddot{\mathbf{u}} = -K\mathbf{u}\). The natural frequencies are $$\omega_k^2 = \lambda_k(M^{-1}K), \qquad k = 1, \dots, 3n,$$ and the stability condition of symplectic Euler (Theorem 3.5) becomes \(h \leq 2/\omega_{\max} = 2/\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(M^{-1}K)}\).
Example 4.3 — Choosing the step for a garment A discrete cloth of \(20 \times 20\) points, mesh size \(d = 1\) cm, structural springs \(k_s = 50\) N/m, mass per point \(m = 2\) g. The fastest mode couples two neighboring points: \(\omega \approx \sqrt{2k_s/m} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 50 / 0.002} \approx 224\) rad/s, hence \(h \leq 2/\omega \approx 9\) ms (≈ 110 Hz). With "2-hop" bending springs five times stiffer, \(\omega\) is multiplied by \(\sqrt{5}\) and the allowed step divided accordingly — hence the value of the implicit approach. Remember the orders of magnitude: real-time garment simulation: \(h \sim 2\)–5 ms, implicit; effects particles: \(h \sim 16\)–33 ms, symplectic.
Definition 4.4 — Implicit: the system to solve The linearized implicit (or semi-implicit) step writes $$\Big(\,I - h\,J\,\Big)\,\Delta\mathbf{v} = h\,\big(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_n) + h\,J\,\mathbf{v}_n\big), \qquad J = \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \ \ \text{(force Jacobian,}\ \sphericalangle\ \text{negative definite system)},$$ then \(\mathbf{v}_{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_n + \Delta\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n + h\,\mathbf{v}_{n+1}\). In practice one solves by conjugate gradient without assembling \(J\) explicitly (matrix-vector products by looping over the springs) — the same trick of writing as the backpropagation gradient, cf. Machine Learning module, backpropagation chapter.
Beyond springs: spheres and density Interactional particle systems (SPH fluids, MPM sand) replace fixed links with finite-range interactions: each particle carries a density \(\rho_i = \sum_j m_j W(\|\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i\|)\) (kernel \(W\)), and the forces derive from a compression energy potential. The free surface is the isosurface \(\rho = \rho_0\) — this is the smoothed particles paradigm of Desbrun & Cani (1996), chapter 11: merging, splashing and object separation emerge without any imposed topology.
Definition 4.5 — Continuum models: elasticity and cloth Mass-spring networks imitate the continuum; continuum models discretize it. Linear elasticity: the strain energy \(\int_S \|\varepsilon\|^2 \, \mathrm{d}S\) (Hooke's law) is computed after cutting into elements — for surfaces, co-rotational triangles that remove the rigid rotation before measuring the strain; the gradient of the energy yields forces, integrated by the schemes of ch. 2. Cloth: the standard Baraff–Witkin (1998) model decouples stretching (stiff, penalized locally), shearing and bending (soft); a garment becomes a constrained mesh — collisions and friction with the body via ch. 6, and high stiffnesses that impose the implicit method (def. 4.4). A modern alternative: position-based dynamics — projecting the positions directly onto the inextensibility constraints: stable, fast, somewhat dissipative; this is the PBD/XPBD family of real-time engines (ch. 6).
Definition 4.6 — Fluids: particle (SPH) and grid (Eulerian) approaches Two main families, depending on what lives in memory:
  • Lagrangian — particles (SPH): the fluid is a particle system carrying mass and smoothed density; pressure and viscosity derive from kernels (cf. the Note above). Free of topology: splashes and separation for free; difficulty: the sampling degenerates under high pressures;
  • Eulerian — grids: the velocity lives on a fixed grid, the state \((\mathbf{u}, \rho)\) evolves by incompressible Navier–Stokes: advect (semi-Lagrangian, unconditionally stable), add the forces, then project onto divergence-free fields (a Poisson equation per step — the Hodge projection of Stable Fluids, Stam, 1999). The grid captures the vortices, but numerical diffusion kills them at small scales — hence the FLIP/PIC hybrids (particles for advection, grid for pressure) and MPM for granular materials (sand, snow — ch. 11).
Production choices: SPH for interactive splashes, grid and FLIP for movie fluids, MPM for soft materials: all three share the integration loop and the stability concerns of the rest of the chapter. 
import numpy as np

# ------------- Particle simulator: symplectic Euler -------------
# n particles, gravity, chain of springs, impenetrable ground (restitution 0.8)
def simulate(n=40, steps=1200, h=1/240, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 3)); x[:, 2] += 1.5   # initial positions
    v = np.zeros((n, 3))
    m = np.full(n, 0.05)                             # 50 g per particle
    g = np.array([0, 0, -9.81]); mu = 0.7            # gravity, linear drag
    # springs (i, j, stiffness k, damping c, rest length r)
    edges = [(i, i + 1, 80.0, 0.4, 0.15) for i in range(n - 1)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        F = m[:, None] * g[None, :]                  # gravity
        for (i, j, k, c, r) in edges:                # spring + damper
            d = x[j] - x[i]; L = np.linalg.norm(d); u = d / L
            Fij = (k * (L - r) + c * np.dot(v[j] - v[i], u)) * u
            F[i] += Fij; F[j] -= Fij
        v += h * F / m[:, None]     # (1) velocity first   -- symplectic Euler
        v *= (1 - mu * h)           # global linear drag
        x += h * v                  # (2) position next
        hit = x[:, 2] < 0           # ground z = 0: projection + bounce
        x[hit, 2] = 0
        v[hit, 2] = -0.8 * v[hit, 2]
        yield x.copy(), v.copy()

# mechanical energy: must remain bounded (cf. theorem 3.5)
for k, (x, v) in enumerate(simulate()):
    if k % 240 == 0:
        E = 0.5 * 0.05 * (v ** 2).sum() + 0.05 * 9.81 * x[:, 2].sum()
        print(f"t = {k/240:5.2f} s   E = {E:7.3f} J")
Reading the code Two details matter more than everything else: (i) the order of the "velocity then position" lines — swapping these two lines gives back explicit Euler and makes the chain explode (try it with \(k = 500\)); (ii) handling of the ground by projection (position corrected, velocity reflected) foreshadows the constraints of chapter 6: no contact spring, hence no added stiffness. The energy loop must oscillate boundedly — the most useful diagnostic of a physics engine.
