Introduction and overview

ModuleComputer Animation
CodeCSC_51085_EP
Team and workloadInstructors: Mathieu Desbrun & Marie-Paule Cani (Inria · École Polytechnique) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · from keyframes to simulation: numerical integration and stability, particles and mass-spring systems, rigid bodies, collisions, skinning, crowds — then the bridge toward learned animation (motion matching, neural networks).
Status in the programP1 optionOutside recommended track

Computer animation makes virtual worlds "live": characters, crowds, cloth, fluids. This module establishes its scientific foundations — from keyframes and splines to the physical simulation of particles, mass-spring systems and rigid bodies — then builds a bridge toward learned animation (motion matching, neural networks). Within the LLGA master's program, it holds a singular place: it is the terrain where physics-based simulation meets machine learning, under the guidance of two major figures of the field, Mathieu Desbrun and Marie-Paule Cani.

What you will be able to do

  • Build a complete animation pipeline, from keyframe posing to spline interpolation.
  • Choose a numerical integration scheme for an ODE and predict its stability thanks to the oscillator theorem.
  • Model and simulate physical systems: particles, forces, mass-spring systems.
  • Represent the orientation of rigid bodies with quaternions and integrate them correctly.
  • Handle collisions and constraints in a stable simulation loop.
  • Implement linear blend skinning (LBS) and diagnose its artifacts.
  • Write an inverse kinematics solver and simulate crowd behaviors.
  • Compare procedural animation and data-driven animation, and situate neural-network approaches.

Where this course fits in the program

Module of period 1 of M1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS). These materials present it as an option outside the recommended track; the MSc&T brochure counts it among the period's courses. It is taken at the very beginning of the year, with no prerequisites among the other modules of the master's program.

  • Prerequisites — the programming and algorithmic fluency of the Refresher in Computer Science, as well as solid foundations in linear algebra (vectors, matrices, rotations); the refresher chapter puts ODEs, Taylor expansions and energies back in place.
  • Downstream modules — the meshes manipulated here are studied in depth in Digital Representation and Analysis of Shapes (another geometric module of P1); and physical simulation becomes a learning building block in Analysis and Deep Learning on Geometric Data (M2), the natural continuation of the neural-network animation discussed at the end of the course.

Course outline

  • Refresher: ODEs, Taylor, rotations, energy — the simulator's mathematical toolbox, reactivated from the very first section.
  • The animation pipeline: keyframes and splines — how to interpolate poses cleanly between keyframes.
  • Numerical integration of ODEs — discretize time without destroying the motion.
  • Stability: the oscillator theorem — why explicit Euler blows up and how to choose a stable scheme.
  • Particles, forces, mass-spring systems — the first complete physical system you will simulate.
  • Rigid bodies and quaternions — representing and integrating rotations without watching them degenerate.
  • Collisions and constraints — making objects interact with each other and with the world.
  • Skinning: LBS and its artifacts — attaching a deformable skin to an animated skeleton.
  • Inverse kinematics — automatically computing the poses of a character reaching a target.
  • Crowds and behaviors — animating hundreds of agents with simple rules.
  • Procedural versus data-driven — the modern debate: physical rules versus learned models (motion matching, neural networks).
  • Research of the teaching team — a tour of Desbrun's and Cani's research work related to the course.
  • Exercises and References — systematic training and sources to go further.

How to study this module

  • First consolidate the refresher chapter: ODEs, Taylor and rotations come back in each of the following sections.
  • Program small simulators (bouncing ball, mass-spring system) rather than only reading: stability is understood hands-on.
  • Vary the time step and the stiffness of the springs to observe for yourselves the instabilities that theory predicts.
  • Redo the exercises of each section before moving on to the next: they directly prepare the graded assignments.
  • Keep in mind the guiding question: which part of this simulator could a neural network learn, and at what cost?
  • Explore the research of the teaching team and the references if you are aiming for an internship or a PhD in animation and geometry.
The LLGA positioning This module embodies a crossover that the master's program deliberately cultivates: the virtual worlds simulated here become training environments for agents, and learned animation builds on the neural networks seen in P1. It is a gateway toward careers in video games, special effects and industrial simulation, as well as toward research in geometric deep learning extended in M2 by Analysis and Deep Learning on Geometric Data.
