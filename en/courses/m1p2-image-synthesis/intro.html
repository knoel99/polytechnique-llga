Introduction and overview

This module covers realistic image synthesis end to end: the physics of light formalized by the rendering equation, its solution by Monte Carlo path tracing, its accelerations and noise handling, up to neural rendering (NeRF, 3D Gaussians) and real time. It is one of the rare places in the curriculum where probability, integral calculus and GPU systems join hands to produce images. The course comes with a complete Python path tracer, to study, run and extend.

What you will be able to do

  • Handle radiometric quantities, first and foremost radiance.
  • State the rendering equation and explain why solving it exactly is out of reach.
  • Choose a material model (BRDF) according to the intended effect.
  • Model a camera with the pinhole model and connect pixels and rays.
  • Compare rasterization and ray tracing, their trade-offs and their hybrids.
  • Write a Monte Carlo path tracer and accelerate it with intersection structures (BVHs).
  • Handle noise, denoising and tone mapping to go from physical values to a displayable image.
  • Situate NeRF and 3D Gaussians in the rendering landscape, and the challenges of real time.

Official objectives and syllabus (2026-2027 catalog)

The official objective of the course unit is to make students able to develop complete interactive 3D rendering systems, and to give a detailed understanding of the simulation of light transport and of the formation of digital images from a 3D scene. The official description covers the digital models of shape, appearance, lighting and sensors of a scene, the rendering equation and the standard models of illumination, shading and reflectance, the rendering algorithms (projective rasterization, ray tracing), real time, GPU programming and hierarchical spatial structures, with an opening towards global illumination. The course has a strong practical dimension: the models and algorithms are implemented throughout the term (officially in C++ with the OpenGL API, technical notions reviewed in class; this material uses a Python path tracer as its guiding thread). Official prerequisites: algorithmics and data structures, calculus, programming (C or C++), notions of linear algebra.

Where this course fits in the program

An option of period 2 of M1, outside the recommended track. Three P1 courses usefully feed into it: Digital Representation and Analysis of Shapes for the representation of 3D surfaces, Signal Processing for filtering and frequency analysis, and above all Probability Theory for ML: Monte Carlo and Generative Models, whose Monte Carlo methods are the heart of path tracing. Official prerequisites: algorithmics and data structures, calculus, programming in C or C++, notions of linear algebra — the module's Python path tracer is their application ground, the official implementation of the course being in C++/OpenGL. Downstream, neural rendering (NeRF, 3D Gaussians) prepares Multimodal Generative AI of the same period, and mastering generative image models feeds Control in Generative AI (M2).

Course outline

  • Background: probability, integrals, linear ODEs — the mathematics of rendering, put back in hand.
  • Radiometry: radiance — the physical quantities that describe light, and the one that will carry everything.
  • The rendering equation — the integral formulation that grounds the whole rest of the module.
  • BRDFs and material models — how a surface responds to light, from diffuse to specular.
  • Cameras: the pinhole model — forming an image: projection, pixels and rays.
  • Rasterization versus ray tracing — the two great families of rendering algorithms and their trade-offs.
  • Monte Carlo path tracing — solving the rendering equation by random sampling.
  • Acceleration: intersection and BVHs — making ray-scene intersection computations affordable.
  • Noise, denoising, tone mapping — going from noisy physical values to a displayable final image.
  • Neural rendering: NeRF and 3D Gaussians — when networks learn the scene and its rendering.
  • Real time: GPU, hybrid, upscaling — the strategies that bring realism closer to interactive budgets.
  • Research of the instructor — the research papers of Tamy Boubekeur, read with the tools of the module.
  • A complete path tracer in Python — the companion code of the course, to read, run and extend.
  • Exercises — radiometric computations and guided extensions of the path tracer.
  • References — the reference books and papers of rendering.

How to study this module

  • Run the Python path tracer from the first chapters on: every concept of the course has its line of code there.
  • Recode the building blocks in small steps — ray, sphere, then direct lighting — rather than attacking everything at once.
  • Draw the integrands: the rendering equation is understood on a sketch of light bouncing around.
  • Connect the noise in your images to the P1 probability course: variance, sampling, convergence.
  • Test each idea on minimal scenes — one sphere, two lights — before scaling up in complexity.
  • Keep the full pipeline in mind: radiometry, rendering, denoising, tone mapping — an upstream error is paid downstream.
The module in the LLGA master Rendering is the master's generative physics: it provides generative models with a quantitative foundation — light, cameras, integrals — which reappears in the neural rendering of the generation modules and in the control of generative models in M2. The targeted skills open onto careers in computer-generated imagery, real time and computer graphics research.
