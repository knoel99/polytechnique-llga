3. BRDFs and material models

Definition 3.1 — BRDF The bidirectional reflectance distribution function \(f_r(x,\ \omega_i \to \omega_o)\) relates incident and outgoing radiance: $$L_o(x, \omega_o) = \int_{\Omega^+} L_i(\omega_i)\, f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i \quad (\text{units } \mathrm{sr}^{-1}).$$ Two physical properties: (i) Helmholtz reciprocity: \(f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o) = f_r(\omega_o \to \omega_i)\); (ii) energy conservation: \( \int f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o) \cos\theta_o\, d\omega_o \leq 1\) for every \(\omega_i\) (the albedo of chapter 2), and \(f_r \geq 0\).
Theorem 3.2 — The Lambertian model is \(f_r = \rho/\pi\) A perfectly diffuse surface (identical outgoing radiance in all directions) with albedo \(\rho \in [0, 1]\) has the constant BRDF $$f_r = \frac{\rho}{\pi}.$$
By assumption \(L_o\) is constant; the exitance equals \(M = \int_{\Omega^+} L_o \cos\theta_o\, d\omega_o = L_o \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi = L_o \pi\). The received irradiance is \(E = \int L_i \cos\theta_i d\omega_i\), and \(L_o = f_r E\). The definition of albedo (\(M = \rho E\)) gives \(\pi f_r E = \rho E\), hence \(f_r = \rho/\pi\). The factor \(\pi\) disappears: integrating a constant against \(\cos\theta\) over the hemisphere produces \(\pi\) — it will return in the cosine sampling of chapter 6. ∎
Definition 3.3 — Phong, Blinn-Phong, microfacets
  • Phong (1975): \(f_r = \tfrac{\rho_d}{\pi} + k_s\, \dfrac{n+2}{2\pi}\, \cos^n\alpha\) with \(\alpha\) the angle to the mirror direction — the empirical specular; Blinn-Phong replaces \(\alpha\) by the angle to the half vector \(\mathbf{h} = \mathrm{norm}(\omega_i + \omega_o)\);
  • Microfacets (Cook–Torrance, 1982): the surface is a field of micro-mirrors; $$f_r = \frac{D(\mathbf{h})\, G(\omega_i, \omega_o)\, F(\omega_i)} {4\, \cos\theta_i\, \cos\theta_o},$$ with \(D\) the distribution of normals (GGX/Trowbridge-Reitz: heavy tails = realistic "blurry" reflections), \(G\) a masking-shadowing factor (Smith) and \(F\) the Fresnel coefficient. This is the family of standard PBR materials (film and games); layered models (Disney/Burley 2012) add a "sheen" component and a retroreflection lobe to it.
Example 3.4 — Schlick's Fresnel, in numbers Schlick's approximation: \(F(\theta) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \cos\theta)^5\), with \(F_0\) the reflectance at normal incidence. Typical dielectric (\(F_0 = 0.04\)): \(F(0°) = 0.04\), \(F(45°) \approx 0.05\), \(F(60°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.5^5 \approx 0.07\), \(F(85°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times (1 - 0.087)^5 \approx 0.65\). Physical reading: all surfaces become mirrors at grazing incidence — hence the shiny edges of water or varnish. For gold (\(F_0 \approx (1.00,\ 0.71,\ 0.29)\)), even at normal incidence the reflected light is already tinted: the metal "colors" its reflection, a dielectric does not.

*A subtlety that became a quality criterion The "classical" Phong specular lobe (around the mirror direction of \(\omega_o\)) is not symmetric in \((\omega_i, \omega_o)\); the variant around the half vector \(\mathbf{h}\) is. Modern engines require reciprocity and conservation: they guarantee that swapping the light/camera positions does not change the image — indispensable for path tracing (paths are traced backwards, from the eye toward the light!). A non-reciprocal BRDF makes inverse rendering physically inconsistent.
