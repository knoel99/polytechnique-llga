This module covers realistic image synthesis end to end: the physics of light formalized by the rendering equation, its solution by Monte Carlo path tracing, its accelerations and noise handling, up to neural rendering (NeRF, 3D Gaussians) and real time. It is one of the rare places in the curriculum where probability, integral calculus and GPU systems join hands to produce images. The course comes with a complete Python path tracer, to study, run and extend.

What you will be able to do

  • Handle radiometric quantities, first and foremost radiance.
  • State the rendering equation and explain why solving it exactly is out of reach.
  • Choose a material model (BRDF) according to the intended effect.
  • Model a camera with the pinhole model and connect pixels and rays.
  • Compare rasterization and ray tracing, their trade-offs and their hybrids.
  • Write a Monte Carlo path tracer and accelerate it with intersection structures (BVHs).
  • Handle noise, denoising and tone mapping to go from physical values to a displayable image.
  • Situate NeRF and 3D Gaussians in the rendering landscape, and the challenges of real time.

Official objectives and syllabus (2026-2027 catalog)

The official objective of the course unit is to make students able to develop complete interactive 3D rendering systems, and to give a detailed understanding of the simulation of light transport and of the formation of digital images from a 3D scene. 0. Background: probability, integrals, linear ODEs

L3 background mobilized
  • Expectation and variance: linearity of expectation, \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2\), variance of a mean of \(N\) i.i.d. variables divided by \(N\) — all the noise of Monte Carlo rendering (chapter 6) and its denoising (chapter 8) are in these three lines;
  • Law of large numbers: \(\bar{X}_N \to \mathbb{E}X\) almost surely — the convergence of path tracing; central limit theorem: the typical error decreases as \(\sigma/\sqrt{N}\);
  • Multiple integrals, spherical coordinates, solid angle \(d\omega = \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi\), area of the unit hemisphere \(2\pi\) — chapter 1 lives on the sphere;
  • Change of variables for a density: if \(X \sim p_X\) and \(Y = g(X)\), then \(p_Y(y) = p_X(g^{-1}(y))\, |\det Dg^{-1}(y)|\) — the sampling of light (chapter 6);
  • First-order linear ODE: \(y' + a(t)\,y = b(t)\) is solved with the integrating factor \(y(t) = e^{-\int a}\big(y_0 + \int b e^{\int a}\big)\) — the transmittance of chapter 9;
  • Algebra: dot and cross products, orthonormal bases, change-of-basis matrices — the camera (chapter 4).
Proposition 0.1 — The formula to remember from chapter 6 To estimate \(I = \int_D f(x)\,dx\), sample \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) i.i.d. with density \(p\) on \(D\) (with \(p > 0\) where \(f \neq 0\)) and set $$\hat{I}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{f(X_k)}{p(X_k)}.$$ Then \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{I}_N] = I\) (unbiased estimator) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{I}_N) = \mathrm{Var}(f(X)/p(X))/N\). Monte Carlo rendering = choosing \(D\) (the light paths), \(f\) (the radiative contribution) and \(p\) (the sampling strategy) — and all the art lies in \(p\).

1. Radiometry: radiance

Definition 1.1 — Flux, irradiance, exitance, radiance
  • Flux (power): \(\Phi\), in watts — the light energy per second;
  • Irradiance \(E(x)\) in W/m²: flux received per unit area at \(x\); exitance \(M\): flux emitted per unit area;
  • Solid angle: \(\omega = A/r^2\) (surface cut out on the unit sphere), in steradians; the hemisphere: \(2\pi\) sr, the sphere: \(4\pi\) sr;
  • Radiance \(L(x, \omega)\) in W/(m²·sr): the flux per unit area perpendicular to the direction and per unit solid angle, $$d^2\Phi = L(x, \omega)\,\cos\theta\, dA\, d\omega,$$ where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\omega\) and the normal at \(x\). This is the quantity of the module: sensors (pixels, the eye) respond to radiance.
Theorem 1.2 — Invariance of radiance along a ray In a vacuum (no absorption, no emission), radiance is constant along a ray: for two points \(x_1, x_2\) that "see" each other, $$L(x_1 \to x_2) = L(x_2 \to x_1).$$
Consider the beam delimited by two small surfaces \(dA_1\) (around \(x_1\)) and \(dA_2\) (around \(x_2\)), at distance \(r\). The flux crossing this beam can be written on both sides: \(d^2\Phi = L_1 \cos\theta_1\, dA_1\, d\omega_1 = L_2 \cos\theta_2\, dA_2\, d\omega_2\), and the solid angle under which \(dA_2\) is seen from \(x_1\) equals \(d\omega_1 = \cos\theta_2\, dA_2 / r^2\) (and symmetrically \(d\omega_2 = \cos\theta_1\, dA_1 / r^2\)). Substituting: $$L_1 \cos\theta_1 dA_1 \frac{\cos\theta_2 dA_2}{r^2} = L_2 \cos\theta_2 dA_2 \frac{\cos\theta_1 dA_1}{r^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; L_1 = L_2.$$ Conservation of flux in the beam forces the equality — all the geometric factors cancel out exactly. ∎
Example 1.3 — A light bulb, round numbers An isotropic "bulb" of flux \(\Phi = 800\) lm (≈ 1.2 W radiometric at 555 nm) at 2 m from a wall. Flux in a cone of aperture \(d\omega = 0.01\) sr directed at the wall: \(d\Phi = \Phi\, d\omega/(4\pi) \approx 0.93\) mW. Illuminated patch: \(dA = r^2\, d\omega/\cos\theta = 0.04\) m² (at normal incidence), hence irradiance \(E = d\Phi/dA \approx 23\) mW/m². At 4 m: same computation, \(dA\) multiplied by 4, \(E\) divided by 4 (\(\approx 5.8\) mW/m²) — the famous \(1/r^2\) law is not a law of radiance (constant!) but of beam geometry: the pixels see the same radiance, the projected surface of the source decays as \(1/r^2\).
Why so many units? Photometry (lumens, candelas, lux) repeats radiometry (watts, W/sr, W/m²) weighted by the eye's sensitivity (the function \(V(\lambda)\), peak at 555 nm where 1 W = 683 lm). Physical rendering computes in spectral radiance W/(m²·sr·nm) then converts to RGB by integration against the response functions of the three sensors — the "white" of an image is only a convention of these integrals (chapter 8: tone mapping).

2. The rendering equation

Definition 2.1 — The light transport equation (LTE) At a surface point \(x\) with normal \(\mathbf{n}\), the outgoing radiance \(L_o(x, \omega_o)\) in a direction \(\omega_o\) of the hemisphere equals (Kajiya, 1986): $$L_o(x, \omega_o) = L_e(x, \omega_o) + \int_{\Omega^+} L_i(x, \omega_i)\, f_r(x, \omega_i \to \omega_o)\, \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i,$$ with \(L_e\) the self-emission, \(L_i\) the incident radiance, \(f_r\) the BRDF (chapter 3) and \(\cos\theta_i = \omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}\) the projection factor. Writing \(L_i(x, \omega) = L_o(r(x, -\omega), \omega)\) (the ray traced backwards), the equation becomes an integral over surfaces: it is a Fredholm equation of the second kind, $$L = L_e + T\,L, \qquad T\ \text{the transport operator (one bounce)}.$$
Theorem 2.2 — Bounce series (Neumann) and convergence If the materials have bounded albedo \(\rho = \sup \int_{\Omega^+} f_r \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i < 1\), then \(T\) is a contraction in the sup norm and the unique solution of the LTE is the series $$L = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} T^k L_e = \underbrace{L_e}_{\text{emission}} + \underbrace{T L_e}_{1^{\text{st}}\ \text{bounce}} + \underbrace{T^2 L_e}_{2^{\text{nd}}\ \text{bounce}} + \dots$$ with truncation error \(\|T^{k+1} L\|_\infty \leq \rho^{k+1} \|L\|_\infty\) — geometric decay of the weight of the bounces.
For two functions \(f, g\) on rays, \(\|T f\|_\infty \leq \sup_x \int_{\Omega^+} f_r \cos\theta_i d\omega_i \cdot \|f\|_\infty \leq \rho \|f\|_\infty\). By induction \(\|T^k L_e\|_\infty \leq \rho^k \|L_e\|_\infty\): the series converges in norm (geometric series of ratio \(\rho < 1\)), and \(L = \sum T^k L_e\) satisfies \(L = L_e + T L\) by summing. Uniqueness follows from contractivity: if \(L, L'\) are solutions, \(\|L - L'\|_\infty = \|T(L - L')\|_\infty \leq \rho \|L - L'\|_\infty\) forces \(L = L'\). ∎
Example 2.3 — Two surfaces face to face: the series in action Two diffuse planes of albedo \(\rho = 0.5\), one emitting \(L_e = 1\) toward the other. Radiance leaving the receiving plane: \(0.5\) (1st bounce) + \(0.25\) (2nd: one extra round trip) + \(0.125 + \dots = 1\). With \(\rho = 0.9\) (white paint): \(0.9 + 0.81 + \dots\) — the 10th bounce still carries \(0.9^{10} \approx 35\)% of the emission: interreflections dominate direct lighting in a white room (a white alcove looks brighter than the wall alone — this is the "color bleeding" of global illumination).
Alternative forms Depending on the context the LTE is rewritten: as a surface integral (integrate \(L_i\) over all visible surfaces, with the visibility factor \(V(x \leftrightarrow y)\) and the geometry \(G = \cos\theta_x \cos\theta_y / \|x - y\|^2\)) — useful for sampling emissive surfaces (next event estimation, chapter 6); or as a flight equation (volume rendering, chapter 9: the emission-scattering term \(\sigma_s \int p\, L\) replaces the BRDF). Changing the formulation = changing the sampling strategy.

3. BRDFs and material models

Definition 3.1 — BRDF The bidirectional reflectance distribution function \(f_r(x,\ \omega_i \to \omega_o)\) relates incident and outgoing radiance: $$L_o(x, \omega_o) = \int_{\Omega^+} L_i(\omega_i)\, f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, \cos\theta_i\, d\omega_i \quad (\text{units } \mathrm{sr}^{-1}).$$ Two physical properties: (i) Helmholtz reciprocity: \(f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o) = f_r(\omega_o \to \omega_i)\); (ii) energy conservation: \( \int f_r(\omega_i \to \omega_o) \cos\theta_o\, d\omega_o \leq 1\) for every \(\omega_i\) (the albedo of chapter 2), and \(f_r \geq 0\).
Theorem 3.2 — The Lambertian model is \(f_r = \rho/\pi\) A perfectly diffuse surface (identical outgoing radiance in all directions) with albedo \(\rho \in [0, 1]\) has the constant BRDF $$f_r = \frac{\rho}{\pi}.$$
By assumption \(L_o\) is constant; the exitance equals \(M = \int_{\Omega^+} L_o \cos\theta_o\, d\omega_o = L_o \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi = L_o \pi\). The received irradiance is \(E = \int L_i \cos\theta_i d\omega_i\), and \(L_o = f_r E\). The definition of albedo (\(M = \rho E\)) gives \(\pi f_r E = \rho E\), hence \(f_r = \rho/\pi\). The factor \(\pi\) disappears: integrating a constant against \(\cos\theta\) over the hemisphere produces \(\pi\) — it will return in the cosine sampling of chapter 6. ∎
Definition 3.3 — Phong, Blinn-Phong, microfacets
  • Phong (1975): \(f_r = \tfrac{\rho_d}{\pi} + k_s\, \dfrac{n+2}{2\pi}\, \cos^n\alpha\) with \(\alpha\) the angle to the mirror direction — the empirical specular; Blinn-Phong replaces \(\alpha\) by the angle to the half vector \(\mathbf{h} = \mathrm{norm}(\omega_i + \omega_o)\);
  • Microfacets (Cook–Torrance, 1982): the surface is a field of micro-mirrors; $$f_r = \frac{D(\mathbf{h})\, G(\omega_i, \omega_o)\, F(\omega_i)} {4\, \cos\theta_i\, \cos\theta_o},$$ with \(D\) the distribution of normals (GGX/Trowbridge-Reitz: heavy tails = realistic "blurry" reflections), \(G\) a masking-shadowing factor (Smith) and \(F\) the Fresnel coefficient. This is the family of standard PBR materials (film and games); layered models (Disney/Burley 2012) add a "sheen" component and a retroreflection lobe to it.
Example 3.4 — Schlick's Fresnel, in numbers Schlick's approximation: \(F(\theta) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \cos\theta)^5\), with \(F_0\) the reflectance at normal incidence. Typical dielectric (\(F_0 = 0.04\)): \(F(0°) = 0.04\), \(F(45°) \approx 0.05\), \(F(60°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.5^5 \approx 0.07\), \(F(85°) = 0.04 + 0.96 \times (1 - 0.087)^5 \approx 0.65\). Physical reading: all surfaces become mirrors at grazing incidence — hence the shiny edges of water or varnish. For gold (\(F_0 \approx (1.00,\ 0.71,\ 0.29)\)), even at normal incidence the reflected light is already tinted: the metal "colors" its reflection, a dielectric does not.

*A subtlety that became a quality criterion The "classical" Phong specular lobe (around the mirror direction of \(\omega_o\)) is not symmetric in \((\omega_i, \omega_o)\); the variant around the half vector \(\mathbf{h}\) is. Modern engines require reciprocity and conservation: they guarantee that swapping the light/camera positions does not change the image — indispensable for path tracing (paths are traced backwards, from the eye toward the light!). A non-reciprocal BRDF makes inverse rendering physically inconsistent.

4. Cameras: the pinhole model

Definition 4.1 — The pinhole An ideal camera is a point \(O\) (optical center) and an image plane at distance \(f\) (focal length). Every ray through \(O\) contributes: the pixel corresponding to direction \(\omega\) receives the radiance \(L(\omega)\). In camera coordinates (\(z\) forward), the projection reads $$\pi(x, y, z) = \Big(\, f\,\frac{x}{z},\ \ f\,\frac{y}{z} \,\Big), \qquad K = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$ \(K\) gathers focal lengths in pixels \(f_x = f/\delta_x\) (pixel size \(\delta\)) and optical center \((c_x, c_y)\) (principal point); the camera pose (extrinsics) is the world \(\to\) camera change-of-basis matrix \( [R\,|\,t]\). Ray generation: \(\mathbf{d} = \mathrm{norm}\big(R^\top K^{-1} (u, v, 1)^\top\big)\), origin \(O\) — the inverse loop of the projection.
Theorem 4.2 — A central projection maps lines to lines The image under a pinhole of any 3D line not passing through \(O\) is a line (or a point if the line passes through \(O\)).
Let \(\ell\) be a 3D line. The plane \(\Pi = \mathrm{span}(O, \ell)\) (plane containing \(O\) and \(\ell\)) exists as soon as \(\ell\) does not pass through \(O\). Every ray from a point of \(\ell\) is included in \(\Pi\); the image of \(\ell\) — the set of intersections of these rays with the image plane — is therefore included in \(\Pi \cap \text{image plane}\), the intersection of two distinct planes: a line. ∎
Example 4.3 — Projecting then pixelizing 640×480 sensor, square pixels of 50 µm ⟹ \(f_x = f_y = f/\delta\); for \(f = 50\) mm: \(f_x = 1000\) px, \(c = (320, 240)\). Camera point \((x, y, z) = (1, 2, 5)\) m: projection \((f x/z,\ f y/z) = (10,\,20)\) mm, then pixels, in image convention (\(y\) downward): \(u = f_x\, x/z + c_x = 200 + 320 = 520\), \(v = c_y - f_y\, y/z = 240 - 400 = -160\) — the point is out of the vertical field: the vertical field of view equals \(2\arctan(H/(2 f_x)) = 2 \arctan(480/2000) \approx 27°\) (half-field 13.5°), whereas the direction of the point rises \(\arctan(2/5) \approx 22°\) above the axis — well beyond the top edge. Remember: short focal = wide field = strong perspective; long focal = narrow field = compressed "telephoto" perspectives.
Definition 4.4 — Beyond the pinhole
  • Thin lens: aperture of radius \(r\) and focus at distance \(D\); out-of-focus points form on the sensor a disk (circle of confusion) of size \(\propto r\,|D - z|/z\) — the bokeh; in rendering: sample the aperture (lens) and the focus, then average;
  • Distortions: the pinhole + lens model is linear in homogeneous coordinates; real lenses add radial distortion (\(r \mapsto r + k_1 r^3 + \dots\)) — the "fish-eye" model of the vision module;
  • Exposure: the sensor integrates radiance for a duration \(\Delta t\) at a given aperture; the (aperture, time) pair does not change the scene's radiance, only the measured signal — hence rendering in high dynamic range values (HDR, chapter 8).

5. Rasterization versus ray tracing

Definition 5.1 — The two orders of traversal
  • Rasterization (object order): for each triangle, determine the covered pixels and write the framebuffer; complexity \(O(\sum_i A_i)\) (area of the triangles), independent of lighting complexity; native GPU hardware;
  • Ray tracing (image order): for each pixel, generate a ray (chapter 4) and find the first intersection (chapter 7); complexity \(O(N_{pixels} \cdot \log N_{triangles})\) with an acceleration structure; secondary rays (shadows, reflections) come naturally — the decisive argument for global illumination.
Theorem 5.2 — Edge functions and barycentric coordinates In the image plane, define the edge function from \(a\) to \(b\) at \(p\): \(E_{ab}(p) = (b_x - a_x)(p_y - a_y) - (b_y - a_y) (p_x - a_x)\) (2D cross product). Then \(p\) is inside triangle \((a, b, c)\) iff \(E_{ab}, E_{bc}, E_{ca}\) have the same sign at \(p\), and the barycentric coordinates are the area ratios $$\lambda_a = \frac{E_{bc}(p)}{E_{bc}(a)},\qquad \lambda_b = \frac{E_{ca}(p)}{E_{ca}(b)},\qquad \lambda_c = \frac{E_{ab}(p)}{E_{ab}(c)},\qquad \lambda_a + \lambda_b + \lambda_c = 1.$$ Moreover \(E_{ab}\) is affine in \(p\) ⟹ incremental update: \(E_{ab}(p + (1, 0)) = E_{ab}(p) + (b_y - a_y)\) — two additions per pixel.
\(E_{ab}(p)\) is (up to sign) the area of the parallelogram spanned by \((b - a)\) and \((p - a)\), hence the oriented area of triangle \((a, b, p)\). The point \(p\) is on the same side of \((ab)\) as \(c\) iff \(E_{ab}(p)\) and \(E_{ab}(c)\) have the same sign; the three conditions together characterize the interior of the triangle (convexity). For the barycentrics: \(p \mapsto E_{bc}(p)/E_{bc}(a)\) is affine, equals 1 at \(a\) and 0 at \(b\) and \(c\) (bases of \((a,b,c)\)); it is therefore \(\lambda_a\), and the sum equals 1 because it equals 1 at the three vertices (uniqueness of barycentric interpolation). Affinity gives the increment by differentiation: the gradient of \(E_{ab}\) is \((-(b_y - a_y),\ b_x - a_x)\). ∎
Definition 5.3 — Z-buffer and visibility Rasterization resolves visibility with the z-buffer: a depth buffer initialized to \(+\infty\); for each fragment of depth \(z_p\) (interpolated), write the color only if \(z_p < z_{buffer}\). Memory cost: one float per pixel; independent of the drawing order (unlike painter's sorting) — but ambiguous at equal depths (z-fighting: add a polygonal bias). In ray tracing, visibility is resolved by the nearest intersection — the z-buffer is useless there: the two families also differ on where visibility is computed (per fragment versus per ray).
Example 5.4 — A triangle over pixels, by hand Projected vertices \(a = (1, 1)\), \(b = (5, 1)\), \(c = (1, 4)\) (pixel coordinates). Edge functions: \(E_{ab}(p) = (b - a) \times (p - a) = 4(p_y - 1)\); \(E_{bc}(p) = (c - b) \times (p - b) = -4(p_y - 1) - 3(p_x - 5)\); \(E_{ca}(p) = 3(1 - p_x)\). Test of the pixel with center \((1, 2)\): \(E_{ab} = 4\), \(E_{bc} = 8\), \(E_{ca} = 0\) — on edge \([a, c]\), inside by the "top-left" rule. Barycentrics: \(\lambda_a = E_{bc}(p)/E_{bc}(a) = 8/12 = \tfrac23\), \(\lambda_b = E_{ca}(p)/E_{ca}(b) = 0\), \(\lambda_c = E_{ab}(p)/E_{ab}(c) = 4/12 = \tfrac13\) — consistent: \((1, 2)\) is one third of the way along edge \([a, c]\). The pixel \((2, 2)\): \(E_{ab} = 4\), \(E_{bc} = 1\), \(E_{ca} = -3\) — mixed signs, outside. The same computation, repeated and made incremental (the gradient of \(E_{ab}\) is constant), renders tens of millions of triangles per second: rasterization is the arithmetic of finite differences.
Whitted and the synthesis of the two worlds Whitted's (1980) "ray tracing" follows mirror and refraction rays and then stops: perfect reflections, hard shadows — already out of reach for pure rasterization. Current engines are hybrid: rasterization for primary visibility (fast, memory-coherent), rays on demand for soft shadows, reflections, approximate global illumination (chapter 10); path tracing (chapter 6) remains the reference for architectural rendering and animated film — when render time is counted in hours per frame.

6. Monte Carlo path tracing

Definition 6.1 — The rendering estimator Path tracing estimates the LTE (chapter 2) by sampling paths \(\mathbf{x} = (x_0, \dots, x_k)\) from the eye toward the sources, with density \(p(\mathbf{x})\), via $$\hat{L} = \frac{L_e\, f_r\, \cos\theta\, f_r \cos\theta \cdots}{p(\mathbf{x})} \quad \text{(product of transport factors / probability of the path)}.$$ A path is built bounce after bounce: at each bounce, draw an outgoing direction according to a density \(p(\omega)\) (hemispherical uniform, or proportional to \(\cos\theta/\pi\) — cosine sampling), accumulate the contributions, terminate with Russian roulette (Definition 6.4). It is an unbiased estimator of the complete bounce series — in contrast with deterministic methods (radiosity, photon mapping: biased but less noisy).
Theorem 6.2 — Unbiased, and variance in \(\sigma/\sqrt{N}\) (i) \(\mathbb{E}\big[f(X)/p(X)\big] = \int_D f\) as soon as \(p > 0\) on the support of \(f\). (ii) For \(N\) i.i.d. samples, the mean estimator \(\hat{I}_N\) satisfies \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{I}_N) = \mathrm{Var}(f(X)/p(X))/N\) and \(\hat{I}_N \to I\) almost surely. To divide the typical error by 2, you need 4 times more samples — the curse of Monte Carlo (and its salvation: the \(-1/2\) exponent is independent of the dimension, hence the triumph of MC in rendering, with its infinite dimension of paths).
(i) \(\mathbb{E}[f/p] = \int (f/p)\, p = \int f\). (ii) the variance of an independent sum adding up, \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat I_N) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum \mathrm{Var} = \mathrm{Var}/N\) — and the strong law of large numbers gives the convergence. For the typical error, the CLT gives \(\hat I_N - I \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathrm{Var}/N)\): standard deviation in \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}/N}\). ∎
Theorem 6.3 — Importance sampling: the zero-variance optimum Among the densities \(p\), the variance of \(f(X)/p(X)\) is minimal (zero) for \(p^\ast \propto |f|\); in practice one chooses \(p\) close to \(f\) where \(f\) is large. The module's perfect example: for \(I = \int_{\Omega^+} \cos\theta\, d\omega = \pi\) (point-to-point form factor), uniform sampling (\(p = 1/2\pi\)) gives the estimator \(2\pi\cos\theta\), of variance \(\pi^2/3\); cosine sampling (\(p = \cos\theta/\pi\)) gives the constant estimator \(\pi\) — exactly zero variance.
\(p^\ast = |f|/\int|f|\): then \(f/p^\ast = \pm\int|f|\) is constant ⟹ zero variance. For the uniform sampling computation: \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int (2\pi\cos\theta)^2 \frac{d\omega}{2\pi} = 2\pi \int_{\Omega^+}\cos^2\theta\, d\omega = 2\pi \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{3}\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X]^2 = \pi^2\), hence \(\mathrm{Var} = \frac{4\pi^2}{3} - \pi^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\) (using \(\int_{\Omega^+}\cos^2\theta\,d\omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta \sin\theta\, d\theta\, d\varphi = \frac{2\pi}{3}\)). For the cosine: \(f/p = \cos\theta / (\cos\theta/\pi) = \pi\) for every draw. ∎
Definition 6.4 — The three ingredients of a real path tracer
  • Next Event Estimation (NEE): at each bounce, connect the point directly to the emissive sources (shadow ray) instead of waiting for a path to hit them by chance — multiple importance sampling (MIS, Veach): combine the "BRDF" strategy and the "light" strategy with balance weights;
  • Russian roulette: terminate the path with probability \(q\) (the albedo of the point), weighting the survivors by \(1/(1-q)\): unbiased, expected length bounded by the reflectance — the geometric series of Theorem 2.2, sampled;
  • Sampling of extended lights: a \(1\,\mathrm{m}^2\) panel seen from \(3\) m at oblique incidence occupies only a few thousandths of a steradian — draw points on the source (weighted by its area and the distance) rather than directions; this is the factor of 100 between a render "noisy in 10 minutes" and "clean in 10 seconds".
Connections with the rest of the curriculum Estimating the LTE is a high-dimensional integration problem — exactly the setting of Wasserstein distances and their sampling estimators (cf. Optimal Transport module: importance sampling, concentration, curse of dimensionality); MIS is a special case of combining estimators (variance minimization under an unbiasedness constraint). The same proofs serve rendering and learning; only the integrand changes.

7. Acceleration: intersection and BVHs

Definition 7.1 — Ray-triangle intersection (Möller–Trumbore) For a ray \(o + t\mathbf{d}\) and a triangle \((v_0, v_1, v_2)\), one solves \(o + t\mathbf{d} = v_0 + u\,(v_1 - v_0) + v\,(v_2 - v_0)\) in \((t, u, v)\): a \(3 \times 3\) linear system solved by Cramer's rule (2 cross products and a few divisions), the triangle being hit iff \(t > 0\), \(u, v \geq 0\), \(u + v \leq 1\) — and the test returns the barycentrics of Theorem 5.2 for free (interpolation of normals and UVs). Cost: ~20 flops, versus ~36 for the ray-sphere intersection; hence the universal conversion of scenes into triangles.
Definition 7.2 — Bounding volume hierarchies (BVHs) A BVH is a binary tree whose every node carries the bounding box (AABB) of its triangles; a ray traversal descends a node iff its box is hit (slab test: intersection of 3 intervals, ~12 comparisons); the leaves test their triangles. Average cost: \(O(\log n)\) boxes for \(n\) triangles (well-distributed scenes), versus \(O(n)\) brute force; construction \(O(n \log^2 n)\) (median), in practice almost \(O(n \log n)\). The alternatives: kd-trees (splitting planes, optimal for static scenes, expensive construction) and uniform grids (uniform scenes, "teapot in a stadium" destroys them). The BVH wins in animation: refitting the tree when the boxes move is nearly free.
Theorem 7.3 — SAH heuristic: area governs probability For a ray of uniform random direction, the probability of hitting an AABB is proportional to its surface area; $$P[\text{hit } B] = \frac{A(B)}{A(B_{parent})}.$$ From this follows the surface area heuristic (SAH) for choosing a split: $$\mathrm{cost}(x) = C_{trav} + P_L(x)\, C_L(x) + P_R(x)\, C_R(x), \qquad P_{L,R} \propto A(B_{L,R}),$$ and one minimizes \(\mathrm{cost}\) over the candidate positions — the optimal kd/BVH partition to first order.
(Argument, by projection onto the sphere of directions.) For a convex box \(B\), the set of directions \(\omega\) such that a ray cast from a point uniform in \(B_{parent}\) hits \(B\) corresponds, upon projection onto the unit sphere, to the projected area of \(B\); by Cauchy's theorem (mean projected area formula), the mean projected area of a convex body is proportional to its surface area \(A(B)\). Normalizing by the projected area of the parent, \(P = A(B)/A(B_{parent})\). The cost formula then adds the expectations (linearity): traversal paid once, plus the intersection cost of the children weighted by their probabilities. ∎
Example 7.4 — Choosing a split with SAH, round numbers Cubic mother box of edge 2 (area \(A = 24\)), 8 triangles: 5 in the left half, 3 in the right. Median split \(x = 0\): two cubes of edge 1, areas 6 and 6 ⟹ \(\mathrm{cost} = C_{trav} + \tfrac{6}{24}\cdot 5\,c_i + \tfrac{6}{24}\cdot 3\,c_i = C_{trav} + 2\,c_i\). Split at \(x = -0.5\) (left box \((0.5 \times 2 \times 2)\), area 12, for the 5 triangles; right box \((1.5 \times 2 \times 2)\), area 20, for the 3): \(\mathrm{cost} = C_{trav} + \tfrac{12}{24}\cdot 5\,c_i + \tfrac{20}{24} \cdot 3\,c_i = C_{trav} + 5\,c_i\) — worse here. The median balances the triangles, SAH balances the areas; on real scenes (triangles concentrated on surfaces, large empty volumes), the SAH split departs from the median to "cut through the void" and typically gains 20–40% in traversal time — the difference reads directly in render time.
In practice: coherence and SIMD The primary rays of an image are coherent (neighboring, close directions): traversing them in packets of 4–64 (SIMD) maximizes the rejection of entire branches of the tree. Diffuse rays (chapter 6) are incoherent: they are processed one by one, or by dynamic regrouping. These implementation details make a 10× in performance — this is where the "real time" of chapter 10 is played out.

8. Noise, denoising, tone mapping

Definition 8.1 — The noise/time budget An MC render at \(N\) samples per pixel (spp) has noise \(\propto 1/\sqrt{N}\); the mean squared error decomposes as bias² + variance; pure path tracing is unbiased (variance only), truncated-bounce or photon mapping methods trade a little bias for a lot of variance. Typical numbers: interior with sky through the window — 256 spp: visible noise in the corners; 4096 spp: "clean" for cinema; 16–64 spp: the target of real-time engines — then denoising.
Definition 8.2 — Three families of denoising
  • Geometric kernel filters: weighted average by distances (position, normal, depth, albedo) — the bilateral filter and its multi-scale "à-trous" cascade (5 levels, increasing bias, decreasing variance); still the standard of architectural rendering;
  • Rendered signal decomposition: separate albedo × lighting before filtering (the noise lives mostly in the lighting); filter then recompose — free texture edges gained;
  • Learning: temporal recurrent network (Chaitanya et al., 2017) accumulating the frames of an interactive render; OIDN / OptiX denoiser: CNNs trained on (noisy, reference) pairs with auxiliary buffers — ML denoising is today more accurate than 4096 spp on diffuse materials, and the essential link of real time (chapter 10).
Example 8.3 — Measuring the gain Uniform patch of mean radiance \(\bar{L} = 0.5\), noise standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.15\) at 16 spp (SNR ≈ 10.5 dB): at 64 spp, \(\sigma = 0.075\) (SNR ≈ 16.5 dB, +6 dB at each quadrupling); a bilateral filter over 9² neighbors of the same normal and albedo divides the variance by ~81 (81 effective samples): SNR ≈ 29 dB at 16 spp — the equivalent of 1300 spp. This is the weapon of real time: the filter "knows" the patch is flat, MC does not. The difficulty is at edges: different normals ⟹ fewer valid neighbors ⟹ residual noise; hence the multi-scale cascades.
Definition 8.4 — Tone mapping and gamma Radiance is HDR (ratio 10⁴–10⁸ between shadow and sun); the screen is LDR (8 bits, gamma 2.2); the eye is relative (approximately logarithmic response). Tone mapping compresses the scale: Reinhard operator \(L_d = L/(1 + L)\) (global), local extensions (block-wise dodge and burn), or artistic curves (filmic ACES). Then gamma correction: \(V_{enc} = V_{lin}^{1/2.2}\). Classic mistake: filtering (antialiasing, mipmaps, denoising) in sRGB space — nonlinear: the averages are wrong; the whole pipeline of this module works in linear space and converts only at the output.
Aliasing is noise too Antialiasing by stochastic supersampling (per-pixel jitter, code chapter) is an MC estimator of the pixel integral — same theory as chapter 6; deterministic aliasing (sample at the center) is a quadrature bias that turns into periodic moiré patterns. Rule: as soon as an integral is difficult, jitter it — Gaussian noise is visually preferred over structural moiré.

9. Neural rendering: NeRF and 3D Gaussians

Definition 9.1 — The volume rendering equation In a participating medium of density \(\sigma(x)\) (extinction, m⁻¹) and colored emission \(c(x, \omega)\), the radiance along a ray \(r(t) = o + t\mathbf{d}\), \(t \in [s, \infty)\), equals $$L(r) = \int_s^{\infty} T(t)\, \sigma(r(t))\, c(r(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\Big(\!-\int_s^{t} \sigma(r(u))\, du\Big),$$ where \(T\) is the transmittance — the probability that the ray "survives" (without interaction) until \(t\).
Theorem 9.2 — Transmittance solves a linear ODE \(T\) is the unique solution of $$\frac{dT}{dt}(t) = -\,\sigma(r(t))\,T(t), \qquad T(s) = 1,$$ and for \(\sigma\) piecewise constant on intervals \([t_i, t_{i+1})\) of lengths \(\delta_i\), volume rendering discretizes into alpha compositing $$L \approx \sum_i \Big(\prod_{j<i} (1 - \alpha_j)\Big)\, \alpha_i\, c_i, \qquad \alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}.$$
The ODE is first-order linear (chapter 0 recall): setting \(\Sigma(t) = \int_s^t \sigma(r(u))\,du\), \(d(e^{\Sigma} T)/dt = e^{\Sigma}(\Sigma' T + T') = e^{\Sigma}(\sigma T - \sigma T) = 0\), so \(e^{\Sigma(t)}T(t)\) is constant equal to \(T(s) = 1\) (since \(\Sigma(s) = 0\)): \(T(t) = e^{-\Sigma(t)}\). On an interval of constant \(\sigma_i\), \(T(t_{i+1}) = T(t_i)\, e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}\), hence the interaction probability \(\alpha_i = 1 - T(t_{i+1})/T(t_i) = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\); summing "interaction at \(i\) AND survival before" gives the discrete series (the telescoping products \(\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j) = T(t_i)\)). ∎
Definition 9.3 — NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) represents the scene with a neural network \(F_\theta\): $$F_\theta\big(\gamma(x), \gamma(\mathbf{d})\big) = \big(\sigma(x),\ c(x, \mathbf{d})\big),$$ where \(\gamma\) is the positional encoding \(\gamma(u) = (u, \sin 2^k \pi u, \cos 2^k \pi u)_k\) — it gives the MLP the high-frequency spectrum it cannot learn alone (the "spectral theorem" of Tancik et al., 2020). Training minimizes the photometric error on rays from calibrated photos (known pose, SfM); evaluation renders 100–300 samples per ray and alpha-composites (Theorem 9.2). Result: photorealism, novel views — at the price of hours of training and one second per image; the successors (Instant-NGP and its hash grids encoding space) bring training down to minutes.
Definition 9.4 — 3D Gaussians (splatting) The 3D Gaussian representation (Kerbl et al., 2023) replaces the network with 10⁵–10⁷ anisotropic Gaussians (position, 3D covariance, opacity, spherical-harmonic color); a ray accumulates them by alpha compositing ordered by depth, each Gaussian being projected (rasterized) into a 2D ellipse. The gradients of the covariance make the optimization differentiable and fast (the number of Gaussians adapts: cloning/splitting): training in minutes, rendering in real time — today's standard for scene capture. The kinship with chapter 5: it is a rasterization (object order!) of volumetric primitives — the two paradigms of chapter 5 are reconciled.
Example 9.5 — Checking alpha compositing by hand Ray crossing 4 equal intervals \(\delta = 0.5\), densities \(\sigma = (2, 0, 4, 2)\). Alphas: \(\alpha_1 = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632\), \(\alpha_2 = 0\), \(\alpha_3 = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865\), \(\alpha_4 = 0.632\). Colors \(c_i = (1, 0.5, 1, 0.25)\): $$L \approx 0.632 \cdot 1 + 0.368 \cdot 0 \cdot 0.5 + 0.368 \cdot 0.865 \cdot 1 + 0.368 \cdot 0.135 \cdot 0.632 \cdot 0.25 \approx 0.957,$$ (transmittances: \(T_1 = 1\), \(T_2 = 0.368\), \(T_3 = 0.368\), \(T_4 = 0.0498\)) — to be compared with the exact integral \(L = \int T\sigma c\, dt\) of the piecewise-constant densities: NeRF's "midpoint sample" quadrature introduces a small bias (random samples ⟹ unbiased in expectation).
Differentiable rendering, the common paradigm NeRF, 3D Gaussians, ML denoising (chapter 8), upscaling (chapter 10): everywhere, a differentiable renderer connects parameters and image, and learning does the rest — the rendering equation becomes the final layer of a network (cf. Machine Learning module, backpropagation chapter; Multimodal GenAI module, chapter on 3D generation). The theory of chapters 1–6 is not replaced: it provides the structure (physics, estimators, bias/variance) that the networks can only exploit.

10. Real time: GPU, hybrid, upscaling

Definition 10.1 — The hardware graphics pipeline Hardware rasterization (OpenGL/Vulkan/DirectX) chains: vertex shading (transformations, chapter 4) ⟹ assembly/clipping ⟹ rasterization (Theorem 5.2 in silicon) ⟹ pixel/fragment shading (materials, chapter 3) ⟹ z-buffer ⟹ framebuffer. Coherence exploited: triangles cover blocks of contiguous pixels — massive parallelism (thousands of SIMT cores), hierarchies of early depth rejection. Budget: 16.6 ms/frame (60 Hz) for everything — geometry, materials, shadows, post-effects. Pre-MC soft shadow techniques remain essential: filtered shadow maps (PCF, variance shadow maps), screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO), precomputed lightmaps — a hierarchy of bias/variance trade-offs.
Definition 10.2 — RTX hybrid and upscaling
  • Hardware ray tracing (RTX, 2018): units dedicated to BVH traversal (chapter 7) — rays on demand: reflections, soft shadows, GI at 1–2 sampled bounces... then denoised (chapter 8) and recombined over rasterization: the hybrid;
  • Temporal upscaling (DLSS, FSR): render at 1080p, display at 4K; the network (or filter) receives the low-resolution frame + the re-projected temporal history (motion) + the geometric buffers; the formulation is a supervised inverse problem — video super-resolution with the constraint set of chapter 8;
  • Game path tracing (Cyberpunk 2077, Alan Wake 2): full MC at 1–4 spp + recurrent denoising + temporal reconstruction: the theory of chapters 6 and 8, in 16 ms.

What "real time" means for an engineer Real time is not a different theory: it is the same set of equations (chapters 1–6) under a budget constraint — and hence a spending strategy: where to accept bias (shadows, approximate GI), where to keep variance (specular reflections, the eye is sensitive there), where to learn (denoising, upscaling). The design of a modern engine is an exercise in managing bias and variance per dollar of millisecond — the same trade-offs as in ML, transposed to rendering.

A complete path tracer in Python

The program below (~70 lines, NumPy only) implements chapters 4 (camera), 6 (cosine sampling, depth roulette), 1–3 (radiance, Lambert, shadows) and 5/9 (bounces: the series of Theorem 2.2). Reduce W, H, SPP to test; at full resolution, expect ~1 minute (interpreter): each pixel is an independent MC estimator — parallelization is trivial.

import numpy as np

W, H, SPP = 240, 180, 8
def norm(v): return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

# scene: spheres (center, radius, albedo, albedo rho)
S = [(( 0.0, -100.0, -1.0), 100.0, (0.85, 0.80, 0.75), 0.6),   # ground
     (( 0.0,    1.0, -5.0),   1.0, (0.75, 0.25, 0.20), 0.7),
     ((-2.3,    0.7, -4.2),   0.7, (0.25, 0.70, 0.30), 0.7),
     (( 2.3,    0.7, -4.2),   0.7, (0.25, 0.35, 0.90), 0.7)]
Lp  = np.array([6.0, 9.0, 1.0])      # point light
I   = np.array([180.0, 170.0, 150.0])
SKY = np.array([0.45, 0.60, 0.85])   # sky radiance

def intersect(o, d):
    tbest, ibest = 1e9, -1           # first intersection (naive ch. 7)
    for i, (c, r, _, _) in enumerate(S):
        oc = o - c; b = oc @ d; disc = b*b - (oc @ oc - r*r)
        if disc > 0:
            t = -b - np.sqrt(disc)
            if 1e-3 < t < tbest: tbest, ibest = t, i
    return tbest, ibest

def trace(o, d):
    col, att = np.zeros(3), np.ones(3)          # L = sum of T^k Le (th. 2.2)
    for depth in range(4):                      # ... + implicit roulette
        t, i = intersect(o, d)
        if i < 0:
            return col + att * SKY * max(0.1, d[1])
        c, r, albedo, rho = S[i]
        p = o + t*d; n = norm(p - c)
        # next event estimation: point light + shadow ray (ch. 6)
        Lv = Lp - p; dist = np.linalg.norm(Lv); L = Lv / dist
        if intersect(p + 1e-3*n, L)[0] > dist:
            col += att * rho * albedo * I / dist**2 * max(0.0, n @ L)
        # diffuse bounce: cosine sampling (p = cos/pi, th. 6.3)
        d = norm(n + np.random.normal(size=3))
        o, att = p + 1e-3*n, att * rho * albedo
    return col

img = np.zeros((H, W, 3))
for y in range(H):
    for x in range(W):
        acc = np.zeros(3)
        for _ in range(SPP):                    # stochastic AA (ch. 8)
            u = (x + np.random.rand()) / W  - 0.5
            v = 0.5 - (y + np.random.rand()) / H
            acc += trace(np.array([0.0, 1.2, 0.0]), norm([u, v, -1.0]))
        img[y, x] = acc / SPP

img = np.clip(img, 0, None) ** (1.0 / 2.2)      # gamma tone map (ch. 8)
img = img / img.max()                           # exposure normalization
with open("render.ppm", "wb") as f:
    f.write(f"P6\n{W} {H}\n255\n".encode())
    f.write((img * 255).astype(np.uint8).tobytes())
