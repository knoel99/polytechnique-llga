LLM research moves so fast that a lecture course would be outdated before the end of the semester: this module therefore takes the form of a seminar, where each chapter organizes the critical reading of a pivot paper — reasoning (chain-of-thought, RLVR), long context, memory, mixture-of-experts, alternative architectures (Mamba, RWKV), inference efficiency, agents and evaluation. The goal is not to pile up results, but to make you able to read a new paper, spot its fragile assumptions and situate its contributions. In the LLGA master, it is the hinge module between the training and the research frontier — and the only one that literally carries the "LLMs × graphs" crossroads that gives the master its name.

  • Read an LLM research paper critically: claims, evidence, protocol, what the authors do not say;
  • Explain model reasoning: chain-of-thought, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR);
  • Analyze long-context techniques (RoPE, ring attention) and arbitrate between long context and RAG;
  • Compare agent memories: episodic, vector stores, GraphRAG;
  • Explain how mixture-of-experts architectures and alternatives to transformers (SSM, Mamba, RWKV) work and why they matter;
  • Speed up inference with speculative decoding and quantization, quantifying the trade-offs;
  • Analyze the LLMs × graphs crossroads and agent architectures (planning, tools, multi-agents);
  • Evaluate an LLM without deceiving oneself: benchmark contamination, LLM judge bias, open problems.

1. Reasoning: chain-of-thought, RLVR

Today's readings J. Wei et al., Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2201.11903); X. Wang et al., Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models, ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2203.11171); H. Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step, ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2305.20050); DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning, 2025 (arXiv:2501.12948); N. Lambert et al., Tulu 3, 2024 (arXiv:2411.15124) — which names RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards).

1.1. Chain-of-thought and self-consistency

The prompt "let's think step by step" triggers the generation of an intermediate reasoning chain \(z\) before the answer \(y\) — and the gains emerge with scale (clear from ~1011 parameters in the original study): the model allocates compute tokens to the problem. The formal reading:

Proposition 1.1 — Self-consistency = marginalization (Wang et al., 2023) Generating a chain \(z\) then an answer \(y\) realizes a joint model \(p(y, z \mid x) = p(z\mid x)\,p(y \mid x, z)\). The probability of the correct answer under the full model is the margin $$p(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{z} p(y, z \mid x).$$ Self-consistency approximates \(\arg\max_y p(y\mid x)\) by majority vote over \(k\) sampled chains \(z_1, \dots, z_k \sim p(\cdot \mid x)\): a Monte Carlo of "latent reasoning". Typical gains: +10 to +20 points on GSM8K compared to greedy decoding — for an inference cost of ×\(k\).
Sampling \(z_i \sim p(z\mid x)\) then \(y_i \sim p(y \mid x, z_i)\) produces pairs \((y_i, z_i)\) distributed according to the joint; the count \(\frac1k \sum_i \mathbf{1}[y_i = y]\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sum_z p(z \mid x)\,p(y \mid x, z) = p(y\mid x)\) (the two successive samplings compose exactly the joint). The empirical argmax converges to the true argmax as \(k \to \infty\) (concentration of frequencies) — it is a special case of the Monte Carlo marginalization seen in the ML module, with the particularity that the latent space is text. ∎

1.2. Process vs outcome rewards

Training a reward model on the final answer (ORM, outcome) is noisy: an answer that is right by chance receives the same reward as a clean reasoning. Lightman et al. build PRM800K (800,000 step-by-step labels given by humans) and train a PRM (process reward model): at equal labeling budget, the PRM selects better solutions on MATH than the ORM — supervision of the process is more informative than that of the outcome. Immediate limit: step-by-step labeling is expensive and subjective; hence the idea of automating verification...

1.3. RLVR: verifiable rewards and the emergence of reasoning

Definition 1.2 — RLVR Instead of a learned reward model (subject to reward hacking), the reward is a verifiable rule: does the mathematical answer match the ground truth? does the code pass the tests? is the format correct? Training (GRPO — cf. LLMs module ch. 5; PPO — cf. RL module ch. 4) maximizes $$\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim \pi_\theta} \bigl[\, r_{\text{verif}}(x, y) \,\bigr]$$ over domains where truth is decidable. Tulu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024) makes it the third stage of open post-training (SFT → DPO → RLVR).
Pivot result — DeepSeek-R1 (2025) R1-Zero: purely RL (no initial SFT) with rule-based rewards on math/code/format; the model develops by itself reasoning behaviors — verification, backtracking, reflection ("aha moment"), lengthening chains — measurable by the growing generation length over the course of training. R1: adding cold-start data + multiple stages (SFT → RL → re-SFT) stabilizes and aligns, up to rivaling the best closed reasoning models on math/code benchmarks. Associated publication in Nature (2025) — read both: the technical account and the scientific letter do not tell exactly the same story.
Critical analysis (a whole session) (i) Generalization: RLVR only improves domains close to pre-training + verifiable; transfer to "general" reasoning is debated. (ii) Benchmarks: MATH/AIME are saturated and contaminable (ch. 9); are the advances measured on the scale of their own pollution? (iii) Residual reward hacking: even "hard" rules are bypassable (misleading format, weak tests); (iv) Costs: chains of tens of thousands of tokens — reasoning is a product that is compute-bound at inference (cf. ML Ops module ch. 4, green ops ch. 10); (v) Alternative reading: does R1-Zero select capabilities already present (pre-training) rather than create them? The distillation experiments (small models trained on R1's traces outperforming their direct RL cousins) lean toward the first reading. Exercise: defend both theses with the figures of the paper.

2. Long context: RoPE, ring attention, RAG or LC?

Today's readings J. Su et al., RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), 2021 (arXiv:2104.09864); S. Chen et al., Extending Context Window via Positional Interpolation, 2023 (arXiv:2306.15595); B. Peng et al., YaRN, 2023 (arXiv:2309.00071); H. Liu et al., Ring Attention with Blockwise Transformers for Near-Infinite Context, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2310.01889); N. Liu et al., Lost in the Middle, 2023 (arXiv:2307.03172); Z. Li et al., Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?, EMNLP 2024 industry (arXiv:2407.16833); P. Xu et al., Retrieval meets Long Context LLMs, ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2310.03025).

2.1. RoPE: position by rotation

Definition 2.1 — RoPE (Su et al., 2021) Each even coordinate pair \((2i, 2i{+}1)\) of the vector is viewed as a complex number to which a rotation of angle \(m\theta_i\) is applied, with \(m\) the position of the token and \(\theta_i = b^{-2i/d}\) (geometric bases): $$q_m = R(m\Theta)\, q, \qquad k_n = R(n\Theta)\, k, \qquad \Theta = \mathrm{diag}(\theta_0, \dots).$$ The attention dot product becomes a function of the relative position — and the high frequencies \(\theta_i\) resolve the local, the low frequencies the global.
Proposition 2.2 — RoPE encodes only relative position \(\langle R(m\Theta)\,q,\; R(n\Theta)\,k \rangle\) depends only on \(m - n\) (and on \(q, k\)). The proof is one line of trigonometry: per coordinate pairs, $$\langle R(m\theta)q,\, R(n\theta)k\rangle = \langle R((m-n)\theta)\, q,\; k \rangle,$$ since \(R(m\theta)^\top R(n\theta) = R((n-m)\theta)\) (composition of the rotations — they commute in the plane).
Plane rotations are orthogonal: \(\langle R(m\theta)q, R(n\theta)k\rangle = q^\top R(m\theta)^\top R(n\theta)k = q^\top R((n - m)\theta)k\). Summing over the coordinate pairs (each pair with its own frequency \(\theta_i\)), the attention score is a function of \(m - n\) only. Engineering consequence: extending the context amounts to re-parameterizing the angles, not to relearning positional embeddings. ∎

2.2. Extending the window: interpolation and YaRN

Positional interpolation (Chen et al., 2023): trained with angles up to \(L\), one serves \(L' > L\) by linearly compressing the positions \(m \mapsto m \cdot L/L'\) — the model sees angles already covered; a few hundred fine-tuning steps suffice (Llama-2 4k → 32k). YaRN (Peng et al., 2023) refines: interpolate selectively by frequency (the low — global — frequencies are interpolated; the high — local — extrapolated; "NTK-aware") and rescale the attention temperature (\(\times\) constant depending on the extension factor) to compensate for the dilution of the scores; result: an extension that reduces data and fine-tuning steps by roughly 10× compared to PI. Spectral viewpoint (cf. Signal Processing module): handling a larger window = handling a wider frequency spectrum without aliasing.

2.3. Ring attention: cutting the memory cost in a ring

Proposition 2.3 — Ring Attention (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2023) Cut the sequence of length \(N\) into \(D\) blocks distributed over \(D\) GPUs; at each step, each GPU computes the attention of its query block against a key/value block received from a neighbor, then passes this block to the next (ring topology) — overlapping communication and computation. After \(D\) steps, each query has seen all the blocks: the attention is exact (not an approximation), and the memory per GPU drops from \(\mathcal{O}(N)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(N/D)\). Demonstration at scale: contexts on the order of 32 million tokens on 32 A100 GPUs — ~27× the memory state of the art of the time.

2.4. RAG or long context? Lost in the middle and the hybrid router

"Lost in the Middle" (N. Liu et al., 2023): when the information is placed in the middle of a long context, performance collapses in a U shape (beginning and end favored) — a 128k window does not "read" its 128k uniformly. The RAG vs LC dossier: Li et al. (2024) compare systematically on long-context tasks: with sufficient resources, long context wins on average (retrieval can miss the needle); but RAG remains much more economical — and their Self-Route (the model estimates whether the query is answerable with the retrieved excerpts; if not, the whole document is sent) achieves LC quality for ~39% less cost. Xu et al. (ICLR 2024) complete the picture: retrieval improves models whatever the window (4k/16k/32k), and a 4k + retrieval approaches a 16k without retrieval — RAG is not dead, it is complementary.

Critical analysis (i) "Needle in a haystack" (finding a sentence planted in a long text) is a retrieval test, not a long-context reasoning test — multi-document aggregation benchmarks break the scoreboard; (ii) the KV cache grows linearly with \(N\) (cf. ML Ops module ch. 3): "free" LC at the API is an economic choice of the provider, not a physical property; (iii) RoPE extensions are measured in perplexity and in downstream tasks — YaRN shows they can diverge; (iv) Self-Route relies on the model's self-assessment (meta-cognition): how good is it on adversarial queries? To be related to ch. 3 (memory) and the ML Ops module ch. 6 (index cost vs context cost). 
# Self-Route self-evaluation (sketch) -- RAG first, LC as fallback
def self_route(question, docs, llm, budget):
    snippets = retrieve(question, docs, k=8)          # classic RAG
    verdict = llm(f"Sauras-tu repondre avec SEULEMENT ces extraits ? "
                  f"(OUI/NON)\n---\n{snippets}", max_tokens=2, temp=0)
    if verdict.strip().upper().startswith("OUI"):
        return llm(f"Extraits :\n{snippets}\nQuestion : {question}")
    return llm(f"Documents complets :\n{concat(docs)}\nQuestion : {question}")
    # cost: ~39% of full LC if the router is well calibrated (Li et al. 2024)

3.2. Three papers, three ideas of memory

  • Memorizing Transformers (Wu et al., 2022): an attention layer, non-differentiable, looks up its keys/values in an external memory of \((k, v)\) pairs by nearest neighbors (kNN) over past tokens — including other documents. The model learns to use a raw memory; effective for perplexity, but the memory is frozen (no update during generation).
  • Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023): 25 simulated agents in a virtual small town; each agent owns a timestamped memory stream; retrieval weights recency × importance × relevance; a reflection process periodically synthesizes the memories into higher-level beliefs ("I should organize a party"), re-injected as memories. The conceptual architecture of every memory-equipped conversational agent.
  • MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023): the model becomes its own operating system: main context (RAM) vs external storage (disk), paging driven by function calls the LLM triggers itself (core_memory_append, archival_memory_insert…). Memory management becomes a learned/instructed behavior — the ancestor of the "agent + notepad" loops.
  • GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024): structured memory — an entity graph extracted by an LLM, communities detected (Leiden), hierarchical summaries; global questions ("which themes?") become a map-reduce over the community summaries, where vector RAG falls apart (cf. Graph ML module ch. 11 for the graph foundations; ML Ops module ch. 6 for the indexing cost).
Proposition 3.1 — The retrieval triptych of generative agents The retrieval score of a memory \(s\) at time \(t\) for the query \(q\) reads $$\mathrm{score}(s; q, t) \;=\; w_r\, e^{-\Delta t/\tau} \;+\; w_i\, \mathrm{imp}(s) \;+\; w_p\, \cos\bigl(e(s), e(q)\bigr),$$ exponential recency + importance (predicted by the LLM at writing time) + semantic relevance. This is exactly the signal fusion of a search engine reranker (cf. NLP ch. 2): "agent memory" reinvents learning to rank — the originality lies in the reflection loop that creates new synthetic memories.
Critical analysis (i) Evaluation: each of these papers evaluates memory differently (perplexity, simulated interviews, multi-session dialogues, global questions) — no consensus benchmark; wariness toward small-sample gains. (ii) Writing: the hard question is what one stores (everything? a summary? a graph?) and when one forgets; MemGPT delegates to the model, GraphRAG to a pipeline — two philosophies (agency vs infrastructure). (iii) Context fraud: with windows of a million tokens (ch. 2), "keep everything in the context" becomes credible again — until the next "lost in the middle". (iv) LLGA position: graph-structured memory is the exact place where the master crosses its two legs (LLMs + graphs); ch. 7 comes back to it.

4. Mixture-of-Experts

Today's readings N. Shazeer et al., Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer, ICLR 2017 (arXiv:1701.06538); W. Fedus, B. Zoph, N. Shazeer, Switch Transformers, JMLR 2022 (arXiv:2101.03961); A. Jiang et al., Mixtral of Experts, 2024 (arXiv:2401.04088); DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report, 2024 (arXiv:2412.19437).
Definition 4.1 — Sparse MoE layer Each feed-forward block is replaced by \(N\) experts \(E_1, \dots, E_N\) and a router: $$y \;=\; \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}(g(x))} g_i(x)\, E_i(x), \qquad g(x) = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(W_g\,x\bigr),$$ only the \(k\) strongest experts are computed (k = 1-2 typically). The total parameters grow (capacity), the active parameters per token remain bounded (cost): Mixtral 8×7B = ~47B parameters, ~13B active; DeepSeek-V3 = 671B of which ~37B active.

4.1. Balancing the experts: the auxiliary loss

Proposition 4.2 — Auxiliary load-balancing loss (Switch) With \(f_i\) = fraction of tokens routed to expert \(i\) and \(P_i\) = average router probability mass on \(i\): $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \;=\; \alpha\, N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i\, P_i, \qquad \sum_i f_i = \sum_i P_i = 1,$$ whose minimum \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\) is reached at uniform routing \(f = P = \mathbf{1}/N\). The loss penalizes the degenerate routing where a few experts capture everything — otherwise the router gradient collapses (the unpicked experts receive no gradient: the rich get richer).
At the uniform point, \(\sum_i f_iP_i = N \cdot \frac1N \cdot \frac1N = \frac1N\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha N \cdot \frac1N = \alpha\). To show that this is indeed a minimum in the targeted regime, we use Chebyshev's sum inequality: if the sequences \((f_i)\) and \((P_i)\) are similarly ordered (co-monotone — the likely experts are also the most loaded, exactly the degenerate regime that we want to penalize), then $$\frac1N \sum_i f_i P_i \;\geq\; \Bigl(\frac1N \sum_i f_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac1N \sum_i P_i\Bigr) = \frac{1}{N^2}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \sum_i f_i P_i \;\geq\; \frac1N,$$ with equality if and only if one of the sequences is constant. Hence \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \geq \alpha\), equality at uniform. Finally, the dynamic effect: \(\partial (f_iP_i)/\partial P_i = f_i \geq 0\) — the gradient pulls \(P_i\) down proportionally to the load \(f_i\): a negative feedback loop against concentration. ∎
Critical analysis — read the hidden assumption The lower bound is only proved for co-monotone \((f, P)\): an anti-monotone pair (a very likely but lightly loaded expert) could have a product below \(1/N\) — the loss is not a symmetric divergence in the strict sense; it is a load proxy whose gradient dynamics does most of the work. This is exactly the kind of detail a critical paper reading must catch: what quantity does the loss actually measure, under what assumption, and what does its gradient do? DeepSeek-V3 goes as far as replacing the loop with auxiliary-loss-free balancing: a per-expert bias term, dynamically adjusted to the observed load — direct control rather than a differentiable penalty, with reported quality gains. Less elegant, more effective: up for debate.

4.2. What the experts learn

The Mixtral analysis (the "routing" figure) shows syntactic and domain-level specialization (code, math, languages) rather than fine-grained topics — and substantial redundancy across experts: the MoE mostly democratizes capacity, not the division of labor. DeepSeek-V3 pushes two structural innovations: fine-grained experts (more numerous, smaller) + shared experts (always active, capturing cross-cutting knowledge — reducing the redundancy), and FP8 training at scale (14.8T tokens) — the technical report is a systems-engineering course in itself (expert-parallel communication, cf. Emerging ML module ch. 2 on distributed computing).

Critique in practice — what are the "active parameters" for? Comparing a 47B/13B-active MoE to a 13B dense model is misleading: the MoE keeps 47B in memory (cf. ML Ops module ch. 3: the KV cache and the weights reside in full in VRAM) but ~13B of compute per token. MoE wins provided large batches (amortizing memory through throughput): excellent for shared serving, debatable for single-user. The LLGA reading grid: every architecture must be evaluated at full cost (memory, compute, communication) — not in advertising "parameters".

5. Alternative architectures: SSM, Mamba, RWKV

Dao, Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces, 2023 (arXiv:2312.00752); B. 5.1. From RNNs to state space models

Definition 5.1 — Discrete SSM A state space model maps the input \(u_t\) to the output \(y_t\) via a linear hidden state \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\): $$x_{t} = \bar A\, x_{t-1} + \bar B\, u_t, \qquad y_t = C\, x_t,$$ where \((\bar A, \bar B)\) come from the (zero-order) discretization of the continuous system \(\dot x = A x + B u\) with step \(\Delta\): \(\bar A = \exp(\Delta A)\), \(\bar B = (\Delta A)^{-1}\bigl(\exp(\Delta A) - I\bigr)\,\Delta B\). The classic RNN reappears with a structured state matrix (S4: diagonal + low-rank terms, inherited from HiPPO to memorize the long history).
Proposition 5.2 — LTI ⇒ convolution (S4) If \((\bar A, \bar B, C)\) are constant (independent of time and input — LTI), then the system is a convolution product: $$y_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \overline{K}_s\, u_{t-s}, \qquad \overline{K} = \bigl(C\bar B,\; C\bar A \bar B,\; C\bar A^2 \bar B,\; \dots\bigr),$$ computable in \(\mathcal{O}(L \log L)\) via FFT (Signal Processing module): parallel training over the whole sequence, like a transformer.
Let us unroll the recurrence from \(x_{-1} = 0\) (zero initial state): \(x_0 = \bar B u_0\); \(x_1 = \bar A \bar B u_0 + \bar B u_1\); by immediate induction, \(x_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \bar A^{\,t-s}\, \bar B\, u_s\). Then \(y_t = C x_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} (C \bar A^{\,t-s} \bar B)\, u_s = \sum_{s=0}^{t} \overline K_{t-s}\, u_s\) — a discrete convolution with kernel \(\overline K_s = C \bar A^s \bar B\), where the structure of \(\bar A\) (diagonal) makes each term computable in \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). ∎

5.2. Mamba: making the state selective

The LTI SSM is a fixed filter: it cannot choose to memorize or forget depending on the content. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) makes \(\Delta_t, \bar B_t, C_t\) functions of the input (small MLPs on \(u_t\)): the selection. Cascade consequence: the convolution (prop. 5.2) is destroyed (the kernel depends on position) → back to a recurrence, but parallelizable via the prefix scan (associativity of \((\bar A_t, \bar B_t)\) — Blelloch's algorithm; cf. Refresher CS) with fused GPU kernels ("hardware-aware"): \(\mathcal{O}(L)\) training, \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) inference per token and constant state memory — the selling point against the transformer's KV cache (cf. ML Ops module ch. 3). Finally, Mamba embeds the SSM inside a gated MLP-style block. Mamba-2 (SSD) will reformulate the SSM × linear attention duality — the loop comes full circle with ch. 6.

5.3. RWKV and the hybrids

Proposition 5.3 — RWKV: linearizing attention into an RNN (Peng et al., 2023) RWKV rewrites attention as a recurrence with per-channel decay (time-mixing): $$w_t = \sigma(W_t)\odot w_{t-1} + k_t, \qquad y_t = r_t \odot \frac{v_t}{w_t + \epsilon},$$ (schematic form): the state \(w_t\) accumulates past values channel-wise with a learned exponential decay (\(r_t\) = receptance, \(v_t\) the step's value) — a linear attention normalized over time, trainable in parallel (like a transformer), executable as an RNN (constant memory). At scale: models up to 14B — the largest RNNs ever trained at its publication.

Hybrids: pure SSMs lose on exact recall tasks (copying an association seen a long time ago: the compressed state has no room to keep everything — a recurring critique, cf. the associative recall studies); hence mixed architectures: Jamba (AI21, 2024) interleaves attention + Mamba + MoE blocks (1:7:1) and shows that ~one attention layer in eight suffices to restore recall; Griffin/De et al. (2024) alternate recurrence (RG-LRU) and local attention. The 2024-2026 consensus: attention is irreplaceable for precise retrieval; the SSM excels at long context on a constant memory budget; the hybrid beats both.

Critical analysis (i) The "Mamba matches transformers at X times less memory" curves are established at calibrated data and compute sizes; transformers have an ecosystem (serving, quantization, ch. 6) that SSMs lack — the real adoption cost exceeds the paper. (ii) Exact recall is a limit case (one key among millions): needle-in-a-haystack-style benchmarks are highly sensitive to it, comprehension tasks far less so. (iii) RWKV illustrates the virtue of deployable simplicity (no KV cache, no keys/values); Mamba that of algorithmic design (the scan). (iv) A favorite exam question: "why is the transformer KV cache a feature and not just a cost?" — because it stores the history exactly (perfect recall), where the SSM state summarizes it.

6.1. Speculative decoding: the small proposes, the large disposes

Definition 6.1 — Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) A draft model \(q\) (small, fast) generates \(\gamma\) tokens; the target model \(p\) (large, slow) verifies them in a single parallel pass (the \(\gamma\) positions are checked simultaneously — prefilling); each accepted token is free, the first rejected one is corrected by a residual sample; the distribution of \(p\) is preserved exactly (the paper proves it: correction by sampling according to \(\mathrm{norm}(\max(0, p - q))\) at the rejection point). Typical ×2-3 speedups at identical quality.
Theorem 6.2 — Expected number of tokens per iteration If \(\alpha\) is the (average) probability that a token of \(q\) is accepted by \(p\), the expected number of tokens generated per full iteration (verification + correction) is $$\mathbb{E}[\#\text{tokens}] \;=\; \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma + 1}}{1 - \alpha}.$$ With \(\alpha = 0.8\) and \(\gamma = 4\): ≈ 3.36 tokens per target pass instead of 1.
Let \(A\) be the number of tokens accepted before the first rejection. The acceptance of each token is modeled as independent with probability \(\alpha\); then \(A \sim \mathrm{Geo}(\alpha)\) truncated at \(\gamma\): in case of rejection at position \(j < \gamma\), the iteration produces \(j\) tokens accepted plus the corrected token, i.e. \(j+1\) tokens; if all \(\gamma\) are accepted, the pass produces \(\gamma\) tokens plus one bonus token sampled from \(p\) (position \(\gamma{+}1\) is directly generated by the target in the same pass): \(j+1\) tokens for all \(j \leq \gamma\). Hence \[ \mathbb{E} = \sum_{j=0}^{\gamma-1} (1-\alpha)\,\alpha^{j}\,(j+1) \;+\; \alpha^{\gamma}\,(\gamma + 1). \] We recognize the truncated series of the geometric expectation: by using \(\sum_{j=0}^{m} \alpha^j = \frac{1-\alpha^{m+1}}{1-\alpha}\) and its derivative for the \((j+1)\) factor, the sum reduces exactly to \(\frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}\). ∎
Critical analysis The gain depends on three clocks: \(\alpha\) (small/large agreement — high on ordinary text, low on tricky code or creative outputs); the cost ratio \(c\) (verifying \(\gamma\) tokens costs more than one decoding step — prefilling); \(\gamma\) (too large = many rejections at the last rank). Leviathan et al. give the optimal \(\gamma\) as a function of \((\alpha, c)\) — a lab exercise. Descendants: self-speculation (the model verifies itself via skipped layers), multiple heads (Medusa), trees — to be documented in your tech watch.

6.2. Quantization: GPTQ and AWQ

Proposition 6.3 — GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023): layer by layer, Hessian in hand Quantize \(W\) into \(\hat W\) (e.g. 4 bits, with an error \(\Delta = W - \hat W\)) by minimizing the error on the outputs actually produced: $$\min_{\hat W}\; \bigl\| W X - \hat W X \bigr\|_F^2 \;=\; \min_{\Delta}\; \mathrm{tr}\bigl(\Delta\, X X^\top\, \Delta^\top\bigr), \qquad H = 2 X X^\top,$$ where \(X\) is a sample of calibration activations. The algorithm proceeds column by column (greedy): quantize a block of weights, compute the error on the outputs, propagate it onto the remaining columns via the Cholesky inverse of \(H\) — an approximation of the exact method (OBS/OBD, cf. ML module ch. regularization) made GPU-friendly. Models with billions of parameters are quantized to ~4 bits in a few GPU-hours, with low perplexity losses.
Proposition 6.4 — AWQ (Lin et al., 2024): protecting the salient channels Observation: ~1% of activation channels (per matrix) carry extreme amplitudes; quantizing them naively destroys performance. AWQ does not quantify them specially — it rescales: multiplying an input channel by \(s\) and dividing the weight column by \(s\) preserves the exact mathematics of the layer while moving the quantization error away from the salient channels (the optimal scale is estimated by search over the calibration activations, without backpropagation). Result: robust 4 bits, no exotic per-group hybrid reconstruction, commodity deployment.

7.1. The "bare" LLM facing serialized graphs

Pivotal result — NLGraph (Wang et al., 2023) 29,370 problems in natural language covering 8 tasks (connectivity, shortest path, maximum independent set… up to the Hamiltonian path and max flow), increasing difficulty (graph size, number of steps). GPT-3/GPT-4 clearly beat chance on the easy tasks… and collapse on the hard ones; the authors propose Build-a-Graph (making the graph explicit in the prompt) and a graph translator (converting the output into structure), which help — without closing the gap with a few-line algorithms. Measured conclusion: LLMs have a preliminary graphical intuition, not an algorithmic skill.
Proposition 7.1 — Encoding is a first-order variable (Fatemi et al., 2024) "Talk like a Graph" systematically studies how to serialize a graph: edge lists, matrix, incident relations, degree-based ordering, etc. Findings: (i) the encoding choice shifts accuracy by tens of points — more than the model choice in some regimes; (ii) the degree-based order helps overall; (iii) performance decays with graph size far faster than with text length — it is the structure, not the length, that costs. Practical takeaway: serializing a graph is an engineering design act (and of research), not a formality.

7.2. Hybridizing: GNN-encoder + LLM-decoder

Definition 7.2 — G-Retriever (He et al., NeurIPS 2024) To answer questions over a large textual graph (scenes, knowledge maps): (1) retrieve the relevant subgraph by formulating retrieval as a prize-collecting Steiner tree (PCST — "relevant" nodes carry prizes, edges carry costs: maximize prize − cost of the connected subtree); (2) encode the subgraph with a GNN (message passing — Graph ML module ch. 5); (3) inject node embeddings as soft tokens into the LLM (learned adapter); (4) joint fine-tuning. Reported effects: better answers and reduced hallucinations on ExplaGraphs/SceneGraphs/WebQSP, scalable to graphs of hundreds of thousands of nodes (one never sends the whole graph in the prompt).

8.1. ReAct: reasoning and acting

The ReAct prompt alternates reasoning traces (Thought) and actions (Action / Observation) within a single trajectory: the model unrolls "thought → action → observation → thought → …". On HotpotQA and ALFWorld, ReAct beats pure chain-of-thought (fewer hallucinations of impossible steps — the world answers) and pure acting (the observations are interpreted). It becomes the default grammar of agents — that of the native "function calls" of current LLMs. RL link ch. 7: ReAct is a policy over an interaction tree; the chain-of-thought, its narrative value function.

8.2. Toolformer: learning where to call the tools

Proposition 8.1 — Toolformer's self-training criterion For each candidate position \(t\), insert an API call annotated \(c(t)\) (the call and its result); compare the next-token prediction loss without the call vs with its result provided: $$I_t \;=\; -\log p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}\bigr) \;+\; \log p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}, c(t)\bigr),$$ (schematic form): \(I_t > 0\) — the call reduces the loss — flags a useful call. The model itself filters its own retained calls, trains on them, and learns to decide when to compute (calculator, retrieval, translation, calendar…) — the direct precursor of the native tool-calls of current LLMs (LLMs module ch. 8).

8.3. Reflection, multi-agent, and the question of emergence

  • Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023): after a failure, a verbal feedback (self-generated or from the environment) is stored and re-injected at the next attempt — "verbal RL": improvement happens in the context, not in the weights. Striking gains on HumanEval and ALFWorld at zero training cost.
  • CAMEL (Li et al., 2023): two agents role-play (user/assistant) to generate instruction data — the multi-agent conversation as a data synthesis engine.
  • AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023): a framework where configurable agents (LLM, human, tool) converse programmatically — the industrial design pattern of multi-agent teams (orchestrator, critics, executors), to be connected to the guardrails and the tracing of the module ML Ops ch. 8.
Proposition 8.2 — The unforgiving arithmetic of chains A \(k\)-step task with per-step success \(p\) succeeds with \(p^k\) (independence): \(p = 0.98\), \(k = 50\) → 36%. For a reliable agent, three levers: shorten the chains (composite tools), make each step reliable (Reflexion, retries, deterministic tools), and avoid long chains where an algorithm exists — an agent that plans a topological sort step by step is an architecture mistake (ch. 7: NLGraph).
Critical analysis (i) Evaluation: agent gains are measured on environments (ALFWorld, HumanEval, SWE-bench-like) whose fidelity to the real world is debated — unmodeled costs (ML Ops ch. 4/8), noisy environment, absent adversary. (ii) Multi-agent emergence: when "several agents" beat "one agent", is it emergence — or the effect of a better prompt / more compute? Several ablation studies suggest the second reading: demand honest ablations (a single agent, same token budget). (iii) Safety: every tool is an injection surface (Security module ch. 5-6); multi-agent environments multiply implicit trusts — the least-privilege pattern (ML Ops ch. 8-9) remains the only serious defense. (iv) RL link: the LLM agent as a policy — agentic "behaviors" are now trained by RLVR on trajectories (ch. 1): the prompt/training boundary is shifting under our eyes.

Liang et al., Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM), TMLR 2023 (arXiv:2211.09110).

9.1. Contamination: when the test leaks into training

Definition 9.1 — Benchmark data contamination (BDC) The pollution of a benchmark when its data (questions and/or answers) have been seen during pre-training — accidental (the web contains the solutions), or even deliberate (training on the test's variant). Signals: n-gram overlaps with the corpus, embedding similarity, abnormally low test perplexity, the ability to complete the items without their instruction, "random" performance on renamed variants (changing the figures of a math problem). Responses: canaries ("do not train on this string"), dynamic benchmarks refreshed (e.g. LiveBench), private sets, kept-current evaluations (challenges with a cutoff date).
Proposition 9.2 — What a contaminated score estimates (and what it does not) A benchmark measures performance on a task distribution \(P_{\text{test}}\). If the model has seen \(n\) examples of \(P_{\text{test}}\) during training, the score observes a mixture \(\rho \cdot \text{memory} + (1-\rho) \cdot \text{generalization}\): the estimand has shifted — the score overestimates and distorts comparisons (the models trained more recently, on larger corpora, are more contaminated: the bias advances with time). The methodological remedy: report jointly (i) the raw score, (ii) the score on re-generated variants, (iii) an overlap measure — and treat the (i)-(ii) gap as an interval, not as noise.

9.2. LLM judges: useful, biased, calibratable

MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) validates the LLM judge: agreement with human preferences is high (GPT-4 as judge ≈ 80%+ of inter-human agreement level), which legitimizes its industrial use (ML Ops module ch. 5). But the biases are systematic: position (favoring the first answer — corrected by alternating order), verbosity (long answers win), self-preference (the judge favors the answers of its own model family). Wang et al. (ACL 2024) show that a simple order swap flips a substantial share of verdicts — "not fair evaluators". HELM (Liang et al.) answers with the holistic: same models, dozens of scenarios, multiple metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, bias, toxicity…), published protocol — evaluation as a slow science versus the leaderboard as a fast sport.

Critical analysis — the "methods" session (i) Goodhart: every public benchmark becomes a training objective — the value of a score decays with its popularity; (ii) Variance: a leaderboard at ±0.5 points with 500 questions ranks nothing (cf. ML Ops module ch. 2, Hoeffding); (iii) Judge the judge: calibrate each judge on a human golden set before using it (measured biases, alternating orders, rubrics); (iv) Saturation: when everyone exceeds 90% accuracy, the benchmark measures noise — move to the dynamic/adversarial evaluations (red teaming of the Security module ch. 10, agents of ch. 8). LLGA rule: no evaluation number without its protocol — neither in the papers you read, nor in your own.

10. Open problems

Today's readings P. Villalobos, J. Sevilla, L. Heim, et al., Will we run out of data? Limits of LLM scaling based on human-generated data, 2022 (arXiv:2211.04325); I. 10.1. Data exhaustion

Proposition 10.1 — The Villalobos et al. (2022) window The stock of "quality" public text produced by humans is finite (on the order of a few 1014 tokens actually usable); training set sizes grow exponentially (~2 to 2.5×/year at the time of the study); by extrapolating the two curves, the effective stock would be exhausted between 2026 and 2032 — high-quality data first. This is not a prediction of progress stopping but a regime change: the constraint shifts to the data side.

The answers in progress: synthetic data (models generate their own training material — with the risk of model collapse: Shumailov et al. show, on toy models and Gaussians, that recursive training on generated data erodes the distribution tails — diversity collapses generation after generation); data efficiency (tokens counted, curriculum, "quality over quantity"); new sources (audio, video, interactions); rights — the question becomes legal (AI Act: copyright policy of GPAI — Security module ch. 10) before being purely technical.

10.2. Continual learning

LLMs are frozen at publication: any change requires a new training run or a fine-tune (with its train: catastrophic forgetting of earlier capabilities, safety regression — ML Ops module ch. 2 and 7). Continual learning targets incremental updating: replay of a core of experiences, regularization toward the previous weights, additive modules (successive LoRAs, merging), model merging (combining the weights of specialists — simple averages, TIES: surprising and under-theorized results). The Yu et al. (2024) survey maps the field; the honest assessment: no scheme dominates yet, and the success criterion itself is blurry (remember what, from how many versions back?).
