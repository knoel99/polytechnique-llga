10. Open problems

Today's readings P. Villalobos, J. Sevilla, L. Heim, et al., Will we run out of data? Limits of LLM scaling based on human-generated data, 2022 (arXiv:2211.04325); I. Shumailov et al., AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data (the "curse of recursion"), Nature 2024; H. Yu et al., Continual Learning of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey, 2024 (arXiv:2402.01344).

10.1. Data exhaustion

Proposition 10.1 — The Villalobos et al. (2022) window The stock of "quality" public text produced by humans is finite (on the order of a few 1014 tokens actually usable); training set sizes grow exponentially (~2 to 2.5×/year at the time of the study); by extrapolating the two curves, the effective stock would be exhausted between 2026 and 2032 — high-quality data first. This is not a prediction of progress stopping but a regime change: the constraint shifts to the data side.

The answers in progress: synthetic data (models generate their own training material — with the risk of model collapse: Shumailov et al. show, on toy models and Gaussians, that recursive training on generated data erodes the distribution tails — diversity collapses generation after generation); data efficiency (tokens counted, curriculum, "quality over quantity"); new sources (audio, video, interactions); rights — the question becomes legal (AI Act: copyright policy of GPAI — Security module ch. 10) before being purely technical.

10.2. Continual learning

LLMs are frozen at publication: any change requires a new training run or a fine-tune (with its train: catastrophic forgetting of earlier capabilities, safety regression — ML Ops module ch. 2 and 7). Continual learning targets incremental updating: replay of a core of experiences, regularization toward the previous weights, additive modules (successive LoRAs, merging), model merging (combining the weights of specialists — simple averages, TIES: surprising and under-theorized results). The Yu et al. (2024) survey maps the field; the honest assessment: no scheme dominates yet, and the success criterion itself is blurry (remember what, from how many versions back?).

10.3. Other fronts — and conclusion of the seminar

  • Memory vs context vs weights (ch. 2-3): where should knowledge be stored — parameters, KV, external index? The marginal cost of each byte differs by three orders of magnitude; the answer will depend on serving economics (ML Ops module ch. 3-4);
  • Reasoning beyond the verifiable (ch. 1): extending reliable rewards beyond math/code;
  • Safety at scale: statistical alignment facing competing objectives (Security module ch. 5-6);
  • Interpretability as a science: from IRM (probes, SAE) to verifiable mechanics (Security module ch. 1-3);
  • LLGA: the LLM × graphs confrontation (ch. 7) remains an open construction site — serialization, GNN-LLM hybrids, incremental GraphRAG: your internship topics.
Module conclusion The seminar had you read ten families of papers with a single grid: claim, evidence, costs, reproducibility, LLGA link. This is the skill the master wants to install: quickly read the essentials, doubt methodically, and connect every advance to the complete system (data → training → serving → evaluation → safety) described by the LLMs, ML Ops and Explainability/Security/Privacy modules. The instructors of this proposed course are not confirmed; the cited papers and standards form the foundation, and the LLGA (LLMs & Graphs) group of the X will provide the guest sessions when relevant.
