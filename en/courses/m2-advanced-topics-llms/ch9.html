9. Evaluation: contamination, judge bias

Today's readings C. Xu, S. Guan, D. Greene, M.-T. Kechadi, Benchmark Data Contamination of Large Language Models: A Survey, 2024 (arXiv:2406.04244); L. Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2306.05685); P. Wang et al., Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators, ACL 2024 (arXiv:2305.17926); P. Liang et al., Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM), TMLR 2023 (arXiv:2211.09110).

9.1. Contamination: when the test leaks into training

Definition 9.1 — Benchmark data contamination (BDC) The pollution of a benchmark when its data (questions and/or answers) have been seen during pre-training — accidental (the web contains the solutions), or even deliberate (training on the test's variant). Signals: n-gram overlaps with the corpus, embedding similarity, abnormally low test perplexity, the ability to complete the items without their instruction, "random" performance on renamed variants (changing the figures of a math problem). Responses: canaries ("do not train on this string"), dynamic benchmarks refreshed (e.g. LiveBench), private sets, kept-current evaluations (challenges with a cutoff date).
Proposition 9.2 — What a contaminated score estimates (and what it does not) A benchmark measures performance on a task distribution \(P_{\text{test}}\). If the model has seen \(n\) examples of \(P_{\text{test}}\) during training, the score observes a mixture \(\rho \cdot \text{memory} + (1-\rho) \cdot \text{generalization}\): the estimand has shifted — the score overestimates and distorts comparisons (the models trained more recently, on larger corpora, are more contaminated: the bias advances with time). The methodological remedy: report jointly (i) the raw score, (ii) the score on re-generated variants, (iii) an overlap measure — and treat the (i)-(ii) gap as an interval, not as noise.

9.2. LLM judges: useful, biased, calibratable

MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) validates the LLM judge: agreement with human preferences is high (GPT-4 as judge ≈ 80%+ of inter-human agreement level), which legitimizes its industrial use (ML Ops module ch. 5). But the biases are systematic: position (favoring the first answer — corrected by alternating order), verbosity (long answers win), self-preference (the judge favors the answers of its own model family). Wang et al. (ACL 2024) show that a simple order swap flips a substantial share of verdicts — "not fair evaluators". HELM (Liang et al.) answers with the holistic: same models, dozens of scenarios, multiple metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, bias, toxicity…), published protocol — evaluation as a slow science versus the leaderboard as a fast sport.

Critical analysis — the "methods" session (i) Goodhart: every public benchmark becomes a training objective — the value of a score decays with its popularity; (ii) Variance: a leaderboard at ±0.5 points with 500 questions ranks nothing (cf. ML Ops module ch. 2, Hoeffding); (iii) Judge the judge: calibrate each judge on a human golden set before using it (measured biases, alternating orders, rubrics); (iv) Saturation: when everyone exceeds 90% accuracy, the benchmark measures noise — move to the dynamic/adversarial evaluations (red teaming of the Security module ch. 10, agents of ch. 8). LLGA rule: no evaluation number without its protocol — neither in the papers you read, nor in your own. 
# N-gram contamination detection (sketch, thresholds to calibrate)
def ngrams(s, n=13):  return {" ".join(s.split()[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(s.split())-n+1)}

def overlap(test_item, pretrain_index):     # pretrain_index: set of shingles
    q = ngrams(test_item.question) | ngrams(test_item.answer)
    return len(q & pretrain_index) / max(len(q), 1)
