Introduction and overview

ModuleFundamentals of Security and Robustness for AI
CodeM2 · P2 (brochure)
Team and workloadInstructor: master's team · ~24 h · the mathematical foundation of learning security: threat model, adversarial examples (FGSM/PGD proven), adversarial training and its cost, certification (randomized smoothing), trojans, poisoning, extraction and membership inference, up to evaluation protocols (RobustBench) and defensive deployment.
Status in the programM2Outside the recommended track

Sometimes a perturbation invisible to the eye suffices for an image classifier to change its mind with confidence: this is the adversarial phenomenon, and it spares neither LLMs nor graph models. This module builds the mathematical foundation of learning security: threat model and taxonomy of attacks, adversarial examples built by optimization (FGSM, PGD, CW, AutoAttack), defenses by adversarial training and its cost, certification by randomized smoothing, then trojans, poisoning, extraction and membership inference. In the LLGA master, it is the course that gives robustness the same mathematical status as accuracy: a criterion with its definitions, its theorems and its evaluation protocols.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the threat model of a system: what the adversary knows, can modify, and seeks to obtain;
  • Build adversarial examples by optimization (FGSM, PGD, CW) and understand what AutoAttack guarantees;
  • Measure the adversarial phenomenon with the right distances and separate the problem from plain classification errors;
  • Train a robust model (adversarial training) and quantify its extra cost in compute and accuracy;
  • Certify robustness by randomized smoothing or formal verification, and state what the certificate covers;
  • Detect and analyze trojans, backdoors and data poisoning;
  • Situate intellectual-property attacks: model stealing, membership inference;
  • Transpose the approach to LLMs and graphs, and read a robustness benchmark (RobustBench) with a critical eye.

Where this course fits in the program

An M2 module offered in period 2, outside the recommended track: it is the "security" foundation on which the specialized modules at the end of the master open. Prerequisites: Deep Learning (gradient training, convolutions — the optimization attacks are grafted onto it); Advanced Graph Neural Networks, whose Nettack attack from ch. 7 is one of the starting points of the "graphs" chapter; and the federated learning part of Emerging ML, where Byzantine servers prepare the poisoning question.

Downstream modules: Explainability, Security, Privacy of LLMs directly takes up this taxonomy to deploy it on LLMs (jailbreaks, injections, defenses in depth, ch. 5–6); the threat-model reflexes also serve the operational guardrails of the ML Ops and LLM Engineering Principles module.

Course outline

  • Recap: the foundation and the vocabulary — attacker, surface, guarantee: the lexicon without which nothing can be discussed.
  • Threat model and taxonomy of attacks — classify attacks by knowledge (white/black/gray box) and by goal.
  • Adversarial examples: the phenomenon and its distances — the experimental phenomenon, and why the choice of distance changes everything.
  • Optimization-based attacks: FGSM, PGD, CW, AutoAttack — the derived and proven attacks, up to standardized evaluation.
  • Defenses: adversarial training — the reference defense, its minimax formulation and its real cost.
  • Certification: randomized smoothing and formal verification — going from "it resists tests" to "it is proven", and under what conditions.
  • Trojans and backdoors — models that behave well until the attacker's chosen trigger.
  • Data poisoning — corrupting learning upstream, when only the data is touched.
  • Model stealing and membership inference — the intellectual-property and confidentiality attacks, a bridge to the LLM privacy module.
  • Beyond images: LLMs and graphs — adversarial prompts, structure attacks: the foundation transported to the master's objects.
  • Evaluating and deploying — rigorous protocols (RobustBench) and defensive deployment decisions.
  • Exercises / Labs — implement FGSM/PGD, adversarial training and a graph attack.
  • References — the founding papers of each family of attacks and defenses.

How to study this module

  • Attack before defending: implement PGD from the very first week — one understands a defense only after failing to break it.
  • For each attack, write formally what the adversary optimizes, under which constraints, with what knowledge: this is the central discipline of the module.
  • Beware of "miracle defenses": train yourself to identify why a defense not evaluated against an adaptive attack proves nothing.
  • Redo the numbers of the cost of robustness (accuracy, training time) on a small model: the trade-off is better seen than read.
  • Reread ch. 7 of Advanced GNN and the Byzantine part of Emerging ML at the start of the module: the graphs and poisoning chapters build directly on them.
The LLGA perspective Robustness is a crossroads of the master: adversarial examples are born on images, and carry over to graphs (structure attacked around a node) and to LLMs (hostile prompts) — the three families of models of the LLGA. Completed by differential privacy and by the dedicated LLM module, this foundation makes you able to audit a learning system, a skill that European regulation now expects from industry as much as from research laboratories.
