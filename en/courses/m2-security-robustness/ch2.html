2. Adversarial examples: the phenomenon and its distances

Definition 2.1 — Adversarial example (Szegedy et al., 2014) For a classifier \(f : \mathcal{X} \to \{1, \dots, K\}\), an adversarial example of \(x\) (of class \(y\)) is an \(\tilde{x} = x + \delta\) such that \(f(\tilde{x}) \neq f(x) = y\) (or a target class \(t\) chosen by the attacker: \(f(\tilde{x}) = t\)) while \(\|\delta\|\) is "small" in the sense of a distance to be fixed. Szegedy et al. showed that these points exist at surprising density around every test point, for networks with very high accuracy — they are not isolated misclassified points, but imperceptible perturbations of correctly classified points.
Definition 2.2 — The three useful balls $$ \|\delta\|_0 = |\{j : \delta_j \neq 0\}| \quad (\text{modified pixels}), \qquad \|\delta\|_2 = \Big(\sum_j \delta_j^2\Big)^{1/2} \quad (\text{energy}), \qquad \|\delta\|_\infty = \max_j |\delta_j| \quad (\text{max amplitude}). $$ The associated balls \(B_p(x, \varepsilon)\) define the attacker's budget. Standard protocols: \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) in \(L_\infty\) on MNIST (pixels in \([0,1]\)), \(\varepsilon = 8/255\) on CIFAR-10 (ch. 10). Each norm models a different threat: sporadically altered sensors (\(L_0\)), physical noise (\(L_2\)), brightness/contrast (\(L_\infty\)).
Proposition 2.3 — The linear hypothesis (Goodfellow et al., 2015) Even a linear classifier admits adversarial examples in high dimension. For \(f(x) = w^\top x + b\) (the score of one class), and \(\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\): $$ |f(x + \delta) - f(x)| = |w^\top \delta| \leq \varepsilon\, \|w\|_1. $$ In dimension \(d\) with typical weights \(|w_j| \approx m\), the shift reaches \(\varepsilon\, m\, d\): each coordinate only "moves" by \(\varepsilon\), but the \(d\) contributions add up. With \(d \sim 10^5\) pixels, a relative perturbation of \(1\%\) per coordinate suffices to change the score by several logistic units.
Upper bound: Hölder's inequality \( |w^\top \delta| \leq \|w\|_1 \|\delta\|_\infty\), attained for \(\delta = \varepsilon\, \mathrm{sign}(w)\). The order of magnitude follows by taking \(\|w\|_1 \approx m d\). ∎
Transfer and the physical world Two properties make the threat practical: (i) transfer — an adversarial example computed on one (substitute) model often fools another model, including one with a different architecture: the black-box attack reduces to the white-box case; (ii) the physical world — Kurakin et al. (2017) printed adversarial images: the perturbation survives printing, photography and change of viewpoint. Adversarial examples on tabular data also exist (transaction amounts adjusted below the thresholds of detection rules); note that on GBDTs, since the function is piecewise constant, gradient-based attacks do not apply directly — one then attacks by discrete search or via a differentiable substitute.
