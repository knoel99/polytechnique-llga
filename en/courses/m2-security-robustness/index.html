Sometimes a perturbation invisible to the eye suffices for an image classifier to change its mind with confidence: this is the adversarial phenomenon, and it spares neither LLMs nor graph models. This module builds the mathematical foundation of learning security: threat model and taxonomy of attacks, adversarial examples built by optimization (FGSM, PGD, CW, AutoAttack), defenses by adversarial training and its cost, certification by randomized smoothing, then trojans, poisoning, extraction and membership inference. In the LLGA master, it is the course that gives robustness the same mathematical status as accuracy: a criterion with its definitions, its theorems and its evaluation protocols.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the threat model of a system: what the adversary knows, can modify, and seeks to obtain;
  • Build adversarial examples by optimization (FGSM, PGD, CW) and understand what AutoAttack guarantees;
  • Measure the adversarial phenomenon with the right distances and separate the problem from plain classification errors;
  • Train a robust model (adversarial training) and quantify its extra cost in compute and accuracy;
  • Certify robustness by randomized smoothing or formal verification, and state what the certificate covers;
  • Detect and analyze trojans, backdoors and data poisoning;
  • Situate intellectual-property attacks: model stealing, membership inference;
  • Transpose the approach to LLMs and graphs, and read a robustness benchmark (RobustBench) with a critical eye.

How to study this module

  • Attack before defending: implement PGD from the very first week — one understands a defense only after failing to break it.
  • For each attack, write formally what the adversary optimizes, under which constraints, with what knowledge: this is the central discipline of the module.
  • Beware of "miracle defenses": train yourself to identify why a defense not evaluated against an adaptive attack proves nothing.
  • Redo the numbers of the cost of robustness (accuracy, training time) on a small model: the trade-off is better seen than read.
  • Reread ch. 7 of Advanced GNN and the Byzantine part of Emerging ML at the start of the module: the graphs and poisoning chapters build directly on them.
The LLGA perspective Robustness is a crossroads of the master: adversarial examples are born on images, and carry over to graphs (structure attacked around a node) and to LLMs (hostile prompts) — the three families of models of the LLGA. Completed by differential privacy and by the dedicated LLM module, this foundation makes you able to audit a learning system, a skill that European regulation now expects from industry as much as from research laboratories.

0. Recap: the foundation and the vocabulary

Prerequisites (M1/M2 modules)

  • Generalization (cf. Machine Learning module ch. 7): empirical risk vs true risk, overfitting — the vocabulary to understand why robustness generalizes poorly (ch. 4);
  • Statistics (Refresher Statistics): hypothesis testing, type I/II errors, the Neyman-Pearson lemma — the hidden tool behind randomized smoothing (ch. 5) and membership inference (ch. 8);
  • Differential privacy (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 5): the \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) definition, Gaussian mechanism, composition — reused without re-proving in ch. 7-8;
  • Byzantine robustness and federated learning (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 4): malicious clients, robust aggregation — ch. 7 is its "centralized data" counterpart;
  • LLM security (cf. Explainability, Security and Privacy of LLMs module): jailbreaks, injections, extraction — ch. 9 systematically refers to that module.
Definition 0.1 — Robustness, security, safety Three properties that are often confused: robustness is a mathematical property of the model (bounding the variation of the output under a constraint on the input); security is a property of the system faced with an intentional attacker (it requires a threat model); safety covers even unintentional harm (bias, in-distribution errors). This module covers the first two; the third crosses over with the Responsible AI and Explainability modules. A model can be robust and not safe, and vice versa.
Module method Each chapter follows the same grid: (i) formalize the attack as an optimization problem; (ii) prove what is provable (FGSM in ch. 3, the hidden regularization of adversarial training in ch. 4, the certified radius in ch. 5); (iii) give the standard defense and the way it breaks — a defense not evaluated by adaptive attack is not a defense. Labs on MNIST/CIFAR (FGSM, PGD, adversarial training) then full audits.

1. Threat model and taxonomy of attacks

Definition 1.1 — Threat model Every security analysis starts with three explicit assumptions: the attacker's capabilities — white-box (architecture and weights known), gray-box (architecture known, weights not), black-box (access only to the predictions, possibly limited in queries or without scores); the timing of the attack — at training time (poisoning, backdoor) or at inference time (evasion, extraction, membership inference); the goal — untargeted error, targeted error (one specific class), information leakage, theft of intellectual property, denial of service. Changing one assumption changes the whole analysis: most misunderstandings in the field come from different implicit threat models.

Reference framework This taxonomy (evasion / poisoning / extraction / inference) is the one from the survey by Biggio & Roli (Wild Patterns: Ten Years After the Rise of Adversarial Machine Learning, 2018), which also structures the evaluation frameworks (ch. 10). The important conceptual point: unlike classical software security (hunting for bugs), here the attack surface is the prediction function itself — every non-trivial classifier has decision boundaries, and boundaries have two sides.

2. Adversarial examples: the phenomenon and its distances

Definition 2.1 — Adversarial example (Szegedy et al., 2014) For a classifier \(f : \mathcal{X} \to \{1, \dots, K\}\), an adversarial example of \(x\) (of class \(y\)) is an \(\tilde{x} = x + \delta\) such that \(f(\tilde{x}) \neq f(x) = y\) (or a target class \(t\) chosen by the attacker: \(f(\tilde{x}) = t\)) while \(\|\delta\|\) is "small" in the sense of a distance to be fixed. Szegedy et al. showed that these points exist at surprising density around every test point, for networks with very high accuracy — they are not isolated misclassified points, but imperceptible perturbations of correctly classified points.
Definition 2.2 — The three useful balls $$ \|\delta\|_0 = |\{j : \delta_j \neq 0\}| \quad (\text{modified pixels}), \qquad \|\delta\|_2 = \Big(\sum_j \delta_j^2\Big)^{1/2} \quad (\text{energy}), \qquad \|\delta\|_\infty = \max_j |\delta_j| \quad (\text{max amplitude}). $$ The associated balls \(B_p(x, \varepsilon)\) define the attacker's budget. Standard protocols: \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) in \(L_\infty\) on MNIST (pixels in \([0,1]\)), \(\varepsilon = 8/255\) on CIFAR-10 (ch. 10). Each norm models a different threat: sporadically altered sensors (\(L_0\)), physical noise (\(L_2\)), brightness/contrast (\(L_\infty\)).
Proposition 2.3 — The linear hypothesis (Goodfellow et al., 2015) Even a linear classifier admits adversarial examples in high dimension. For \(f(x) = w^\top x + b\) (the score of one class), and \(\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\): $$ |f(x + \delta) - f(x)| = |w^\top \delta| \leq \varepsilon\, \|w\|_1. $$ In dimension \(d\) with typical weights \(|w_j| \approx m\), the shift reaches \(\varepsilon\, m\, d\): each coordinate only "moves" by \(\varepsilon\), but the \(d\) contributions add up. With \(d \sim 10^5\) pixels, a relative perturbation of \(1\%\) per coordinate suffices to change the score by several logistic units.
Upper bound: Hölder's inequality \( |w^\top \delta| \leq \|w\|_1 \|\delta\|_\infty\), attained for \(\delta = \varepsilon\, \mathrm{sign}(w)\). The order of magnitude follows by taking \(\|w\|_1 \approx m d\). ∎
Transfer and the physical world Two properties make the threat practical: (i) transfer — an adversarial example computed on one (substitute) model often fools another model, including one with a different architecture: the black-box attack reduces to the white-box case; (ii) the physical world — Kurakin et al. (2017) printed adversarial images: the perturbation survives printing, photography and change of viewpoint. Adversarial examples on tabular data also exist (transaction amounts adjusted below the thresholds of detection rules); note that on GBDTs, since the function is piecewise constant, gradient-based attacks do not apply directly — one then attacks by discrete search or via a differentiable substitute.

3. Optimization-based attacks: FGSM, PGD, CW, AutoAttack

Definition 3.1 — The evasion problem Untargeted attack with budget \(\varepsilon\) (norm \(p\)): $$ \max_{\delta}\ \ell(f_\theta(x + \delta),\, y) \quad \text{subject to}\quad \|\delta\|_p \leq \varepsilon,\ x + \delta \in [0,1]^d, $$ where \(\ell\) is the cross-entropy. Targeted version: maximize \(\ell(f(x+\delta), y) - \ell(f(x+\delta), t)\) (push the target class \(t\) up and the true one down). The whole difficulty is the non-concave inner max — it cannot be evaluated exactly, only lower-bounded (attacks) or overestimated.
Theorem 3.2 — FGSM = linearization of the problem (Goodfellow et al., 2015) To first order in \(\delta\), \(\ell(x + \delta) \approx \ell(x) + \nabla_x \ell(x)^\top \delta\). The maximum of this linear approximation over the \(L_\infty\) ball is attained at $$ \delta^\star = \varepsilon\, \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x \ell(x)\big) \qquad (\text{Fast Gradient Sign Method}), $$ with increase \(\varepsilon\, \|\nabla_x \ell(x)\|_1\). Over the \(L_2\) ball: \(\delta^\star = \varepsilon\, \nabla_x \ell(x) / \|\nabla_x \ell(x)\|_2\) (FGM).
Hölder: for every \(\delta\) with \(\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon\), \(g^\top \delta \leq \sum_j |g_j|\,|\delta_j| \leq \varepsilon \sum_j |g_j| = \varepsilon \|g\|_1\), with \(g = \nabla_x \ell(x)\). There is equality iff \(|\delta_j| = \varepsilon\) and \(\mathrm{sign}(\delta_j) = \mathrm{sign}(g_j)\) for every nonzero \(j\) — which is exactly \(\delta^\star\). For \(L_2\): Cauchy-Schwarz \(g^\top\delta \leq \|g\|_2\|\delta\|_2 \leq \varepsilon\|g\|_2\), equality iff \(\delta\) is collinear with \(g\). ∎
Definition 3.3 — PGD (Madry et al., 2018) The linearization is only valid locally; one iterates while re-projecting onto the ball and onto \([0,1]^d\) (projection \(=\) clamping \(\min(\max(x+\delta - \varepsilon, x - \varepsilon), x + \varepsilon)\) for \(L_\infty\)): $$ \delta^{(k+1)} = \Pi_{B_\infty(\varepsilon)}\Big(\delta^{(k)} + \alpha\, \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x \ell(x + \delta^{(k)})\big)\Big), \qquad \delta^{(0)} \in \{\,0,\ \text{or uniform in } B_\infty(\varepsilon)\,\}, $$ with step size \(\alpha \approx 2.5\,\varepsilon/K\) for \(K\) iterations, and multiple restarts (the inner objective is non-concave: several starting points are sampled). PGD is the qualitative standard: "robust to PGD" has become a (too convenient) synonym for robust.
Definition 3.4 — CW (Carlini & Wagner, 2017) Attack optimized on the logit margin \(Z(x)\), with the change of variable \(\delta = \tfrac{1}{2}(\tanh(w) + 1) - x\) to get rid of the box constraints: $$ \min_{w}\ \Big\| \tfrac{1}{2}(\tanh(w)+1) - x \Big\|_2^2 + c \cdot \max\big(Z(x+\delta)_y - \max_{t \neq y} Z(x+\delta)_t,\ -\kappa\big), $$ with a bisection search over \(c\) and confidence \(\kappa \geq 0\). CW-L2 long remained the most effective attack — and the tool that demolished the first generation of defenses (ch. 4).
Black box and standard evaluation Without gradients: (i) transfer (ch. 2); (ii) score-based attacks (gradient estimation by queries, random square attacks); (iii) decision-based attacks (random walk on the boundary). For evaluation, one no longer uses a single isolated attack but AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, 2020): a parameter-free ensemble — APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square — which cuts the displayed "robustness" of several defended models by more than 10 points. Rule of the module: any robustness measure is worth exactly the worst attack that has been tried. 
# FGSM and PGD in about ten lines (PyTorch)
def fgsm(model, x, y, eps):
    x.requires_grad_(True)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    return (x + eps * x.grad.sign()).clamp(0, 1).detach()

def pgd(model, x, y, eps, alpha, iters, restarts=1):
    worst, worst_loss = None, float("-inf")
    for _ in range(restarts):
        d = torch.zeros_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)      # random start
        for _ in range(iters):
            d.requires_grad_(True)
            loss = F.cross_entropy(model((x + d).clamp(0, 1)), y)
            g = torch.autograd.grad(loss, d)[0]          # gradient of the inner max
            with torch.no_grad():
                d = (d + alpha * g.sign()).clamp(-eps, eps)
        x_adv = (x + d).clamp(0, 1).detach()
        l = F.cross_entropy(model(x_adv), y).item()      # candidate loss
        if l > worst_loss:                               # keep the worst case
            worst, worst_loss = x_adv, l
    return worst

4. Defenses: adversarial training

Definition 4.1 — Adversarial training (Madry et al., 2018) One minimizes the robust risk, a min-max problem: $$ \min_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\Big[\ \max_{\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon} \ell\big(f_\theta(x + \delta),\, y\big)\ \Big], $$ the inner max being approximated by PGD (def. 3.3): at each descent step, each example is replaced by its current adversarial version. This is a form of stochastic minimax; in practice: training cost multiplied by the number of PGD iterations (typically 7-10). 
# full lab: PGD-AT on MNIST (the min-max, line by line)
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

model = nn.Sequential(                                 # small CNN
    nn.Conv2d(1, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Conv2d(32, 64, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(64 * 7 * 7, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 10))

def pgd(model, x, y, eps, steps, alpha):               # the inner MAX (def. 3.3)
    d = torch.zeros_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)        # random start
    for _ in range(steps):
        d.requires_grad_(True)
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model((x + d).clamp(0, 1)), y)
        g = torch.autograd.grad(loss, d)[0].sign()
        with torch.no_grad():
            d = (d + alpha * g).clamp(-eps, eps)
    return (x + d).clamp(0, 1).detach()

opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
eps, steps, alpha = 0.3, 7, 0.3 * 2.5 / 7             # Madry protocol
for epoch in range(10):
    for x, y in train_loader:
        x_adv = pgd(model, x, y, eps, steps, alpha)    # approximate the max...
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x_adv), y)        # ...then minimize (outer MIN)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

def accuracy(model, x, y, attack=None):                # honest evaluation
    xb = x if attack is None else attack(model, x, y)
    return (model(xb).argmax(1) == y).float().mean().item()

Theorem 4.2 — The min-max, proven on a linear case For a linear classifier \(f(x) = w^\top x + b\) and the hinge loss \(\ell(y\, f(x)) = \max(0,\ 1 - y\, f(x))\) (labels \(y \in \{\pm1\}\)), the \(L_\infty\) robust risk reads $$ \max_{\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon}\ \max\big(0,\ 1 - y\,(w^\top(x+\delta) + b)\big) \;=\; \max\big(0,\ 1 - y\,(w^\top x + b) + \varepsilon \|w\|_1\big). $$ Adversarial training of a linear model with hinge loss is therefore exactly an \(L_1\)-regularized SVM regression with coefficient \(\varepsilon\): robustness acts as a penalty on the weights.
The max of \(1 - y\,(w^\top x + b) - y\, w^\top \delta\) over the ball is attained (Hölder, cf. proof of thm. 3.2) at \(\delta^\star = -\varepsilon\, y\, \mathrm{sign}(w)\), and equals \(1 - y(w^\top x + b) + \varepsilon \|w\|_1\). If this quantity is negative, the robust hinge loss equals \(0\) — hence the global \(\max(0, \cdot)\): the outer and inner max commute because the hinge is increasing. Summing over the examples gives \(\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i f(x_i) + \varepsilon\|w\|_1)\): the term \(\varepsilon \|w\|_1\) does not depend on \(i\), it is the \(L_1\) penalty. ∎
What Theorem 4.2 teaches (i) robustness has a statistical price: penalizing \(w\) reduces the effective margin, hence the standard accuracy — the robustness/accuracy trade-off is not a bug; (ii) the scale of \(w\) is the real lever: hence in practice bounded weights, input normalization, shared weights (cf. Deep Learning modules, regularization chapter). Schmidt et al. (2019) formalize point (i): robust generalization requires more data than standard generalization.
Decomposition of the robust error (TRADES, Zhang et al., 2019) For every classifier, the robust error (worst case within the balls) decomposes as $$ \mathcal{R}_{rob} \;\leq\; \mathcal{R}_{nat} \;+\; \mathcal{R}_{bdy}, $$ natural error + "boundary error" (points correctly classified but within \(\varepsilon\) of a decision boundary). TRADES explicitly optimizes the second term via a KL term between \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+\delta)\) — and dominates pure PGD-AT on several benchmarks. "Naive" adversarial training maximizes the margin; TRADES shows that one must also smooth the model.
Definition 4.3 — Robust overfitting (Rice et al., 2020) A specific phenomenon: during adversarial training, test robustness peaks abruptly (often within the first few dozen epochs) then decreases while train robustness keeps rising — a robust overfitting, mitigated (not solved) by early stopping and massive data augmentation (Rebuffi et al., 2021). Counter-intuitive but systematically reproduced: it is one of the most solid experimental facts in the field.
How not to defend The 2017-2019 decade produced hundreds of "masked-gradient" defenses (shrinkage, discretization, derandomization...) broken as soon as they were attacked adaptively: Athalye et al. (2018) ("Obfuscated gradients give a false sense of security") break several of them with a backward-pass attack (backward-pass differentiable approximation) and reparameterization; Tramèr et al. (2020) codify the answer: every defense must be evaluated by an attack that knows it. Lab 3 enforces this protocol. For networks already deployed without retraining: detection (filters, distribution detection), rejection/abstention, preprocessing (quantization, JPEG compression, total-variation denoising — Guo et al., 2017); useful against transferred attacks, these "palliative" defenses are systematically bypassed by an attack that knows the filter — always test against an adapted attack.

5. Certification: randomized smoothing and formal verification

Chapters 3-4 bound robustness from below (attacks) — no guarantee about what has not been found. Certification bounds it from above: a radius within which no perturbation of a given budget can change the prediction.

Definition 5.1 — Randomized smoothing (Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, 2019) Let \(f\) be a base classifier and \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\). The smoothed classifier is $$ g(x) = \arg\max_{c}\ \mathbb{P}\big(f(x + \varepsilon) = c\big), $$ the most likely class when the input is perturbed with Gaussian noise. \(g\) inherits a tight \(L_2\) guarantee (see thm. 5.2), computable for any black-box \(f\) — it is the only certification method that has scaled (ImageNet).
Theorem 5.2 — Certified radius (Cohen et al., 2019) Let \(c_A = g(x)\), \(p_A = \mathbb{P}(f(x + \varepsilon) = c_A)\) and \(p_B = \max_{c \neq c_A} \mathbb{P}(f(x + \varepsilon) = c)\). If one knows bounds \(\underline{p_A} \leq p_A\) and \(p_B \leq \overline{p_B}\) with \(\underline{p_A} > \overline{p_B}\), then \(g\) predicts \(c_A\) over the entire \(L_2\) ball of radius $$ R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\Big), $$ where \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the inverse of the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function.
(Sketch — the full version is in the paper.) Separation lemma: for every Borel set \(S\) and every \(\delta\), if \(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2I)}(\varepsilon \in S) \geq p\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon \in S - \delta) \leq q\), then \(\|\delta\|_2 \geq \tfrac{\sigma}{2}(\Phi^{-1}(p) - \Phi^{-1}(q))\). Proof of the lemma: the most powerful test for distinguishing \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2I)\) from \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\delta, \sigma^2 I)\) (Neyman-Pearson lemma) is thresholding the likelihood ratio \(-\delta^\top \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2}\|\delta\|_2^2\) — Gaussian linear, hence \(\delta^\top \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2\|\delta\|_2^2)\). Express the two probabilities via \(\Phi\) and eliminate the threshold: this yields the crude bound \(\|\delta\|_2 \geq \sigma\,(\Phi^{-1}(p) - \Phi^{-1}(q))\); the tight analysis of Cohen et al. then refines the constant to \(\tfrac{\sigma}{2}\) (the factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) comes from the optimal allocation of the budget between the two hypotheses). Application: set \(S = f^{-1}(c_A)\); \(p_A = \mathbb{P}(x + \varepsilon \in S)\) and every point of the ball \(B_2(x, R)\) satisfies \(\mathbb{P}(\cdot \in S) > \mathbb{P}(\cdot \in S')\) for every competing class \(S'\) — class \(c_A\) remains the majority, so \(g\) equals \(c_A\) there. ∎
Example 5.3 — Certification in practice \(p_A, p_B\) are unknown: they are estimated by Monte Carlo — \(n_0\) draws to choose \(g(x)\), \(n\) draws to build a binomial confidence interval (exact: Clopper-Pearson) on \(p_A\); \(\overline{p_B}\) is taken as one minus that bound. The procedure is sound with probability \(1 - \alpha\) — the final guarantee combines sampling randomness and a deterministic theorem. One then plots certified accuracy vs radius: a curve necessarily below the empirical curve (attacks), the gap measuring our ignorance. Precious link: randomized smoothing is the classification analogue of the Gaussian mechanism of DP (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 5); the "PixelDP" variant (Lecuyer et al., 2019) makes it explicit.
Deterministic certification For ReLU networks, local robustness is decidable: encode the network and the ball as arithmetic constraints (mixed linear/ReLU) and ask a solver — Reluplex (Katz et al., 2017) extends the simplex to ReLU constraints and has verified aircraft collision-avoidance networks. Complete but exponential: in practice one uses incomplete methods (interval bounds, zonotopes) that quickly certify a smaller region. Decision trees: trivial exact certification by enumerating the leaves reachable within the ball — one of the rare cases where "classical" tabular is structurally more certifiable than deep.

6. Trojans and backdoors

Definition 6.1 — Backdoor / Trojan horse (Gu et al., 2017) A backdoored model \(f\) behaves normally on clean inputs, but an \(\mathcal{A}(x)\) carrying the trigger (patch, high-frequency pattern, watermark) is classified into the target class: $$ f(x) \approx f_{clean}(x) \ \text{on the clean distribution}, \qquad f(x + \tau) = t \ \text{almost always}. $$ Unlike evasion (ch. 2-3), the attack is cast into training: BadNets obtains it by contaminating a fraction of the training data (legitimate examples + trigger + target label) — validation accuracy remains excellent, which makes the contamination undetectable by standard metrics.
Example 6.2 — Clean-label (Shafahi et al., 2018) "Poison Frogs": the attacker controls no labels. One fabricates a single poisoned example: starting from an image of the target class \(t\), it is optimized to enter into a feature collision with the victim image (close in the latent space of a pre-trained network) while remaining visibly of class \(t\). After fine-tuning, the victim (a clean image!) is classified as \(t\). A single data point suffices in transfer — the worst nightmare of collaborative data pipelines; cf. Emerging ML module ch. 4 for the federated case (malicious clients) and ch. 7 for poisoning at scale.
Definition 6.3 — Detection: trigger reverse-engineering (Neural Cleanse, Wang et al., 2019) For each class \(c\), one optimizes the minimal perturbation \(\delta_c\) that forces all inputs toward \(c\); a backdoored model exhibits an anomaly: for the target class \(t\), \(\|\delta_t\|_1\) is noticeably smaller (the existing trigger provides a "shortcut"). The anomaly is detected by median absolute deviation (MAD), the trigger is reconstructed, then one repairs: pruning the hyperactive neurons under the trigger, or fine-tuning/unlearning on the reconstructed inputs.
Limits and practice Existing defenses assume small, fixed, patch-shaped triggers: adaptive triggers (distributed, input-dependent, semantic — "a cap" — or sample-specific) escape reverse-engineering. The second family of detection is spectral (Spectral Signatures, Tran, Li & Madry, NeurIPS 2018): backdoored examples form a cluster in the space of last-layer activations — outliers are isolated in the principal directions (SVD of the activation matrix) and removed from the training set; complementary to reverse-engineering (it assumes no trigger shape), but broken by attacks that minimize their spectral signature. Deployment rules: traceable data chain, audited source models, dedicated tests ("does the model react oddly to this pattern?"), and above all monitoring the region around the model (ch. 10); cf. Explainability, Security and Privacy of LLMs module ch. 5 for the LLM variant (instruction poisoning).

7. Data poisoning

Definition 7.1 — Poisoning The attacker modifies a fraction \(\rho\) of the training set before learning. Variants: label flipping (\((x_i, y_i) \to (x_i, 1 - y_i)\); or chosen "optimal" flips), feature manipulation (\(x_i \to x_i + \delta_i\), labels intact — covers the clean-label attack of ch. 6), and insertion of forged examples. Goal: global degradation, targeted bias on a subgroup, or a backdoor. The optimal attack (Biggio et al., 2012, on SVMs) maximizes the attacker's validation risk on the contaminated data — solved by deriving the optimality condition (gradient of the final model with respect to the poison point, via the implicit function theorem).
Proposition 7.2 — Where flipping matters With a fixed budget of flips, the impact on the risk is maximized by moving the boundary as close as possible to well-classified points with a large margin: flipping a point very close to the boundary moves the classifier little (it was already almost an error); flipping points deep inside a class pulls the boundary toward the dense zone of the other class. Empirical consequence: a few percent of "smart" flips degrade more than many random flips — and strong regularization dampens both (the regularized classifier "resists" isolated points).
Definition 7.3 — Influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) The influence of a training point \(z\) on the prediction at \(x_{test}\) measures what would happen if \(z\) were upweighted by an \(\varepsilon \to 0\): $$ \mathcal{I}(z, x_{test}) = -\nabla_\theta \ell(x_{test};\hat\theta)^\top H_{\hat\theta}^{-1}\, \nabla_\theta \ell(z; \hat\theta), \qquad H_{\hat\theta} = \nabla_\theta^2 \hat R(\hat\theta), $$ estimable without retraining (Hessian-vector products, LiSSA). It is the "defensive" tool: poisons with strong negative influence are candidates for audit/removal — and the symmetric offensive tool: crafting poisons with maximal influence. In practice: filtering by influence, anomaly detection in the gradients, truncated losses (robust to outliers), and for the distributed setting, Byzantine aggregates (cf. Emerging ML module ch. 4: trimmed mean, coordinate-wise median...).
Example 7.4 — Data hygiene, the real defense Deduplication (poisons are often amplified duplicates), human validation of examples with high loss + high influence, provenance (data lineage, cf. ML Ops module), and trust budgets: every external source enters with a fraction cap. DP (Emerging ML module ch. 5) bounds by construction the effect of an individual point — an \(\varepsilon\)-DP robust to poisoning at the price of utility: the trade-off is the same as in ch. 8 for privacy.

8. Model stealing and membership inference

Definition 8.1 — Extraction / model stealing The attacker queries a prediction API to reconstruct the model (or a functional equivalent). Two regimes (Jagielski et al., 2020): high accuracy (a copy that predicts well on the task — functional counterfeiting) or high fidelity (a copy that imitates the boundary, including its errors — to build transferable adversarial examples).
Proposition 8.2 — Exact extraction of a logistic regression (Tramèr et al., 2016) If the API exposes \(p(y=1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)\), then \(d + 1\) queries on linearly independent \(x\)'s suffice to recover \((w, b)\) exactly.
From \(p = \sigma(w^\top x + b)\), one gets \(\mathrm{logit}(p) = \log \frac{p}{1-p} = w^\top x + b\) (the API gives \(p\), the logit is a function of \(p\)). Each query therefore provides a linear equation in \((w, b)\); with \(d+1\) independent points (with affine coordinate 1), the linear system has a unique solution. If the API rounds the probabilities, each equation becomes a bounding interval — one solves by linear programming, with a polynomial number of additional queries. ∎
Definition 8.3 — Membership inference (MIA, Shokri et al., 2017) The attacker wants to decide whether an individual \((x, y)\) was part of the training set — a confidentiality attack (membership itself can be sensitive: medical record, fraud). Canonical attack: train shadow models on similar data, label member/non-member, learn this membership classifier on the confidence scores. Modern version (Carlini et al., 2022, "LiRA"): likelihood ratio test — for each target, an ensemble of shadow models with/without the target estimates the two logit densities \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{in}, \sigma_{in}^2)\), \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{out}, \sigma_{out}^2)\); the optimal test is applied. Honest metric: TPR at low FPR (0.1\%) — that is where membership is dangerous.
Defenses and links Reduce the exposed precision (top-1 only, rounding — partial effectiveness, cf. prop. 8.2), limit/monitor queries (model stealing is a volume attack), model watermarking (cf. LLMs / Explainability-Security-Privacy module ch. 9); the only strong guarantee is training-time DP (DP-SGD, cf. Emerging ML module ch. 5; Privacy and Uncertainty M2 module): an \((\varepsilon, \delta)\) bounds the advantage of any MIA — at the price of a utility loss to be budgeted. MIA is also the auditing tool for synthetic data (cf. Boosting/Foundation Tabular module ch. 8): same box, same tool, two communities.

9. Beyond images: LLMs and graphs

Definition 9.1 — LLM robustness (overview) Attacks move from the pixel to the token: jailbreaks by suffixing (concatenate a universal adversarial suffix optimized by gradient — GCG, Zou et al., 2023: the same mathematics as PGD, on discrete tokens), prompt injections (the payload sits in a document read by the agent), poisoning of the fine-tuning corpus or of RLHF preferences. The Explainability, Security and Privacy of LLMs module (M2) covers all of this in depth; we retain here only the mathematical continuity: maximize \(P(\text{harmful output} \mid x + \delta)\) under discrete constraints — hence coordinate-wise optimizations and tree searches rather than gradient signs.
Case study 9.2 — Graphs: Nettack (Zügner et al., 2018) GNNs inherit a relational attack surface: the attacker modifies edges, node features, or other nodes in the same component. Nettack perturbs the structure and features of a citation graph to change the classification of a target node, by greedy search on marginal gains — with a direct effect (neighborhood) and an indirect one (perturbations of the neighbors of neighbors modify the aggregations): the classification of a node flips with a few dozen modifications invisible at the scale of the graph. Non-robustness is aggravated by propagation; the dedicated defenses (certification by propagation, robust learning) are covered in ch. 7 of the Advanced GNN module (M2) — this chapter only lays down the prerequisites.
And tabular models? On GBDTs (piecewise constant), no gradient: threshold attacks (moving \(x_j\) just beyond the active splits), differentiable substitutes, or discrete search dominate — and exact certification by leaf enumeration (ch. 5) is trivial there. Robustness therefore depends strongly on the model family: the same system can be vulnerable via its scoring network and certifiable via its GBDT — one more argument for fallback architectures (ch. 10).

10. Evaluating and deploying

Definition 10.1 — RobustBench (Croce et al., 2021) Standardized protocol: fixed threats per dataset (\(L_\infty\), \(\varepsilon = 8/255\) on CIFAR-10; \(L_2\); self-supervised attacks), evaluation by AutoAttack + adapted attacks, public leaderboard, reproducible models and details. Reporting rules: standard AND robust accuracy, attack budget, number of restarts, training cost. Without these elements, a robustness number is not information.
In-depth deployment (layered defense)
  1. Reduce the surface: do not expose full scores (ch. 8), query quotas, query monitoring (volumes, distributions);
  2. Detect: adversarial-example detectors, drift/OOD detection (cf. ML Ops module): rejection is an honest defense;
  3. Resist: adversarial training (ch. 4) or certification (ch. 5) where the risk justifies it — otherwise: a secondary "hardened" version;
  4. Limit the damage: fallback models (falling back to a certifiable GBDT or rules), confinement of agent actions (ch. 9), "human in the loop" for sensitive decisions;
  5. Trace and respond: logs of suspicious adversarial queries, post-mortems, adversarial tests in the CI — treat the attack as a reproducible bug;
  6. Framework: the AI Act (EU 2024/1689) classifies critical systems as high risk (ch. 10 of the Explainability-Security-Privacy module for the LLM variant); the NIST AI RMF provides the governance vocabulary.
Conclusion of the module The security of learning is not a property one adds at the end: it is an optimization constraint (min-max, ch. 4), a statistical guarantee (certification, ch. 5) and a process (protocols, monitoring, ch. 10) — on a common foundation: make the threat model explicit before anything else (ch. 1). The three "R"s to remember: Realistic (the adaptive attacker), Rigorous (proven attacks and guarantees), Reproducible (standardized evaluations).

Exercises / Labs

  1. FGSM/PGD on MNIST (ch. 2-3 — opening lab). Train a small CNN; implement FGSM and PGD (thm. 3.2, def. 3.3); plot accuracy vs \(\varepsilon\) for \(K \in \{1, 7, 20, 50\}\) steps and 1/5/10 restarts; visualize the examples (MNIST grid at \(\varepsilon \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3\}\)); measure white→black transfer to a second model (ch. 2).
    Guidance (expected results): clean CNN ≈ 99%; at \(\varepsilon = 0.3\), FGSM drops accuracy below 10% (each pixel moves by a third of the dynamic range — thm. 3.2 in action); \(K = 1\) (one-step PGD, null start) ≈ FGSM; the gain saturates quickly in \(K\) (7 steps suffice), restarts add only a few tenths of a point at this budget — the real role of restarts appears on defended models (lab 2-3); transfer degrades but does not disappear (30-60% of the attack carries over to the target model): the "black box by secrecy of the weights" defense is futile. ∎
  2. Adversarial training (ch. 4 — central lab). PGD-AT (def. 4.1, 7 steps) on MNIST then CIFAR-10 at \(\varepsilon = 8/255\); plot standard and robust accuracy per epoch on the same figure — highlight the robust overfitting (def. 4.3) and the trade-off of thm. 4.2; compare to TRADES (error decomposition); report the RobustBench grid (standard vs robust).
    Guidance (expected results): MNIST — standard accuracy ~98% (the trade-off of thm. 4.2 is small), robust at \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) raised from ~0% to ≈ 90%; CIFAR-10 — standard ~95% → ~80% with AT, robust at 8/255 from ~0% to 45-50% (more with the augmentations of Rebuffi et al., 2021); robust overfitting is visible on CIFAR: test robustness peaks around the middle of training then decreases by several points while train robustness rises — early stopping and massive augmentation mitigate it (def. 4.3); TRADES gains a few robust points at equal standard by reading the boundary error. ∎
  3. Standardized evaluation (ch. 3, 10). Take the models from lab 2 and evaluate them with the AutoAttack library (APGD-CE, APGD-T, Square); compare to the "home-grown" PGD numbers; discuss the gap — then break a naive defense (e.g. re-training on FGSM adversaries only) with an adaptive attack (Athalye/Tramèr, ch. 4) and write the evaluation report in RobustBench format.
    Guidance (expected results): on an adversarially trained model, AutoAttack and the home-grown PGD are close (the model has no masked gradient — a gap on the order of one point); on the naive defense (trained against FGSM only), the "home-grown" PGD overestimates robustness by tens of points and AutoAttack reveals the collapse: FGSM training masks the gradient (piecewise degraded, thm. 3.2 trapped) — the adaptive attack (reparameterization, smoothing) restores it; the honest report: standard accuracy, robust, budget \(\varepsilon\), attack, restarts, cost — without these elements, no conclusion (def. 10.1). ∎
  4. Certification (ch. 5). Implement randomized smoothing: estimation of \(g(x)\) and of the bounds \(\underline{p_A}, \overline{p_B}\) (Clopper-Pearson, example 5.3) on MNIST at \(\sigma \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1.0\}\); plot certified accuracy vs radius (thm. 5.2) and overlay it with the empirical accuracy (\(L_2\) attacks); discuss the certification/empirical gap and the \(\sigma\) accuracy/radius trade-off.
    Guidance (expected results): at \(\sigma = 0.25\), the smoothed classifier keeps a high clean accuracy but the certified radius quickly vanishes (R_max ≈ 0.3-0.4 on MNIST); at \(\sigma = 1.0\), the certified radius reaches ~1 but the clean accuracy of the smoothed classifier drops — the certified accuracy vs radius curve is below the empirical curve everywhere, the gap measuring our ignorance (imperfect attacks from below, conservative certification from above); with \(n = 10^4\) draws and \(\alpha = 0.01\), Clopper-Pearson costs ~2 points of radius — the draw budget is a hyperparameter of the guarantee (example 5.3). ∎
  5. Backdoor and detection (ch. 6). Insert a BadNets trigger (3×3 patch) on 5% of MNIST; check clean accuracy vs trigger success rate; implement the Neural Cleanse reverse-engineering (def. 6.3: minimize \(\|\delta_c\|_1\) per class, detect the MAD anomaly, reconstruct the trigger); repair by pruning then unlearning and measure the clean/trigger/repair triangle.
    Guidance (expected results): with 5% of contaminated data, clean accuracy ≈ unchanged (98-99% — the attack is invisible to standard metrics, def. 6.1) and trigger success rate ≈ 99%; Neural Cleanse (def. 6.3) finds \(\|\delta_t\|_1\) abnormally small for the target class only (MAD > 3, the shortcut already exists); pruning the hyperactive neurons under the reconstructed trigger drops the trigger success but eats into clean accuracy from ~10% of removed neurons — hence the targeted unlearning (fine-tuning on reconstructed inputs) which restores the clean behavior without resurrecting the trigger; conclusion: repair is a trade-off, not a restoration. ∎
  6. Poisoning and influence (ch. 7). Label flipping on a tabular MLP: compare random flips vs "large margin" flips (prop. 7.2) at equal budget; compute the influence functions (Hessian-vector products) and show that the highest-influence flips explain the degradation; test the defenses: filtering by influence, truncated loss, regularization — conclude on the federated setting (referral to Emerging ML module ch. 4).
  7. Extraction (ch. 8). (i) Exact extraction of a logistic regression exposed by an API (prop. 8.2): verify the exact reconstruction, then with rounded probabilities (linear programming); (ii) distillation attack (Jagielski): measure accuracy and fidelity as a function of the query budget; (iii) discuss the defenses (rounding, top-1, quotas) and circumvent them.
  8. Membership inference (ch. 8). On a tabular MLP: shadow models + confidence attack (Shokri) then simplified LiRA (Carlini); plot ROC and TPR at FPR ∈ {0.1%, 1%, 10%}; retrain with DP-SGD (Opacus) at \(\varepsilon \in \{1, 8\}\) and plot utility vs attack advantage — the trade-off curve of the Emerging ML module ch. 5, revisited from the attacker's side.
  9. Final synthesis (ch. 9-10). Write the complete threat analysis (def. 1.1) of a proposed real system (bank scoring, content filter, internal RAG agent): surface, capabilities, goals, applicable attacks chapter by chapter, defenses in depth (ch. 10) with their costs — a 10-minute defense with demonstrations drawn from the previous labs.
