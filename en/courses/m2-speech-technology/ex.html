Exercises / Labs

  1. Lab: Whisper transcription and WER (the module's ASR lab). With transformers: (a) load openai/whisper-tiny as an ASR pipeline, transcribe two French excerpts from Common Voice (one read, one spontaneous); (b) implement the WER (word-level Levenshtein alignment: \(S + D + I\) against the reference); (c) compare tiny/small; (d) pre-filter with Silero VAD (cut the silences) and measure the effect on the hallucinations of (a); (e) redo on English to situate French.
    Guidance (expected results): tiny is 2-3× faster but clearly more fragile; the spontaneous French WER is 1.5-3× read English; the VAD massively reduces the hallucinated loops on silences (Whisper "invents" text without energy — prop. 4.2 experienced); beware of normalizations (capitalization, punctuation: normalize before aligning, otherwise the WER is artifactual). ∎
  2. Lab: TTS with transformers (the synthesis lab). (a) synthesize a French text with a public TTS model (SpeechT5; French MMS-TTS); (b) measure the RTF (computation time / audio duration) and the first-sample latency; (c) try three trap sentences: "1 234 567 €", "le SARS-CoV-2", "à Saint-Étienne, il est 19 h58" — record the normalization failures; (d) inject a different speaker embedding and compare.
    Guidance: RTF \(<\) 1 on GPU, on the order of 1-3 on CPU; the first-sound latency is dominated by the length of the first Mel chunk; numbers and acronyms fail reproducibly — discuss neural vs rule-based front-end (def. 8.3); the embedding shifts the timbre but not the regional accent: perfect zero-shot cloning does not exist with a single vector (ch. 9: one needs the sequential prompt, VALL-E style). ∎
  3. CTC by hand. Take up example 2.2 (\(T = 4\), \(y = \texttt{ab}\)): with \(p_t(\epsilon) = 0.5\), \(p_t(a) = 0.3\), \(p_t(b) = 0.2\) for \(t = 1..4\), compute the 8 products, sum them, check that the sum equals the \(\alpha_4(4) + \alpha_4(3)\) obtained by the recursions of Theorem 2.3 (fill in the \(\alpha\) table row by row).
    Guidance: the \(\alpha\) table has 4 rows \(\times\) 5 columns (with structural zeros — one cannot reach \(\ell_4\) before \(t=2\)); most probable path among the 8: the one that maximizes each column; check that the bound \(T \geq |y|\) (here 2) is necessary and explain the jump condition \(\mathbf 1\{\ell_s \neq \ell_{s-2}\}\) on a counter-example "aa". ∎
  4. CTC in code, gradient verified. Implement \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) (thm. 2.3) in NumPy for a mini-vocabulary; compute \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial p_t(k)\) by the closed formula; check against automatic differentiation (autograd/PyTorch of the sum of paths) to within \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\).
    Guidance: the convention (\(\beta\) with or without the \(p_t\) factor) is the classic error — the autograd check detects it immediately; measured complexity \(\mathcal O(T S)\) versus enumeration (8 paths here: explodes beyond \(T = 15\)); conclude on the differentiation of the full model's objective (softmax + recursions) — this is exactly what PyTorch does, vectorized. ∎
  5. Latency budget of a voice assistant. A pipeline: VAD (30 ms window) \(\to\) chunking ASR (640 ms chunk, 160 ms lookahead) \(\to\) LLM (time-to-first-token 250 ms) \(\to\) TTS (first Mel chunk 120 ms + vocoder 20 ms). (a) compute the first-sound latency of the response (component-by-component detail); (b) identify the two dominant terms and propose three reductions; (c) compare with Moshi's full-duplex (~200 ms theoretical): what does it remove?
    Guidance: (a) \(\approx 160\) (endpointing to add: silence threshold 300-500 ms! the real cost) \(+\) (640+160)\(\times\)1/80 Hz… take care of the units: the audio chunk counts in seconds, not frames; total typically 1-2 s of which half is endpointing; (b) reduce the threshold by end-of-turn prediction (prop. 10.2), ASR with a smaller chunk, speculative LLM (referral to LLMs M2 ch. 6); (c) Moshi removes VAD/endpointing (continuous stream prediction), ASR/TTS (native audio tokens) and the LLM queue: what remains is network time + one model step. ∎
  6. Audio watermarking: ROC under attack (bonus). Synthesize 20 sentences (lab 2); mark half of them with a public audio watermark (e.g. AudioSeal); plot the detector's ROC (true positive / false positive) on: clean audio, mp3 re-encoding at 64 kbps, light noise addition; conclude on the end-to-end provenance channel (ch. 9).
    Guidance: the clean curve is nearly perfect (AUC \(>\) 0.99); mp3 degrades moderately (the watermark survives usual compressions — it is designed for that); strong additive noise attacks harder; relate to the robustness budget (Control module ch. 10): marking strength \(\uparrow\) = imperceptibility \(\downarrow\) — the same triple trade-off as in text. ∎
