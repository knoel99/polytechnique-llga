1. Classical ASR: the GMM-HMM pipeline

The historical pipeline (1980-2015) survives only as technical memories — but its formalism (emission model, chain of states, Bayesian decoding) reappears everywhere, up to the CTC forward-backward (ch. 2). We cover it in one structured chapter.

Theorem 1.1 — The GMM-HMM acoustic model and decoding One decomposes \(p(x_{1:T} \mid w)\) with a hidden Markov chain over phonetic states \(q_t\): each phoneme is a 3-state left-to-right HMM (beginning/middle/end), put in context (triphones); the emission of a state is a Gaussian mixture over the MFCCs (39 dimensions: 13 + first and second derivatives): $$p(x_t \mid q_t = s) = \sum_{m=1}^{M_s} c_{sm}\, \mathcal N(x_t;\ \mu_{sm}, \Sigma_{sm}),$$ and the retained transcription maximizes Bayesian decoding $$\hat w = \arg\max_w\ p(x_{1:T} \mid w)\, p_{\mathrm{LM}}(w),$$ where \(p_{\mathrm{LM}}\) is an n-gram language model (cf. NLP M1 module ch. 2). The likelihood is computed by the forward recursion $$\alpha_t(j) = \Big[\sum_i \alpha_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\Big]\, b_j(x_t),$$ (\(\mathcal O(T N^2)\), \(N\) states) and the best path by Viterbi (same recursion, max instead of the sum). Training: EM (Baum-Welch) — cf. Graph ML M1 module ch. 5 for the EM of latent-variable models.
Proposition 1.2 — Why GMM? And why is it dead? (i) A Gaussian mixture is a universal density approximator (with enough components, any continuous density is approximated): it was the best parameterization before deep learning. (ii) Fatal flaws: MFCCs are not semantic features (the GMM models acoustic blocks, knows nothing about similar phones); the HMM assumes the signal locally stationary per state and conditional independence of the frames; above all, each piece (lexicon, contexts, LM) is optimized separately — impossible to align globally. Neural systems eat raw data: 10,000 labeled hours replace 300 carefully curated ones (ch. 4).
What survives of the pipeline Three direct legacies: (i) the acoustic + LM fusion at decoding (the "shallow fusion" reappears with LLMs to rescore N-bests); (ii) the forward-backward — the central algorithm of CTC in the next chapter; (iii) forced alignment (finding the instants of each word, still used for subtitling and training TTS). Historical reference: Rabiner's tutorial (Proc. IEEE, 1989) remains a limpid read.
