4. Whisper: robustness and multi-task

Theorem 4.1 — Whisper (Radford et al., ICML 2023): the strength of the weak Trained on \(\approx\) 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio/text pairs collected from the web (versus a few hundred to thousand carefully curated hours before), Whisper is a simple transformer encoder-decoder (88M-1.5B parameters); the audio is cut into 30 s windows, log-Mel 80 bins. The output is a sequence of special tokens + text: \(\langle\!|\)start\(\,|\rangle \to\) language \(\to\) task (transcribe/translate into English) \(\to\) timestamps \(\to\) text — everything (language identifier, translation decision, time) is learned as language, no dedicated module. Results: unprecedented robustness to noise/reverberation/accents (trained on real, not read data), WER close to humans on clean English; best zero-shot performance on most languages at the time.
Proposition 4.2 — Why it works: the scaling law of dirty data (i) Coverage: robustness is not a module, it is a consequence of diversity — the 680 kh cover real-world mess (background noise, music, accents), whereas LibriSpeech (1000 h of read books) overlearns clean reading; (ii) Filtering beats labeling: the training pipeline filters aggressively (language detection, audio-text matching by an initial ASR, anti-hallucination heuristics) rather than correcting by hand — the moderately clean but massive data wins; (iii) Multi-task as regularization: transcribing AND translating AND timestamping share one encoder — translation forces semantic, not only acoustic, representations. Documented limits: hallucinations on long silences (looped repetitions — the model "invents" text), no native streaming (ch. 5), difficult fine specialization.
Example 4.3 — French in the crosshairs On clean English, a good system reaches human WER (~5%); the same model on spontaneous contemporary French typically does 2 to 3 times worse (accents, speaking rates, code-switching, proper names) — lab 1 measures it. Three French phenomena to know for engineering: liaison (les|ami·z·arbitres: spelling is not phonetics — the ASR must relearn the graphy), enchaînement (final consonant tied to the next vowel: word boundaries blur in the spectrogram) and number verbalization (1996: "mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize" or "dix-neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize"? — normalization ambiguity, both are valid; cf. ch. 8 for the reverse direction in TTS).
