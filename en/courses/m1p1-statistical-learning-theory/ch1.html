1. The empirical risk minimization framework

Definition 1.1 — Statistical model of learning We observe \(D_n = ((X_1,Y_1), \dots, (X_n,Y_n))\), i.i.d. with distribution \(\mathbb{P}\) on \(\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}\) (\(\mathcal{Y} = \{-1,+1\}\) in binary classification, \(\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}\) in regression). A predictor is a measurable function \(h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}\) (or into \(\mathbb{R}\), the decision being \(t \mapsto \mathrm{sign}(t)\)). A loss \(\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathbb{R} \to [0,\infty)\) measures the error (0-1: \(\ell(y,t) = \mathbf{1}_{t \neq y}\); quadratic: \((y-t)^2\); hinge: \(\max(0, 1-yt)\)). The risk and the empirical risk are $$L(h) = \mathbb{E}\big[\ell(Y, h(X))\big], \qquad \hat L(h) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(Y_i, h(X_i)).$$ For a class \(\mathcal{H}\) of predictors, the ERM estimator (empirical risk minimization) is \(\hat h \in \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \hat L(h)\). This is the paradigm of almost all of supervised machine learning: least squares, logistic regression, SVMs, neural networks (see the Machine Learning module, chapter 1).
Proposition 1.2 — Risk decomposition (proved) Let \(h^\star_\mathcal{H} \in \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L(h)\) be the best hypothesis in the class (the oracle). For every ERM \(\hat h\): $$L(\hat h) - L(h^\star) \;\leq\; 2 \sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \big|L(h) - \hat L(h)\big|.$$
By definition of \(\hat h\) (empirical optimality): \(\hat L(\hat h) \leq \hat L(h^\star)\). Then $$L(\hat h) - L(h^\star) = \big[L(\hat h) - \hat L(\hat h)\big] + \big[\hat L(\hat h) - \hat L(h^\star)\big] + \big[\hat L(h^\star) - L(h^\star)\big] \leq \big[L(\hat h) - \hat L(\hat h)\big] + 0 + \big[\hat L(h^\star) - L(h^\star)\big],$$ and each of the two brackets is bounded in absolute value by \(\sup_{h} |L(h) - \hat L(h)|\).∎
All the theory that follows consists in bounding this uniform supremum of gaps — a random quantity, controlled with high probability. The classic risk decomposition reads there: \(L(\hat h) = \underbrace{\inf_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L(h)}_{\text{approximation}} + \underbrace{L(\hat h) - L(h^\star)}_{\text{estimation}}\): richness of the class versus sample size.
Definition 1.3 — Consistency and learnability A learning rule \(h_n = h(D_n)\) is universally consistent if \(L(h_n) \to \inf_{h} L(h) = L^\star\) in probability for every distribution \(\mathbb{P}\) (the minimum over all measurable functions; \(L^\star\) is attained by the Bayes rule \(\eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid X=x)\) in 0-1 classification). Central question of the course: at what speed, and for which classes \(\mathcal{H}\)? Chapters 3-6 bound the speed by the "size" of \(\mathcal{H}\); chapter 7 shows that these speeds are optimal in the minimax sense.
Example 1.4 — Thresholds on the line (running example) \(\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}\), \(\mathcal{H} = \{h_t : t \in \mathbb{R},\; h_t(x) = \mathbf{1}_{x \leq t}\}\). Why this small example? The class is continuously parametrized, but: (i) on \(n\) points it produces only \(n+1\) distinct dichotomies (the order of the \(X_i\) suffices to describe everything); (ii) its Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension equals \(d = 2\) (ch. 3); (iii) the associated minimax speed is \(\sqrt{d/n}\) (ch. 7). Theory thus turns an infinite family into a finite combinatorial object — this is the founding gesture of Vapnik and Chervonenkis.
