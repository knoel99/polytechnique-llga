2. Concentration inequalities

Definition 2.1 — Sub-Gaussian variables A centered r.v. \(X\) is sub-Gaussian with parameter \(\sigma\) if \(\mathbb{E}\,e^{\lambda X} \leq e^{\sigma^2\lambda^2/2}\) for all \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\). Examples: centered Gaussian (\(\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}\)), centered bounded (lemma 2.2, with \(\sigma = (b-a)/2\)), any \(L\)-Lipschitz function of a Gaussian vector (parameter \(L\sigma_G\), "Gaussian concentration"; see the Probability Theory for ML module, chapter 2, for the CLT in the same spirit). (Independent) sums of sub-Gaussians are sub-Gaussian with additive \(\sigma^2\): the whole "Gaussian paradigm" of concentration fits in this semigroup of the Laplace transform.
Lemma 2.2 — Hoeffding's lemma (proved) If \(X \in [a,b]\) almost surely and \(\mathbb{E}X = 0\), then for all \(\lambda\): $$\mathbb{E}\,e^{\lambda X} \leq e^{\lambda^2 (b-a)^2/8}.$$
Step 1 (secant line). The convexity of \(u \mapsto e^{\lambda u}\) gives, for \(u \in [a,b]\): $$e^{\lambda u} \leq \frac{b-u}{b-a}\,e^{\lambda a} + \frac{u-a}{b-a}\,e^{\lambda b}.$$ Taking the expectation with \(\mathbb{E}X = 0\) (average weights \(b/(b-a)\) and \(-a/(b-a)\)): \(\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X} \leq \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda Z}\) where \(Z\) is the two-point variable taking values \(a\) and \(b\) with \(\mathbb{E}Z = 0\). Step 2 (variance of a tilted Bernoulli). Set \(L(\lambda) = \log \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda Z}\). Differentiating twice under the expectation (everything is bounded): $$L''(\lambda) = \mathrm{Var}_{\lambda}(Z) \leq \frac{(b-a)^2}{4},$$ where \(\mathrm{Var}_\lambda\) is the variance under the tilted distribution \(d\mathbb{P}_\lambda \propto e^{\lambda z}\,d\mathbb{P}\); the variance of a two-point variable with points separated by \(b-a\) is \(p(1-p)(b-a)^2 \leq (b-a)^2/4\), whatever the tilt. Step 3 (Taylor). \(L(0) = 0\), \(L'(0) = \mathbb{E}Z = 0\), hence \(L(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{2}L''(\xi) \leq \lambda^2 (b-a)^2/8\) for some \(\xi \in [0,\lambda]\).∎
Theorem 2.3 — Hoeffding's inequality (proved) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be independent, \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\), and \(S = \sum_i (X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i)\). For every \(t > 0\): $$\mathbb{P}(S \geq t) \leq \exp\Big(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{P}(|S| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2}\Big).$$
Chernoff's method: for \(\lambda > 0\), by Markov then independence and lemma 2.2 applied to each \(X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i\): $$\mathbb{P}(S \geq t) \leq e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda S} = e^{-\lambda t} \prod_i \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda(X_i - \mathbb{E}X_i)} \leq \exp\Big(-\lambda t + \frac{\lambda^2}{8}\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2\Big).$$ The right-hand side is minimized at \(\lambda = 4t/\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\), where the exponent equals \(-2t^2/\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\). The two-sided version follows by applying the result to \(-S\) and summing.∎
Immediate application: for a binomial empirical frequency (0-1 loss of a fixed \(h\): \(n\hat L(h)\) is binomial), $$\mathbb{P}\big(\hat L(h) - L(h) \geq \varepsilon\big) \leq e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.$$
Example 2.4 — Sample size (numbers) To guarantee \(|\hat L(h) - L(h)| \leq \varepsilon\) with probability \(1 - \delta\) for a fixed \(h\): \(n \geq \frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}\).

Theorem 2.5 — Bernstein and McDiarmid (statements)
  • Bernstein: if the independent centered \(X_i\) satisfy \(|X_i| \leq M\) and \(\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = v\), then $$\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_i X_i \geq \sqrt{2vt} + \tfrac{Mt}{3}\Big) \leq e^{-t},$$ that is, after inversion, \(\mathbb{P}(\sum X_i \geq u) \leq \exp(-u^2/(2v + 2Mu/3))\). Regime \(u \lesssim v/M\): Gaussian (\(e^{-u^2/2v}\), variance); large \(u\): Poissonian (\(e^{-3u/2M}\), amplitude);
  • McDiarmid (bounded differences): if \(f : \prod \mathcal{X}_i \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfies \(|f(x) - f(x')| \leq c_i\) whenever \(x\) and \(x'\) differ only in the \(i\)-th coordinate, then $$\mathbb{P}\big(f - \mathbb{E}f \geq u\big) \leq \exp\Big(-\frac{2u^2}{\sum_i c_i^2}\Big).$$ Proof: Doob's martingale + lemma 2.2 conditionally on each coordinate (admitted here).
Why McDiarmid is the tool of the course: the supremum \(f(D_n) = \sup_{h} |L(h) - \hat L(h)|\) is 1-Lipschitz with respect to each datum \((X_i,Y_i)\) (changing one observation shifts \(\hat L\) by at most \(1/n\) if the losses are bounded by 1): \(c_i = 2/n\) and McDiarmid transports the concentration of the mean to the supremum — all that remains is to control \(\mathbb{E}f\), the heart of chapters 3-5.
Proposition 2.6 — Maximum of sub-Gaussians (proved) If \(X_1, \dots, X_M\) are centered sub-Gaussian with parameter \(\sigma\) (not necessarily independent): $$\mathbb{E}\max_{j \leq M} X_j \;\leq\; \sigma\sqrt{2 \log M}.$$
For \(\lambda > 0\), monotonicity of the log then of the expectation (Jensen), then sub-additivity of the max and finally the sub-Gaussian assumption: $$\mathbb{E}\max_j X_j = \frac1{\lambda}\,\mathbb{E}\log e^{\lambda\max_j X_j} \leq \frac1{\lambda}\log \mathbb{E} e^{\lambda \max_j X_j} = \frac1{\lambda}\log \mathbb{E} \max_j e^{\lambda X_j} \leq \frac1{\lambda}\log \sum_j \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X_j} \leq \frac{\log M}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda \sigma^2}{2}.$$ Optimization at \(\lambda = \sqrt{2\log M}/\sigma\).∎
Interpretation: the max of \(M\) Gaussian noises equals \(\sigma\sqrt{2\log M}\) up to a constant — the "price of the max". It is the key ingredient for controlling suprema: combined with the union bound (Prop. 0.1), it gives \(\sqrt{\log M/n}\) for any finite class of \(M\) bounded losses.
From the finite max to the infinite Proposition 2.6 and the union bound (Prop. 0.1) control any finite class of \(M\) predictors (bounds in \(\sqrt{\log M/n}\)). For infinite classes (\(M = \infty\)), one must count the realizable dichotomies — the subject of the next two chapters; for "geometric" classes, one will go through Rademacher (ch. 5).
