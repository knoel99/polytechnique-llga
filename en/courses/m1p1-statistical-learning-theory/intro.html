Introduction and overview

ModuleStatistical Learning Theory
CodeAPM_51059_EP
Team and workloadInstructor: Karim Lounici (École Polytechnique) · 36 h · 4.5 ECTS · concentration, Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension and Rademacher complexity: the theory of generalization, up to the oracle inequalities of the Lasso and the aggregation of estimators.
Status in the programP1 electiveOutside the recommended track

Why should a model that minimizes error on its training data predict correctly on new data? Statistical learning theory answers this question with theorems: concentration inequalities, the Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension, Rademacher complexity, generalization bounds. This is the most theoretical module of Period 1, the one that turns the intuition of overfitting into quantitative proofs — the expected language if you are aiming for a PhD, and a rare and valued asset in industry for auditing high-risk models.

What you will be able to do

  • Formalize a learning problem in the empirical risk minimization (ERM) framework and identify what needs to be proved.
  • Derive and use concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Bernstein, McDiarmid) in the service of learning bounds.
  • Compute the VC dimension of a function class and apply Sauer's lemma.
  • Establish and interpret a VC generalization bound — and understand what it does not say.
  • Master Rademacher complexity, a finer tool than the VC dimension for modern classes.
  • Exploit margin theory to explain the generalization of large-margin classifiers.
  • Read and construct lower bounds and minimax results: know when a problem is genuinely hard.
  • Analyze high dimension via sparsity and the Lasso (oracle inequalities) and understand the aggregation of estimators.

Where this course fits in the program

Elective of Year 1 · Period 1 (36 h, 4.5 ECTS), outside the recommended track: a choice of theoretical deepening. It assumes two prerequisites: the Machine Learning module, whose "Generalization theory" chapter it deepens, and the Refresher in Statistics module for convergence and estimation.

This is the research-orientation module par excellence: proving bounds "by hand" is the daily routine of a PhD student in theoretical ML (CMAP, Hi!PARIS, Inria), and doctoral schools expect familiarity with it. Downstream, it directly prepares the M2 module Privacy and Uncertainty Quantification, whose guarantees (differential privacy, uncertainty quantification) are written with the same concentration and complexity tools. The oracle inequalities of the Lasso also open onto the high-dimensional statistics encountered in several research seminars of the master's program.

Course outline

  • Review: probability, estimation, convexity: the tools of the two prerequisites, brought to the level required by the proofs of the course.
  • The empirical risk minimization framework: formalizing learning as a statistical problem — the birth certificate of the course.
  • Concentration inequalities: Hoeffding and its cousins, the ingredient of all the bounds that follow.
  • Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension and Sauer's lemma: measuring the "size" of a function class.
  • VC generalization bounds: the central theorem — empirical error versus true error, uniformly.
  • Rademacher complexity: a data-dependent complexity measure, finer, more modern.
  • Margin theory: why a large margin generalizes better, and what this says about SVMs and beyond.
  • Lower bounds and minimax: proving that no algorithm can do better — the dark and necessary side of theory.
  • High dimension: sparsity and the Lasso: learning when the dimension exceeds the number of samples, with oracle inequalities in support.
  • Aggregation of estimators: combining predictors with guarantees — the bridge to boosting as seen in Machine Learning.
  • Research of the teaching team: Karim Lounici's research, from high-dimensional statistics to learning guarantees.

How to study this module

  • Redo each proof on scratch paper, line by line: in this module, understanding the proof is the goal — the result alone is not enough at the exam.
  • Go through the review chapter without skipping it: convexity and concentration are presented there exactly at the level used in chapters 2 to 6.
  • Keep the guiding thread in mind: "control the gap between empirical risk and true risk, uniformly over a class" — each chapter adds a tool for this.
  • Do the exercises on computing VC dimensions and Rademacher complexities on concrete examples (thresholds, intervals, hyperplanes): they form the memory of the course.
  • Reread in parallel the "Generalization theory" chapter of the Machine Learning module: you will see what was sketched there become theorems.
  • If an academic career appeals to you, treat the "Research of the teaching team" chapter as a first reading of a research paper.
A word from the teaching team The LLGA master's program trains researchers as much as engineers of large models. This module is where one learns to prove what the other modules observe: without generalization theory, neither LLMs nor GNNs can be audited, certified, or truly understood.
