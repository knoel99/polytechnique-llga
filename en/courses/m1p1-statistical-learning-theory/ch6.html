6. Margin theory

Definition 6.1 — Functional and geometric margin For an affine classifier \(h_{w,b}(x) = \mathrm{sign}(\langle w, x\rangle + b)\), the functional margin of an example \((x,y)\) is \(y(\langle w,x\rangle + b)\) (positive if correctly classified), and the geometric margin is this quantity divided by \(\|w\|_2\) — it is the Euclidean distance to the hyperplane. The margin of a sample is the minimum over the examples. The maximum margin problem (hard-margin SVM, see the Machine Learning module, chapter 3) seeks \(\max \gamma\) under the constraints \(\|w\| \leq 1\) and \(y_i\langle w,x_i\rangle \geq \gamma\): minimizing \(\|w\|\) is maximizing the margin.
Theorem 6.2 — Margin bound (stated with sketch) Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a class of score functions (e.g. linear), with data satisfying \(\|X\| \leq R\). For every \(\gamma > 0\), denote by \(\hat L_\gamma\) the empirical margin error at \(\gamma\) (proportion of examples with \(y f(x) < \gamma\)). With probability \(1 - \delta\), for all score functions \(f\) in the class: $$L\big(\mathrm{sign}\, f\big) \;\leq\; \hat L_\gamma(f) + \frac{2\,\hat{\mathcal{R}}_S(\mathcal{F})}{\gamma} + 3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.$$ Sketch: (i) chain of losses \(\mathbf{1}_{t < 0} \;\leq\; \varphi_\gamma(t) \;\leq\; \mathbf{1}_{t < \gamma}\) with the margin loss \(\varphi_\gamma(t) = \min\big(1, \max(0, 1 - t/\gamma)\big)\) (immediate case check): the 0-1 loss is dominated by the margin loss, which is itself dominated by the margin-\(\gamma\) error; (ii) Talagrand's contraction (Lemma 5.4) on \(\varphi_\gamma\), which is \(1/\gamma\)-Lipschitz; (iii) oracle bound of Theorem 5.3 on the contracted class. See Mohri, Rostamizadeh and Talwalkar, Theorem 5.9, for the full version with exact constants.
Corollary 6.3 — SVM: dimension-independent complexity For linear functions with \(\|w\| \leq W\), \(\|X\| \leq R\) and \(\hat L_\gamma = 0\) (data separated with margin \(\gamma = 1/W\)): $$L(h_{w,b}) \;\leq\; \frac{2\sqrt2\,R}{\gamma\sqrt n} + 3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}} \;\approx\; \frac{3R}{\gamma\sqrt n} \quad \text{(Prop. 5.5 substituted).}$$ Numbers (\(R = 1\), \(\gamma = 0.1\)): \(n = 10^4\) gives a bound \(\approx 0.29\); \(n = 10^6\), \(\approx 0.03\). The crucial point: neither \(p\) nor the VC dimension \(p+1\) appear — the effective complexity is \(R^2/\gamma^2\). High-dimensional but well-separable data generalize; this is the theorem that justifies SVMs, norm-based regularization, and by extension deep networks with controlled margins (see the Deep Learning module, chapter 3: the role of weight norms in controlling Lipschitzness).
Margins and deep learning Neural networks have infinite VC dimension (or growing with the parameters); yet they generalize. The modern explanation goes through margin- and norm-based bounds of the same type as 6.3 (products of matrix norms with margins), smoothed by depth — and not through VC counting. The "contraction + Lipschitz classes + norms" chain of this chapter is exactly the tool used in that literature.
Complement to the official syllabus: low-noise condition and fast rates The detailed syllabus of the course unit mentions the "low-noise condition and fast rates": this is the probabilistic generalization of the geometric margin of this chapter. Tsybakov's margin condition bears on the joint distribution rather than on the sample: for a threshold \(\Delta > 0\), \(\mathbb{P}\big(|\eta(X) - 1/2| \leq \Delta\big) \leq c\,\Delta^{s}\), where \(\eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x)\) is the posterior probability and \(s \geq 0\) the noise parameter: \(s = 0\) gives back the general case (no useful assumption); large \(s\) means little mass near the boundary — the limit \(s \to \infty\) corresponds to a strict margin almost surely. Its effect: the classification excess risk is bounded by a power of the "approximate" excess risk (that of \(\eta\)), with exponent increasing in \(s\) — typically of the order of \((s+1)/(s+2)\) for plug-in and convex classifiers (logistic, boosting — Machine Learning module, ch. 5 and 9). Convergence rates then leave the "slow" \(n^{-1/2}\) regime of Corollary 6.3 for fast rates, up to parametric order when the margin is strict. In other words: the sample margin (\(\gamma\), Corollary 6.3) is the geometric object, Tsybakov's condition is its statistical assumption, and together they explain why large-margin separators often do better than the \(1/\sqrt n\) bound in practice.
