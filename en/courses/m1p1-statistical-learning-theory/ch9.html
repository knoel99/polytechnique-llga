9. Aggregation of estimators

Definition 9.1 — The aggregation problem We have a dictionary \(f_1, \dots, f_M\) of estimators (hyperparameters, models, features); we want to build an aggregate \(\tilde f\) whose risk approaches that of the best of them (or of a combination). Three frameworks: (MS) model selection \(\tilde f = f_{\hat j}\); (C) convex combination \(\tilde f = \sum_j \pi_j f_j\), with \(\pi\) in the simplex; (L) linear combination ("\(L^2\)-convex" aggregation, for the quadratic loss). Quantitative goal: an oracle excess risk $$L(\tilde f) - \min_{j \leq M} L(f_j) \;\leq\; \text{price}(M, n)$$ with a price that is small compared to the risk itself — typically \(\sqrt{\log M/n}\) or even \(\log M/n\) (quadratic loss, see Thm 9.3).
Definition 9.2 — Exponential weights With a temperature \(\eta > 0\) (and a prior \(\rho\) on the dictionary): $$\pi_j = \frac{\rho_j\, e^{-\eta n \hat L(f_j)}}{\sum_{k} \rho_k\, e^{-\eta n \hat L(f_k)}}, \qquad \tilde f = \sum_j \pi_j f_j \quad (\text{convex loss}).$$ The weights reward good empirical performance exponentially; \(\rho\) penalizes complexity (uniform prior: \(\log M\) in the bound; sparse prior: recovery of Lasso-type results, ch. 10).
Theorem 9.3 — Convex aggregation oracle (proved for (i)) Losses \(\ell\) convex in the prediction, \(\ell \in [0,1]\), uniform prior.
  • (i) With probability \(\geq 1 - \delta\): \(L(\tilde f) - \min_j L(f_j) \leq \sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}}\);
  • (ii) for the quadratic loss, the "progression" argument improves the price to \(\frac{2\log M}{n} + \sqrt{\ldots}\) (order \(\log M/n\)) — sharp form due to Catoni, Tsybakov; this is the regime attained by mirror averaging (ch. 10, Lounici 2007).
(i), full proof in three steps. Convexity: \(\ell\) convex in the prediction and the \(\pi_j\) sum to 1, so (Jensen conditionally on the weights): $$L(\tilde f) \;\leq\; \sum_j \pi_j \hat L(f_j) + \max_j \big|L(f_j) - \hat L(f_j)\big| \;\leq\; \min_j \hat L(f_j) + \max_j \big|L(f_j) - \hat L(f_j)\big|.$$ Uniformization: each \(\hat L(f_j)\) is an average of bounded i.i.d. r.v.; Hoeffding (Thm 2.3) + union bound (Prop. 0.1) over \(j \leq M\) give, with probability \(1-\delta\), \(\max_j |L - \hat L| \leq \sqrt{\log(2M/\delta)/(2n)}\). Back to the true risk: on this event, \(L(\tilde f) \leq \min_j \hat L(f_j) + \sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}} \leq \min_j L(f_j) + 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}}\), and twice the square root is exactly \(\sqrt{2\log(2M/\delta)/n}\).∎
Proposition 9.4 — Cross-validation as aggregation (statement) \(K\)-fold cross-validation (Machine Learning module, chapter 9) is a robust (MS) aggregation: the risk of the selected model \(\hat j_{\mathrm{CV}}\) satisfies oracle inequalities of the same type, with \(M\) replaced by the number of candidates and a correction for the validation bias (the training sample has size \(n(1-1/K)\)); the "one standard error" rule (pick the most parsimonious model within the error bar of the CV minimum) is the empirical analogue of the approximation/estimation trade-off of Prop. 1.2. Bounds of the Thm 9.3 type for CV: selection by cross-validation inherits oracle inequalities of the same type — literature on aggregation and sequential prediction (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006). 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

n, M = 400, 12
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, n); y = np.sin(2*x) + 0.3*rng.standard_normal(n)
target = lambda z: np.sin(2*z)

dictionary = [lambda z, d=d: np.polyval(np.polyfit(x, y, d), z) for d in range(1, M+1)]
losses = np.array([np.mean((f(x) - y)**2) for f in dictionary])   # empirical risk

eta = 20.0
pi = np.exp(-eta*n*(losses - losses.min())); pi /= pi.sum()
agg = lambda z: sum(w*f(z) for w, f in zip(pi, dictionary))

z = np.linspace(-2, 2, 400)
risk = lambda f: np.mean((f(z) - target(z))**2)
print("best model:", int(np.argmin(losses))+1,
      f"risk {risk(dictionary[int(np.argmin(losses))]):.4f}")
print(f"exponential aggregate: risk {risk(agg):.4f}")
print("weights (first 3):", np.round(pi[:3], 3))
PAC-Bayesian: the bridge Exponential weights are a posterior distribution; PAC-Bayesian bounds directly control \(L(\tilde f)\) against the weighted average of the risks, with a KL(weights, prior) divergence term instead of \(\log M\) — hence structured priors (sparse: recovery of \(\log p\) rates without a design condition): this is the direction of Alquier and Lounici (ch. 10). This literature connects Bayesian model choice and frequentist aggregation in a single inequality.
