7. Lower bounds and minimax

Definition 7.1 — Minimax risk For a model \(\{\mathbb{P}_\theta^n : \theta \in \Theta\}\) (n i.i.d. observations) and a loss \(d\): $$\mathcal{M}_n = \inf_{\hat\theta}\ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta}\ \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[d(\hat\theta(D_n), \theta)\big],$$ the infimum ranging over all measurable estimators. The previous chapters show \(\mathcal{M}_n \leq \sqrt{d/n}\) (upper bounds); this chapter establishes the converse: no algorithm can do better in general — the rates obtained are optimal.
Theorem 7.2 — Le Cam's two-point method (proved) If there exist \(\theta_1, \theta_2 \in \Theta\) and \(s > 0\) with \(d(\theta_1,\theta_2) \geq 2s\) and \(\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \delta < 1\), then $$\mathcal{M}_n \;\geq\; \frac{s}{2}\,(1 - \delta).$$
Fix an estimator \(\hat\theta\) and set \(A = \{d(\hat\theta, \theta_1) \leq s\}\). Two observations:
  • on \(A^c\): \(d(\hat\theta, \theta_1) > s\), a contribution \(s\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c)\) to the risk under \(\theta_1\);
  • on \(A\): by the triangle inequality and the separation, \(d(\hat\theta, \theta_2) \geq d(\theta_1,\theta_2) - d(\hat\theta,\theta_1) \geq 2s - s = s\), a contribution \(s\,\mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A)\) under \(\theta_2\).
Hence \(\sup_{j} \mathbb{E}_{\theta_j} d(\hat\theta,\theta_j) \geq \frac12\sum_j \mathbb{E}_{\theta_j}[\dots] \geq \frac{s}{2}\big( \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c) + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A)\big)\). Now \(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A^c) + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A) = 1 + \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}(A) - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}(A) \geq 1 - \|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n - \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \geq 1 - \delta\), by the definition of total variation (supremum over events, including \(A\)).∎
Moral: two separated but statistically barely distinguishable parameters (small TV) trap any estimator; the distances between product distributions are controlled via divergences (Kullback, see the next application).
Example 7.3 — Gaussian mean: \(1/\sqrt n\) is unavoidable (computation) Model \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\), \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}\), absolute loss. Take \(\theta_1 = 0\), \(\theta_2 = \sigma/\sqrt n\), so \(s = \sigma/(2\sqrt n)\). The Kullback-Leibler divergence of the product distributions equals $$\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_1}^n \,\|\, \mathbb{P}_{\theta_2}^n) = n \times \frac{(\theta_1 - \theta_2)^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac12,$$ and Pinsker's inequality \(\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \leq \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}/2}\) gives \(\delta \leq 1/2\). Theorem 7.2 concludes: \(\mathcal{M}_n \geq \sigma/(8\sqrt n)\). Under quadratic loss, the same refined argument (exercise 6) gives \(\mathcal{M}_n \geq c\,\sigma^2/n\) — the classic parametric rate, attained by the empirical mean: it is optimal.
Theorem 7.4 — Lower bounds for VC classes (sketch) For 0-1 classification with \(\mathcal{H}\) of VC dimension \(d\) and \(n \geq c d\), there exists a data distribution for which every ERM learner (or any other) incurs \(\mathbb{E}[L(\hat h) - L^\star] \geq c'\sqrt{d/n}\): the bound of Corollary 4.2 is attainable up to constants. Sketch: let the data live on \(\{1,\dots,d\}\) (uniform) and consider the \(2^d\) classifiers \(h_A = \mathbf{1}_A\). Two random classifiers differ on \(d/4\) points on average (separation \(\sqrt{d}\) in risk); one observation informs about a single coordinate only, so the KL between product distributions \(\leq n/d\); a variant of Le Cam with a mixture over \(A\) (Fano's or Assouad's method) converts the bundle of \(2^d\) hypotheses into the \(\sqrt{d/n}\) bound. See Tsybakov, Introduction to Nonparametric Estimation, chapter 2. The same structure holds for nonparametric rates: Lipschitz regression with \(\beta = 1\), minimax rate \(n^{-1/3}\) in \(L^2\) (two points becoming two blocks of separated balls).
Loop closed with "no free lunch" Theorem 4.4 says: without assumptions, nothing works. The minimax approach refines it: with a dimension-\(d\) assumption, \(\sqrt{d/n}\) is exactly the price. Learning is a bargain between expressivity (small \(L^\star\), large \(d\)) and estimation (large \(n\)); the cross-validation of chapter 9 is its empirical version.
