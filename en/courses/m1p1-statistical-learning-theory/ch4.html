4. VC generalization bounds

Theorem 4.1 — Vapnik-Chervonenkis inequality (statement, proof sketched) Let \(\mathcal{H}\) have VC dimension \(d \geq 1\), with losses in \([0,1]\). With probability at least \(1 - \delta\), simultaneously for all \(h \in \mathcal{H}\): $$\big|L(h) - \hat L(h)\big| \;\leq\; \varepsilon_n(\delta) = \sqrt{\frac{32}{n}\Big(d\,\log\frac{2en}{d} + \log\frac{4}{\delta}\Big)}.$$
Sketch (double sample). The constants are not optimal; the structure is what matters.
  • Step 1 — symmetrization: introduce a ghost sample \(D'_n\), i.i.d. with the same distribution. On the deviation event \(\{L - \hat L \geq \varepsilon\}\), the ghost version \(\hat L'\) is at distance \(\geq \varepsilon/2\) from \(\hat L\) with probability \(\geq 1 - e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8}\): for large \(n\varepsilon^2\), controlling \(L - \hat L\) amounts to controlling \(\hat L' - \hat L\) — two empirical quantities, depending on the distributions only through the \(2n\) sampled points;
  • Step 2 — combinatorial reduction: conditionally on the \(2n\) points, \(\hat L' - \hat L\) is an average of Bernoulli variables (each ghost datum is a uniform draw among the \(2n\) points); the supremum over \(\mathcal{H}\) now bears only on the \(\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(2n) \leq (2en/d)^d\) realizable dichotomies; binomial concentration (Hoeffding, Thm 2.3) then union bound (Prop. 0.1) over these dichotomies;
  • Step 3 — assembly: choosing the thresholds so that the three failure probabilities (symmetrization, binomial, union) sum to \(\delta\), one inverts \(\varepsilon\) and obtains the announced bound (classical form of Vapnik-Chervonenkis 1974; see Devroye, Györfi and Lugosi, A Probabilistic Theory of Pattern Recognition, Theorem 12.5, for the details).
∎
Corollary 4.2 — Risk bound for ERM Combining Prop. 1.2 and Thm 4.1: with probability \(1 - \delta\), $$L(\hat h) \;\leq\; \hat L(\hat h) + 2\,\varepsilon_n(\delta) \;\leq\; L(h^\star_\mathcal{H}) + 4\,\varepsilon_n(\delta).$$ Consequences: (i) every class with finite VC dimension is universally consistent (Def. 1.3); (ii) the sample cost to guarantee an excess risk \(\varepsilon\) is \(n = \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/\varepsilon^2)\) (up to logarithms); (iii) it suffices that \(d \ll n\) — and not \(p \ll n\) — to learn: this is the first appearance of the inverted "curse" of high dimension.

The classical constants make the bound uninformative at small sample sizes (at \(n = 10^3\), it exceeds 1 as soon as \(d = 10\); at \(n = 10^4\) as soon as \(d = 50\)): the theorem is a theorem about rates (\(\varepsilon_n \sim \sqrt{d/n}\)), not a practical calibration tool — that role goes to cross-validation (ch. 9; Machine Learning module, chapter 9). The modern versions (Rademacher, ch. 5) will tighten the constants and the logarithm.
Theorem 4.4 — "No free lunch" (statement) For every learning rule \(h_n\), there exists a distribution \(\mathbb{P}\) on \(\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\}\) with \(L^\star = 0\) (separable data!) such that \(\mathbb{E}[L(h_n)] \geq 1/8\) whenever \(n \leq d/2\), where \(d\) is the size of a set shattered by the class used. Without a class restriction (or a regularity assumption), learning over all distributions is impossible: the VC dimension (or any complexity measure) is the currency with which one pays for generalization. Proof by a counting argument over the \(2^{2n}\) consistent labelings (see Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, chapter 5).
